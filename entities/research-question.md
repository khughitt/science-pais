# Why do some people fail to recover after acute infection, and what shared mechanisms link the post-acute infection syndromes?

## Summary

This project studies post-acute infection syndromes (PAIS) — long COVID, ME/CFS, post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome, post-dengue and post-Q-fever fatigue, post-SARS syndrome, and related conditions — as a shared failure to return to homeostasis after an acute infection resolves (or appears to resolve). In most people, the immune and physiological response to infection is self-limiting and recovery is complete; in a subset, the system locks into a persistent dysregulated state characterized by fatigue, post-exertional malaise, cognitive impairment, dysautonomia, pain, and multisystem dysfunction.

The central question is whether these syndromes — historically studied in pathogen-specific silos — share a common pathophysiology, and if so what that failure mode is. Candidate mechanisms recur across triggers: persistent immune activation and T-cell exhaustion, antigen or pathogen-fragment persistence, infection-triggered autoimmunity and autoantibodies, latent-virus reactivation (notably EBV), thromboinflammation and endothelial dysfunction, dysautonomia and small-fiber neuropathy, mast-cell activation, gut dysbiosis, and metabolic/mitochondrial dysfunction. The project treats each pathogen-specific literature as an entry point into the general question of why recovery fails and how the post-infectious state is sustained.

## Working Frame

- Recovery from acute infection is a homeostatic process: the immune system must clear the pathogen, resolve inflammation, repair tissue, and return effector and regulatory populations to baseline. PAIS represents failure of one or more of these return-to-baseline processes.
- The post-acute syndromes may be partly convergent (a shared dysregulated attractor reachable from many triggers) and partly trigger-specific (distinct mechanisms — e.g. SARS-CoV-2 antigen persistence vs. Borrelia peptidoglycan persistence vs. EBV reactivation — feeding a common phenotype).
- Heterogeneity is intrinsic: case definitions, acute-illness severity, time-since-infection, age, sex, and prior immunity all shape the phenotype, and apparent between-study differences often reflect these covariates rather than distinct biology.
- Evidence maturity varies sharply by mechanism. Some claims (autoantibody enrichment, persistent inflammatory signatures, EBV reactivation) have controlled longitudinal support; others (microclots, tissue antigen persistence) are promising but less settled. Claims are held at their evidence level rather than fused into a single unifying story.
