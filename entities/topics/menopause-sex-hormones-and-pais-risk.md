# Menopause Status, Sex Hormones, and PAIS Risk

## Summary

The current evidence supports female sex as a reproducible long-COVID risk factor, but it does not support a simple claim that menopause causes PAIS.
The strongest empirical anchor is Shah et al.'s RECOVER analysis: female sex was associated with higher long-COVID risk overall, with the clearest excess in ages 40-54 years and continued but smaller excess at age 55 years or older [@Shah2025].
Menopause-specific papers show a large clinical problem: perimenopause, menopause, and long COVID share fatigue, cognitive difficulty, sleep disturbance, palpitations, musculoskeletal pain, and mood symptoms, creating real diagnostic and ascertainment ambiguity [@Stewart2024; @Humphreys2025; @Newson2021].
The most defensible current posture is that hormonal state may modify immune, vascular, autonomic, and symptom-expression pathways, while age, comorbidity, deprivation, acute severity, care-seeking, and symptom overlap remain major alternative explanations.

## Key Concepts

**Sex and menopausal status are distinct variables.**
Female sex, age, menopausal transition, hormone therapy, pregnancy, and menopause-associated comorbidities can point in different causal directions and should not be collapsed into a single "women's hormones" variable.

**Symptom overlap is a measurement problem, not a full explanation.**
Menopause and long COVID share many symptoms, so clinical screening is necessary, but overlap alone does not explain the RECOVER female excess risk.

**Immune dysregulation is plausible but still underidentified.**
Sex hormones can modulate inflammatory, antiviral, B-cell, endothelial, and hemostatic pathways, but the current long-COVID evidence does not yet identify a hormone-mediated causal mechanism.

**Acute infection and post-acute persistence may diverge.**
Rebman2026 and Mishra2020 show that acute severity and post-acute symptom persistence cannot be inferred from sex or menopause in a simple monotonic way.

## Current State of Knowledge

Shah et al. provide the best population-level long-COVID evidence in this batch.
In RECOVER, 21% of infected females versus 16% of infected males met long-COVID criteria, and propensity-score matched models estimated female risk ratios of 1.31 to 1.44 depending on adjustment set [@Shah2025].
The sex association was strongest at ages 40-54 years, absent at ages 18-39 years, and still present at age 55 years or older.
Menopausal females aged 40-54 years had a similar point estimate to nonmenopausal females but wider uncertainty, so the study does not cleanly separate perimenopause from female sex, age, pregnancy, comorbidity, or symptom-reporting effects.

Clinic and survey studies show the measurement problem.
Stewart et al. found very high menopause symptom questionnaire burden among women in NHS post-COVID clinics, with the highest total scores in ages 40-54 years and 51% of menstruating patients reporting menstrual disturbance with infection [@Stewart2024].
Newson et al.'s survey abstract reported many women attributing symptoms to perimenopause/menopause and frequent premenstrual symptom worsening, but the self-selected design makes it hypothesis-generating only [@Newson2021].
Humphreys et al. synthesize the small literature and conclude that menopausal transition likely shapes presentation and care needs, but menopausal status has not been shown to independently increase long-COVID risk [@Humphreys2025].

Boneva2015 extends the reproductive-stage signal outside long COVID: women with CFS reported earlier menopause and more gynecologic morbidity/surgery than controls, and hysterectomy/oophorectomy preceded CFS onset in 71% of the dated-surgery subset [@Boneva2015]. That timing clue weakens a pure reverse-causation reading for surgical menopause, but the study is retrospective, non-post-infection-indexed, and cannot separate natural menopause from gynecologic indications or surgery-associated hormone withdrawal.

Acute-COVID and infection-comparator studies caution against overfitting the long-COVID story.
Mishra et al. found worse unadjusted acute COVID outcomes in menopausal women, but menopause was not an independent predictor after adjustment for age, severity, and comorbidities [@Mishra2020].
Esseffar et al. show that cardiovascular disease and diabetes in menopausal women are strongly associated with acute symptom intensity and recovery duration, underscoring confounding by menopause-associated comorbidities [@Esseffar2025].
Rebman et al. found that in early Lyme disease, males had more objective acute severity and higher seropositivity, while postmenopausal females looked more similar to males than premenopausal females [@Rebman2026].
This suggests that acute infection control, diagnostic test behavior, and post-acute persistence may have different sex/hormone relationships.

## Mechanistic Interpretation

Immune dysregulation remains plausible but not established as the menopause-sex-hormone mechanism for PAIS.
Averyanova et al. summarize mechanisms by which estrogens and progesterone can modulate innate cytokine production, T-cell balance, B-cell responses, vascular endothelium, and hemostasis [@Averyanova2022].
Those mechanisms fit the project's failed immune-homeostasis frame and connect to existing long-COVID signatures involving IL-6/JAK-STAT, interferon, complement, endothelial dysfunction, and cortisol/HPA-axis disruption.
However, the evidence in this batch is mostly indirect, acute-COVID-focused, or narrative.
Costeira et al. further complicate a simple estrogen-protection model: menopausal status and COCP use showed associations consistent with estrogen protection in predicted acute COVID, but HRT findings were inconsistent and vulnerable to route, dose, indication, and healthy-user bias [@Costeira2021].

Vascular and endocrine alternatives need to stay in the model.
Kawai et al. show that SARS-CoV-2 and other viral infections are associated with cardiovascular events, with mechanisms including endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory cytokines, hypercoagulability, and neutrophil extracellular traps [@Kawai2025].
This vascular pathway could interact with menopause-associated cardiovascular risk without requiring menopause to be the primary PAIS cause.
Endocrine explanations are also possible: some symptoms attributed to long COVID may be menopausal, some menopausal symptoms may be worsened by infection or inflammation, and some hormone-axis disruption may be downstream of PAIS rather than upstream.

## Current Working Model

The most useful causal model is multi-pathway:

| Pathway | Current read | Main caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Female sex -> long-COVID risk | Supported by RECOVER [@Shah2025]. | Mechanism unresolved; reporting/selection bias not eliminated. |
| Menopause -> long-COVID risk | Unresolved to weak. | Menopause overlaps with age, comorbidity, and symptom profile. |
| Sex hormones -> immune-resolution threshold | Plausible. | Mostly indirect mechanistic evidence. |
| Menopause-associated comorbidity -> severity/recovery | Plausible and supported in acute COVID. | Not the same as PAIS causation. |
| Symptom overlap -> misclassification | Strong practical concern. | Does not explain all female excess. |
| Viral infection -> vascular risk | Supported cross-virus. | Not specific to menopause or fatigue-predominant PAIS. |

## Relevance to This Project

This batch should update `question:0007-mechanism-of-female-predominance-in-pais` toward a more explicit causal-disentanglement frame.
The project should avoid treating "female sex", "estrogen", "menopause", and "women aged 40-54" as interchangeable.
For future analyses, the minimum covariate set should include age, sex assigned at birth, menopausal status, menstrual-cycle status where relevant, hormone therapy type/route/dose, pregnancy status/history, cardiovascular/metabolic comorbidity, acute severity, vaccination/reinfection history, deprivation or access measures, and case definition.

Long-COVID clinics should screen for perimenopause and menopause, but research documents should keep clinical screening separate from causal attribution.
A symptom improvement after HRT may support a hormone-deficiency component for an individual patient, but it does not prove that PAIS itself was hormone-driven.
The **t019 hormone-therapy audit** (`interpretation:0008`, 2026-06-23) formalizes this separation: the corpus contains **no admissible direct HRT→post-acute-PAIS test** — Costeira2021 is acute and symptom-predicted (and vulnerable to, though it does not itself test, menopause-symptom-overlap ascertainment), Newson2021/Stewart2021 are survey/clinical-management, Silva2024 treats hormone therapy as a covariate, and AlcaldeHerraiz2025 confirms the gap — so hormone-therapy evidence enters PAIS work only as clinical-screening and measurement-confound context, never as causal evidence for or against an HRT effect on PAIS.
