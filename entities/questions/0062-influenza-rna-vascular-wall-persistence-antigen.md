# Does influenza RNA detected in arterial walls represent a durable antigen reservoir contributing to post-viral vascular inflammation?

## Summary

Skaarup 2023 cites evidence that influenza virus, its antigens, and influenza RNA have been
detected in the arterial walls of both atherosclerotic and non-atherosclerotic mice, and in
human endothelial cells (Haidari 2010 [@Haidari2010]; Van Lenten 2002; Colden-Stanfield 1993). This establishes
that influenza can access the vascular compartment beyond the lung. The question is whether this
vascular residency is transient (cleared within days of systemic viremia) or whether it represents
a durable reservoir — analogous to SARS-CoV-2 antigen persistence in tissue-resident macrophages
— capable of sustaining chronic low-grade vascular inflammation and contributing to long-term
atherosclerosis acceleration or post-viral cardiovascular sequelae.

## Why It Matters

- Directly bears on whether hypothesis:0002 (tissue reservoir / antigen fragment) applies to
  influenza as a trigger, not only to Borrelia, SARS-CoV-2, or EBV.
- If influenza RNA persists in arterial walls and drives ongoing macrophage recruitment, it
  provides a mechanism for acute influenza infection to accelerate atherosclerotic disease
  independently of the resolution of systemic viremia.
- Risk if unanswered: the vascular-infection finding could be misread as evidence of persistence
  when it is merely acute-viremia trafficking — or the reverse, causing underestimation of the
  post-acute vascular burden.

## Current Evidence

- **Supporting arterial infection — but acute, not post-acute:** Haidari 2010 [@Haidari2010] cultured viable
  influenza A/HK (H3N2) from aorta and heart, not only lung, in atherosclerotic apoE-deficient
  mice, with viral protein and RNA in infected aortas by immunofluorescence and PCR, alongside
  raised arterial inflammatory gene expression, reduced eNOS, and higher plaque macrophage
  density (*Atherosclerosis* 2010;208:90–96, PMID 19665123; reaches this project via
  Skaarup 2023). Sampling was at **day 7 post-infection, the only timepoint assessed**, with
  blood culture negative at that timepoint. That is a real arterial infection rather than
  contemporaneous viraemic trafficking — but it is an **acute-phase** finding and carries no
  information about post-acute persistence.
- **Limiting (no post-acute timepoint):** no located study samples influenza in the arterial
  wall beyond the acute window. Skaarup 2023 does not report the temporal resolution of the
  Van Lenten 2002 study, so its sampling times remain unknown [UNVERIFIED].
- **Analogous viral evidence (SARS-CoV-2):** in the LIINC cohort — enrolled on RNA-confirmed
  prior infection, **not** long-COVID-selected — one or more SARS-CoV-2 antigens were detected
  above pre-pandemic control background through 10–14 months post-onset (absolute excess
  prevalence +5.4%, p = 0.017; 42/171 participants antigen-positive at some post-acute
  timepoint) [@Peluso2024]. The significant late-window result is the composite "any antigen";
  **spike alone was not significant at 10–14 months** (p = 0.12). Symptom linkage was not
  tested, so this establishes antigen persistence, not a persistence→symptom relation.
  Influenza has no comparable dataset.
- **Analogous bacterial evidence (in-scope cross-pathogen analogue):** *Borrelia burgdorferi*
  is an acute-infection trigger, so this is an **in-scope bacterial cross-pathogen analogue**
  under D-003 — not boundary-monitor / read-across material, a category reserved for PACVS and
  non-infectious GWS/FM. In **mice**, polymeric *B. burgdorferi* peptidoglycan retro-orbitally
  injected or delivered by live infection is retained in the liver for weeks to months after
  the agent itself is cleared, while PG from other bacteria clears within 48 h; the **human**
  line is separate — detection of polymeric PG in Lyme-arthritis synovial fluid
  [@McClune2025]. Precedent for a degradation-resistant, **non-viable fragment** reservoir —
  the mechanism `hypothesis:0002` actually emphasises.
- **Against a replication-competent reservoir:** influenza is a non-integrating RNA virus with
  no latency mechanism and an expected clearance timescale of days to weeks. This weighs
  against a *replicating* arterial reservoir.
- **Untested — retained RNA or non-viable antigen:** the absence of a latency mechanism does
  **not** bear on whether influenza RNA or non-viable antigen is *retained* in the vessel wall,
  which is the `hypothesis:0002` mechanism. No located study tests this. The two possibilities
  must be kept separate: ruling out the first does not rule out the second.

## Thoughts

- Current best interpretation: Haidari 2010 [@Haidari2010] establishes **acute arterial infection** — viable
  virus in the aorta at day 7 with blood culture negative — which is more than contemporaneous
  viraemic trafficking, but is not evidence either way about post-acute persistence. Whether
  influenza leaves a durable RNA or non-viable-antigen residue in the vessel wall is
  **untested**, not answered in the negative.
- The major uncertainty is the time course of vascular clearance, and no located study samples
  past the acute window. Detection at ≥2 weeks post-clearance of systemic infection would be
  highly informative; a well-powered negative at that horizon would be equally decisive.
- If influenza is genuinely a transient arterial visitor, the mechanism of influenza→CVD is
  acute-only, and the chronic vascular burden is explained by post-resolution atherosclerosis
  acceleration (secondary, not primary persistence).
