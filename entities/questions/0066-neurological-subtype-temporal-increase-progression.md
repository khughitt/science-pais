# Does the marked increase in neurological long COVID subtype prevalence at 1-2 year follow-up represent progressive neurological involvement or cohort selection bias from severity-enriched longitudinal samples?

## Summary

Hou et al. 2025 reports pooled neurological long COVID subtype prevalence of 13% at <1 year
follow-up, rising to 27% at 1–2 years (from 16 and 7 studies respectively). A similar
increase is seen for the cardiovascular subtype (8% → 20%). Two competing explanations:
(1) progressive or delayed-onset neurological and cardiovascular involvement after SARS-CoV-2
infection — consistent with a chronic, self-sustaining state; or (2) length-biased sampling
in which longer follow-up cohorts are disproportionately composed of hospitalized/severe-
acute patients who had higher baseline neurological risk.

## Why It Matters

- If the temporal increase is progressive, it is a strong signal for the attractor/persistent
  state hypothesis (hypothesis:0001) and argues for escalating neurological surveillance at
  1–2 years post-infection.
- If it is sampling artifact (severity-enriched longitudinal cohorts), it is a key example
  of ascertainment bias shaping apparent disease trajectory (hypothesis:0008) and should not
  inform natural history models.
- Risk if unanswered: clinical guidelines for neurological follow-up may be either under- or
  over-calibrated.

## Current Evidence

- Hou2025: Neurological prevalence 13% (<1 yr, 16 studies) → 27% (1–2+ yr, 7 studies).
  Cardiovascular: 8% → 20%. Hou2025 acknowledges that 44% of 1–2 year studies had
  hospitalized-only cohorts vs. 13% of <1 year studies — a quantified sampling confound.
- Authors note "studies suggest that individual burdens of long COVID symptoms abate over
  time" citing Bek2022, Mariani2023, Martino2022, Terai2023 — suggesting individual
  trajectories improve, in tension with the rising population-level subtype estimates.
- Taquet et al. 2022 (Lancet Psychiatry; cited in Hou2025 as [17]): neurological/psychiatric
  risk trajectories over 2 years in 1.28M patients suggest persisting but not necessarily
  increasing neurological risk.

## Thoughts

- The most likely explanation is a mixture: both genuine delayed neurological manifestation
  (consistent with post-viral neuroinflammation literature) and severity-based sampling
  artifact (longer follow-up cohorts are hospital-enriched). The two effects cannot be
  cleanly separated in this meta-analysis.
- The attractor hypothesis (h0001) would predict persistence, not necessarily increase; an
  increase would require delayed-onset pathophysiology (e.g., neuroinflammation accumulating
  after initial infection clears) — a more specific mechanism claim.
- Major uncertainty: whether the 7 long-follow-up neurological studies have comparable
  definitions and populations to the 16 short-follow-up studies. With only 7 studies, this
  estimate is underpowered.
