## State

The hypothesis asserts that elevated EBV-reactivation markers in post-COVID fatigue reflect non-specific immune dysregulation downstream of acute SARS-CoV-2 severity — not an independently causal post-infectious mechanism. The core testable claim is that the EBV–long-COVID association will not survive adjustment for acute illness severity plus global immune-activation markers. If correct, EBV reactivation is a readout of how disrupted the immune system was, not a driver, and anti-EBV therapy would not improve PAIS outcomes.

Current evidence is observational and cannot adjudicate this claim. cite:Peluso2022 (hypothesis `source_refs`) reports EBV-marker associations with long-COVID fatigue and cognitive symptoms, but the cross-sectional design cannot separate EBV causality from severity confounding — precisely the gap this hypothesis targets. cite:Chen2023 (hypothesis `source_refs`) characterizes the EBV-reactivation mechanism in long COVID as "enigmatic" with causal pathways unestablished, consistent with the epiphenomenon framing. No study has yet performed the full adjustment (acute severity plus global immune activation) needed to test the core claim.

The hypothesis is the deflationary counterpart to an EBV-mechanism reading and instantiates the general mediator-vs-co-traveler frame of question:0022-immune-state-displacement-mediator-vs-co-traveler. The primary discriminating tests concern temporal ordering of EBV reactivation relative to autoantibody emergence (question:0054-ebv-reactivation-autoantibody-emergence-temporal-ordering) and whether prior symptomatic mononucleosis history confounds reactivation-association studies (question:0051-prior-symptomatic-ebv-mononucleosis-as-pais-risk-amplifier). Hypothesis status: proposed; no graph claims or interpretation files exist to elevate any proposition beyond the hypothesis spec.
