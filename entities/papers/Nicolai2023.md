# Thromboinflammation in long COVID—the elusive key to postinfection sequelae?

- **Authors:** Leo Nicolai, Rainer Kaiser, Konstantin Stark
- **Year:** 2023
- **Journal:** Journal of Thrombosis and Haemostasis, vol. 21, no. 8, pp. 2020–2031
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jtha.2023.04.039
- **BibTeX key:** Nicolai2023
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This review synthesizes evidence that thromboinflammation — the pathological interplay between immune activation, platelet hyperreactivity, neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, coagulation dysregulation, and endothelial injury — persists into the post-acute sequelae phase of COVID-19 (PASC) and constitutes one mechanistic driver of long COVID pathophysiology. The authors present a structured framework of three intertwined hypotheses for sustained thromboinflammation in PASC: (1) lasting structural vascular changes (most prominently endothelial damage from acute infection); (2) persistent viral reservoir; and (3) dysregulated immune responses driven by a misguided immune system. The review also tabulates comparative evidence across acute COVID-19, long COVID, and ME/CFS — highlighting where the syndromes share thromboinflammatory features and where they diverge — and identifies clinical gaps that require large, well-characterized longitudinal cohorts to resolve.

## Methods

This is a narrative review article (submitted February 2023; published in J Thromb Haemost 2023). The authors synthesize published cohort studies, mechanistic studies, meta-analyses, and imaging studies published up to approximately May 2023. Key evidence types included:
- Prospective and retrospective cohort studies of vascular/coagulation biomarkers (VWF, FVIII, ADAMTS-13, D-dimer, thrombomodulin) in PASC patients
- Microfluidic and flow-cytometry assays of platelet binding to VWF/collagen and platelet-neutrophil aggregate (PNA) formation
- Imaging studies: chest CT, SPECT ventilation/perfusion imaging, MRI (brain grey matter thickness, white matter changes)
- Longitudinal multiomic profiling (Klein et al. 2022, n=209; Schultheiß et al. 2022, n=150 in D-dimer cohort)
- Autopsy studies (SARS-CoV-2 persistence up to 230 days post-infection in multiple organs)
- Comparative ME/CFS data drawn from Table 1

No new primary data are presented. The review covers ~120 references. Limitations of the field are explicitly discussed (lack of control arms, selection bias in most existing cohorts, technical constraints on platelet/neutrophil functional assays after freezing).

## Key Findings

**Persistent endothelial dysfunction (Section 3.1)**
- Elevated VWF antigen, factor VIII, and thrombomodulin consistently found in PASC patients across multiple cohort studies (refs 33–35), indicating ongoing endothelial activation/injury.
- ADAMTS-13 (the VWF-cleaving protease) is reduced, creating a prothrombotic VWF:ADAMTS-13 imbalance (refs 36–38).
- Endothelial colony-forming cells elevated post-COVID-19, indicating endothelial injury and attempted repair (refs 39–40).
- Microcirculatory function impaired by postocclusion reactive hyperemia in a subset of PASC patients (ref 41).
- Impaired exercise capacity in PASC correlated 4× higher VWF Antigen/ADAMTS-13 ratio vs non-affected individuals (Prasannan et al., n=21 PASC, ref 77).

**Coagulation abnormalities (Section 3.2)**
- Enhanced thrombin-generating capacity and plasma hypofibrinolysis reported at 4 months post-hospitalization (von Meijienfeldt et al., ref 45), though fibrinogen and prothrombin time normalized.
- Meta-analysis of 15 publications (47,910 adults): D-dimer elevated in 20% of patients (95% CI, 6–39%), more prominent than CRP (8%; 95% CI, 5–12%) or IL-6 (3%; 95% CI, 1–7%), pointing toward persistent coagulation activation (ref 88).
- D-dimer sustained for mean 80.5 days post-acute disease in 150 PASC patients (Townsend et al., ref 69); also elevated in 29% of subjects who did not require hospitalization.
- A longitudinal assessment at up to 16 months post-infection confirmed elevated D-dimer alongside elevated IL-6, indicating ongoing fibrin formation and systemic inflammation (ref 68).

**Platelet-neutrophil phenotype in PASC (Section 3.3)**
- Platelet hyperreactivity is the predominant finding in PASC (Martins-Gonçalves et al., ref 47), with persistent platelet hyperreactivity in COVID-19 survivors.
- In n=21 PASC patients, microfluidic assays showed significantly increased platelet binding to VWF and collagen, correlating with circulating VWF antigen levels (Constantinou-Bercu et al., ref 87).
- Neutrophils in PASC show a CD10-low low-density hyperreactive phenotype, with increased PNA formation and NETosis compared to controls with no residual COVID-19 symptoms (Dean et al., ref 51; Pisareva et al., ref 52).
- In severe COVID-19, persistent interstitial lung changes correlated with elevated neutrophil counts and increased plasma NET-associated proteins (George et al., ref 53).

**Microvascular thrombosis (Section 3.4)**
- D-dimer, chest CT ground-glass opacities, and SPECT perfusion defects suggest small-vessel occlusion in patients with dyspnea (refs 73–76).
- Four SPECT studies in symptomatic PASC patients with dyspnea showed peripheral perfusion defects despite no structural correlate — consistent with microclot-driven obstruction (refs 74–76).
- Reduction in brain grey matter thickness (Douaud et al., n=UK Biobank, ref 79) and white matter microstructural changes on MRI at 3 months (Qin et al., ref 78) in COVID-19 survivors suggest central nervous system microvascular injury.
- MRI evidence of long-term myocardial inflammation in some long COVID patients (refs 42, 26) may indicate microvascular injury/thrombosis.

**Macrovascular thrombosis (Section 3.5)**
- Population-wide cohort of 48 million (Knight et al., ref 92): HR for arterial thrombosis 1.34 (95% CI, 1.21–1.48) and for venous thrombotic events 1.80 (95% CI, 1.50–2.17) after COVID-19 infection.
- Xie et al. (ref 90, n>70,000): increased risk of cerebrovascular disorders, arrhythmias, myocarditis, heart failure, and thromboembolic disease beyond 30 days post-infection.
- Arterial and venous thrombotic events at high 90-day post-discharge rates; ameliorated by anticoagulation regimens (Giannis et al., ref 91).

**Three mechanistic hypotheses (Section 4)**
1. *Lasting structural changes / endotheliopathy:* Virally-induced endothelial damage persists because viral clearance lags behind vascular repair; vasculopathy maintains a procoagulant state via mechanisms active in acute COVID-19 (refs 93–94). Correlates with Omicron's lower long COVID rates due to reduced lung/vascular involvement (ref 95).
2. *Viral persistence:* SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acids and immunoreactivity found in small bowel of 7/14 asymptomatic individuals at 4 months (Gaebler et al., ref 97); SARS-CoV-2 RNA in gut mucosa of 24/46 IBD colonoscopy patients reporting long COVID symptoms (Zollner et al., ref 98); virus in multiple organs up to 230 days in autopsy study (Stein et al., ref 99). Vaccinations lower PASC incidence (refs 100–101), supporting viral persistence as one driver. Caution: RNA studies detected spike protein or viral nucleic acids but could not demonstrate viable virus (ref 98).
3. *Dysregulated immune response:* S100A8 and HMGB1 alarmin upregulation in PASC (ref 102); increased inflammatory markers at 9 weeks post-acute infection correlating with severity (ref 103); IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF cytokine triad elevated up to 24 months after acute illness (Schultheiß et al., ref 63); Phetsouphanh et al. (ref 64, n=209) found risk factors including EBV reactivation, SARSCoV-2 RNAemia, and autoantibodies (n=209 longitudinal multiomics). EBV reactivation and latent HIV infection increase likelihood of long COVID (ref 105).

**Comparison with ME/CFS (Table 1, Section 6)**
- ME/CFS shows evidence of peripheral/microvascular endothelial dysfunction and endothelin-1 upregulation (refs 43–44), but not macrovascular thrombosis.
- Platelet activation evidence is conflicting in ME/CFS; coagulation data are very limited.
- No evidence of microvascular clotting in ME/CFS (contrast: PASC has direct imaging evidence in a subset).
- Neutrophils in ME/CFS show increased apoptosis rather than the hyperactivated NETosis phenotype seen in PASC.

**Clinical management and ongoing trials (Section 5)**
- Expert guidelines do not recommend anticoagulation post-discharge unless prior VTE indications exist (refs 106–107).
- STIMULATE-ICP trial (ISRCTN10665760) to recruit 4,500 long COVID patients; evaluating rivaroxaban (factor Xa inhibitor), colchicine, and antihistamines.
- Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid) shows promise in case reports and primary prevention trials; NCT05595369 to evaluate for long COVID.
- Vaccination after SARS-CoV-2 infection decreased PASC incidence in a study of 28,356 participants (Tannous et al., ref 101).

## Relevance

This review addresses the thromboinflammatory arm of the PAIS research question directly — specifically the question of **why some individuals fail to recover homeostatic coagulation, endothelial, and immune function after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection.** Its relevance to the project's PAIS frame is threefold:

1. **Mechanism of failed homeostatic recovery:** The three hypotheses (endotheliopathy, viral persistence, immune dysregulation) map precisely onto the project's framing of PAIS as a failure to return to baseline homeostasis. Crucially, the review argues these are mechanistically entangled — not competing — and likely co-occurring in subsets of patients, consistent with the project's multimechanism view.

2. **Thromboinflammation as a cross-cutting PAIS mechanism:** While the review focuses on long COVID, Table 1 extends comparisons to ME/CFS and acknowledges the pre-COVID literature on post-viral syndrome. The endothelial dysfunction findings in ME/CFS (endothelin-1, peripheral microvascular dysfunction) overlap with, but are less severe than, PASC thromboinflammation — suggesting a shared but variable-penetrance phenotypic dimension across PAIS entities.

3. **Biomarker and therapeutic implications:** D-dimer, VWF:ADAMTS-13 ratio, and PNA formation are named as candidate biomarkers that could serve as objective indicators of failed coagulation homeostasis. The STIMULATE-ICP and nirmatrelvir trials provide directly actionable leads for therapeutic approaches targeting this mechanism. This is directly relevant to the project's interest in diagnostic biomarkers and treatment strategies in the post-infectious context.

The review explicitly calls for "large, well-characterized clinical cohorts" with fine-grained longitudinal assessment of coagulation, vascular function, and platelet/neutrophil parameters — a need the project's computational track is positioned to help address as datasets become available.

## Limitations

- Narrative review: no systematic search strategy or PRISMA; findings are selectively synthesized, not meta-analyzed. Prevalence estimates (e.g., 20% D-dimer elevation) are drawn from a cited meta-analysis but individual study designs vary widely.
- Most cohort studies cited lack matched controls, rely on patient self-selection, and have high risk of selection bias toward more symptomatic individuals; the authors acknowledge this explicitly.
- The "PASC" populations across studies use inconsistent case definitions (WHO vs. CDC vs. study-specific symptom cutoffs); apparent mechanistic heterogeneity may partly reflect case-definition heterogeneity.
- Platelet and neutrophil functional assays require fresh (non-frozen) samples, severely limiting large-scale multi-site studies; most mechanistic data come from small convenience samples.
- The comparison between PASC and ME/CFS in Table 1 is based on sparse and methodologically heterogeneous ME/CFS data; equivalence or difference claims should be interpreted cautiously.
- Viral persistence data from gut biopsies detect RNA/spike protein but cannot confirm viable, replication-competent virus — the authors note this explicitly. Whether non-infectious viral fragments drive thromboinflammation remains unresolved.
- Vaccination effects on PASC (and thus on thromboinflammation) are heterogeneous across the cited studies, and selection bias in vaccine-then-infected cohorts is likely.
- Most data are from adults; pediatric PASC thromboinflammation is not addressed.

## Follow-up

- Read the Pretorius et al. (ref 34) microclot paper and Martins-Gonçalves et al. (ref 47) platelet hyperreactivity paper for primary evidence on microclots and platelet dysfunction in PASC.
- Constantinou-Bercu et al. (ref 87) microfluidic thrombogenicity study in n=21 PASC is a key primary data source for the VWF-platelet binding axis; worth reading in full.
- Prasannan et al. (ref 77) on VWF:ADAMTS-13 ratio and exercise capacity (Blood Adv 2022) is directly relevant to biomarker development.
- Klein et al. (ref 62) longitudinal multiomics in 209 PASC subjects (with EBV reactivation, autoantibody, and RNAemia risk factors) provides the most comprehensive mechanistic profiling study cited; obtain primary paper.
- Townsend et al. (ref 69) D-dimer cohort (n=150, 80.5-day sustained elevation) provides key coagulation timeline data.
- Evaluate results from STIMULATE-ICP (rivaroxaban arm) when published — directly tests the thromboinflammation hypothesis with a large RCT design.
- Consider how the VWF:ADAMTS-13 imbalance model and D-dimer trajectory relate to the dysautonomia / small-fiber neuropathy arm of PASC (potential microvascular occlusion as a shared upstream cause).
- Assess whether the "three mechanistic hypotheses" framework here (endotheliopathy, viral persistence, immune dysregulation) can be aligned or merged with the four-hypothesis framework in Choutka2022 (pathogen persistence, autoimmunity, dysbiosis/reactivation, tissue damage).
