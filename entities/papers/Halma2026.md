# Autoimmunity, Vascular Dysfunction and Metabolic Alterations: A Trifecta of Impacts in Postacute COVID-19 Vaccination Syndrome (PACVS)

- **Authors:** Matthew Halma, Sirjana Dhillon, Carlos Gracidas, Sidra Hassaan, Paolo Bellavite, Giuseppe Di Fede, Joseph Varon
- **Year:** 2026
- **Book:** Vaccine Development – Lessons Learned and Future Trends (IntechOpen)
- **DOI:** 10.5772/intechopen.1014628
- **BibTeX key:** Halma2026
- **Source:** Full-text PDF (papers/pdfs/2026_Halma_autoimmunity-vascular-metabolic-pacvs.pdf), read 2026-06-20.

> **Scope note:** This chapter concerns **post-acute COVID-19 vaccination syndrome (PACVS)** — a post-vaccination phenomenon — not a post-infectious syndrome. PACVS is therefore **outside the primary scope** of this project (which studies post-acute infection syndromes, PAIS). It is included as a **mechanistic comparator**: the proposed autoimmunity/vascular/metabolic "trifecta" overlaps with PAIS candidate mechanisms, and the chapter explicitly draws mechanistic parallels with long COVID/PACS. The PACVS literature and several of these authors occupy contested/heterodox terrain; claims are held at their evidence level throughout this summary.

## Key Contribution

This book chapter proposes a three-axis mechanistic framework for PACVS: (1) **metabolic dysfunction** centered on mitochondrial energetic failure and impaired fatty-acid oxidation, (2) **autoimmunity** driven by G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) and renin-angiotensin system (RAS) autoantibodies, and (3) **vascular pathology** encompassing clotting abnormalities (ranging from acute VITT to chronic microclot burden) and endothelial dysfunction. The chapter argues that the same three axes operate in long COVID/PACS, positioning the spike protein — whether delivered by infection or vaccine — as a shared mechanistic driver. Each axis is mapped to therapeutic candidates, though the authors acknowledge that clinical validation is very limited and that a single relevant case series in the literature has been retracted.

## Methods

This is a **narrative review chapter** — no primary data, no systematic literature search, no pre-registered protocol. The approach is:

- Selective citation of mechanistic studies, observational cohorts, case series, and case reports to argue for each of the three proposed axes in PACVS and by analogy in PACS/long COVID.
- Cross-referencing PACVS patient survey data (ref. [107]: Mantovani et al. Biomedicines 2024;12:2852 — a retrospective case-series study) for autoantibody prevalence figures.
- Epidemiologic data on VITT/TTS from pharmacovigilance registries and self-controlled case series.
- Mechanistic extrapolation from in-vitro, animal, and structural-biology studies to clinical phenotypes.
- Proposed therapeutic interventions drawn primarily from long COVID / ME/CFS evidence rather than PACVS-specific trials (none exist for most interventions reviewed).

No formal risk-of-bias assessment, no meta-analytic pooling, and no controlled PACVS cohort data are presented. The evidence base is heterogeneous and often indirect.

## Key Findings

### Metabolic axis

- Multiomics studies of long COVID/PACS show disrupted TCA-cycle intermediates, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, elevated acyl-carnitines, and defective beta-oxidation; the authors assert the same pattern is found in PACVS plasma metabolomics [refs 9–15, 21–23].
- Spike protein structural biology reveals a conserved free-fatty-acid (FFA) binding pocket that can sequester linoleic acid and modulate lipid metabolism [refs 16–17]; cell-based experiments link spike exposure to mitochondrial fragmentation, mitophagy inhibition, and ROS production [refs 18–19]. [UNVERIFIED whether these in-vitro findings hold quantitatively in PACVS patients specifically.]
- Persistent circulating spike protein is reported months-to-years post-vaccination [ref 20]; metabolomic changes in post-vaccination cohorts reportedly mirror PACS [refs 21–23]. The chapter treats this as establishing a "common pathway," but direct controlled PACVS metabolomics data are sparse and largely drawn by analogy from PACS studies.
- CPET studies in PACS show lactate-threshold impairment and fat-oxidation deficits; no equivalent PACVS-specific CPET trial is cited.
- Therapeutic proposals: low-intensity aerobic conditioning, buffering strategies (NaHCO3, sodium citrate), hypercapnic/CO2-restoration training, fasting to upregulate beta-oxidation, BCAAs, CoQ10, metformin, baicalin. Most evidence is from ME/CFS or long COVID, not PACVS.

### Autoimmunity axis

- PACVS patients tested for GPCR autoantibodies showed: MAS1 (94%), ACE2 (65%), CHRM4 (59%), ADRA1 (53%), ADRB2 (53%) positivity in one retrospective case-series (Mantovani et al. [ref 107]). [UNVERIFIED — this is a single retrospective case series; controls and selection criteria unclear; the prevalence figures appear high and require replication.]
- Autoantibodies targeting adrenergic and cholinergic GPCR receptors, ACE2, MAS1, AT1R, ETAR, CXCR3, Stab1, FGFR3, and PF4 are identified as clinically relevant markers [refs 109–113].
- Mechanistic hypotheses for autoantibody generation: (a) **molecular mimicry** between spike protein peptides and human proteins (adenosine receptor A2b, adiponectin, C163A, CREB1, IL-10, pericardial proteins, thrombopoietin, tropomyosin, angiotensin II) [refs 114–123]; (b) **anti-idiotype antibodies** against anti-spike antibodies generating anti-ACE2 autoantibodies [ref 107]; (c) **HLA-allele-specific binding** of spike peptides (RRARSVAS, DEDDSEPV) associated with neurological and inflammatory autoimmune risk [refs 124–125]. [SPECULATION: the chapter states it is "very likely" that vaccines induce cross-reactive T lymphocytes via molecular mimicry — this is an author inference extrapolating from structural homology data, not established by controlled immunological studies in PACVS patients.]
- A systematic review with meta-analysis (Wang et al. [ref 67]) found pooled RR of SLE = 1.50 (95% CI 1.05–2.12) and RA = 1.32 (95% CI 1.09–1.60) across heterogeneous vaccinations; the chapter cites this but notes it pertains to many vaccine types and insufficient evidence exists to isolate COVID-19 vaccines.
- Therapeutics proposed: glutathione (redox restoration), hesperidin and quercetin (flavonoids with spike/ACE2 binding affinity and anti-inflammatory properties), angiotensin 1–7 (to restore ACE2/Ang1-7/MAS1 axis), plasmapheresis, IVIG. Clinical validation in PACVS is largely anecdotal or extrapolated from case reports and observational studies; no RCTs for PACVS are cited.

### Vascular / clotting axis

- **Acute VITT/TTS:** Well-documented rare complication of adenoviral-vector vaccines (ChAdOx1: ~1/64,000–125,000; Ad26.COV2.S: ~1/200,000–310,000); anti-PF4 antibody mediated. mRNA vaccines have not shown VITT/TTS above background [refs 159–168]. This is the strongest controlled-evidence claim in the chapter.
- **Chronic microclot burden in PACVS:** The chapter draws heavily on the "microclotting hypothesis" (Pretorius/Kell) — protease-resistant fibrin amyloid microclots, elevated serum amyloid A1/A2, coagulation factors X/XI, and downregulated immune-regulatory proteins [refs 181, 186–188]. The chapter asserts this same pattern occurs across COVID-19, PACS, and PACVS, with the spike S1 subunit inducing fibrinogen-resistant-to-fibrinolysis conformational changes. [UNVERIFIED — the microclotting hypothesis is contested in long COVID literature; the PACVS extension relies on indirect evidence; references include a preprint [ref 181] and a preprint/research square item [ref 194].]
- Platelet hyperreactivity (P-selectin, CD63, GPIIb/IIIa activation markers) is reported in COVID-19 survivors and long COVID; the chapter extends this to PACVS by analogy [refs 169–178].
- **Endothelial dysfunction:** Acute vasculitic syndromes post-COVID-19 vaccination (leukocytoclastic vasculitis, IgA vasculitis, ANCA-vasculitis, large-vessel vasculitis) are documented in case reports and case series [refs 198–218]. Mechanism attributed to vaccine-induced spike expression triggering innate immune activation, cytokine release, complement activation, and immune-complex deposition. Incidence remains lower than post-infection endotheliopathy.
- **Epidemiologic vascular signals:** National SCCS of 29 million (ChAdOx1) showed transient thrombocytopenia increase and slight VTE elevation; Scandinavian data linked AZD1222 to CVT. Hong Kong data identified hundreds of thromboembolic/hemorrhagic stroke cases within 28 days of BNT162b2/CoronaVac (>8.6 million doses). These are pharmacovigilance-level signals, not adjusted incidence comparisons to unvaccinated baseline with modern confounding controls.
- Triple anticoagulant therapy (DAPT + DOAC) was associated with symptom improvement in 91 long COVID patients in an **unpublished preprint** [ref 194]. The chapter notes no studies evaluate this in PACVS specifically. The clotting hypothesis "remains controversial and requires verification" (authors' own words).
- Plasmapheresis: a phase II RCT in long COVID showed no efficacy [ref 196]; the chapter acknowledges this.

## Relevance

**Scope boundary — this is a mechanistic comparator, not a core in-scope syndrome.** PACVS is triggered by COVID-19 vaccination, not by acute infection, and thus falls outside the primary PAIS frame (which requires post-infectious etiology). The relevance to this project is as a **boundary case** that illuminates mechanism overlap:

1. **question:0009 (functional autoantibodies drive dysautonomia):** The GPCR autoantibody data in PACVS (MAS1, ACE2, ADRA1, ADRB2, CHRM4) directly parallels the functional autoantibody hypothesis in PAIS/long COVID. The mechanistic pathways proposed — anti-idiotype antibody formation, molecular mimicry, HLA-mediated T-cell cross-reactivity — are the same candidate mechanisms under discussion in the PAIS frame. The chapter strengthens the plausibility argument but does not provide controlled evidence sufficient to confirm causation in PACVS, let alone by extension in PAIS.

2. **topic:post-infectious-dysautonomia-and-autoimmunity:** The GPCR autoantibody phenotype (tachycardia, hypertension, burning sensations, fainting, visual disturbance) described in PACVS [ref 107] mirrors the dysautonomia cluster in PAIS. If the same autoantibodies operate in both post-infection and post-vaccination contexts, the trigger (antigen exposure route) may matter less than the immune response it generates.

3. **topic:thromboinflammation-and-endothelial-dysfunction:** The spike S1 / fibrin amyloid / microclot mechanism is presented as shared between PACS and PACVS, positioning the spike protein as the effector regardless of delivery route. This is relevant to the thromboinflammation topic but the shared-mechanism claim requires controlled head-to-head comparisons that do not yet exist.

4. **hypothesis:0001-shared-dysregulated-attractor:** The "trifecta" framing (metabolic + autoimmune + vascular failure modes interacting) is conceptually consistent with a shared attractor state across PAIS. The chapter makes this argument explicitly but does not provide formal modeling or pathway analysis; it is a narrative synthesis of plausibility arguments.

## Limitations

**Evidence quality and study design:**
- This is a **narrative review with no systematic search or GRADE assessment**; citation selection is not transparent and likely reflects the authors' priors.
- The primary PACVS autoantibody data (Mantovani et al. [ref 107]) is a retrospective case series with no healthy control group; prevalence figures (e.g., MAS1 94%) are striking but unconfirmed. Replication in controlled cohorts with pre-specified case definitions is absent.
- Most mechanistic claims about PACVS are **inferred by analogy** from long COVID / PACS / ME/CFS studies; direct PACVS-specific data are sparse.
- The microclotting hypothesis, while extensively cited, remains contested in the long COVID field itself; the extension to PACVS rests on even thinner primary evidence.
- The triple anticoagulant therapy evidence base is a single **unpublished preprint** in long COVID — not PACVS — and is described by the authors as preliminary.
- The chapter's conclusion acknowledges "clinical validation of therapeutics is limited, with a single retracted case series in the literature" — a notable self-indictment of the evidence base.

**Conflict of interest:** Authors PB (Bellavite) and GDF (Di Fede) disclosed consultation with Vanda Omeopatici s.r.l. (a natural medicines/food supplements company), though the company had no role in study design or writing. Several of the lead authors (Halma, Varon) have published extensively in the PACVS advocacy space and co-authored the foundational PACVS recognition papers cited here [refs 1–2], introducing potential confirmation bias.

**Contested terrain:** PACVS as a clinical entity is not recognized by major regulatory bodies or clinical societies as of the publication date. The syndrome's case definition, prevalence, and causal attribution to vaccination are actively contested. The heterodox positioning of this author group should be noted when citing mechanistic claims.

**PAIS scope boundary:** The chapter makes a strong claim that PACVS and PACS share the same biology; this is plausible but not demonstrated by controlled comparison. The project should treat the mechanism overlap as a **hypothesis to test**, not an established parallel.

**What was not addressed:** No dose-response or temporal relationship between vaccination and PACVS onset across a defined cohort; no comparison of PACVS incidence across vaccine platforms in a controlled epidemiologic design; no formal pathway or network modeling of the proposed trifecta; no patient stratification by sex, age, HLA type, or prior COVID-19 exposure.

- Scholkmann F, May CA (ref [182], Pathology Research and Practice 2023;246:154497) — systematic comparison of PACS and PCVS similarities/differences.
- Ryu JK et al. (ref [183], Nature 2024;633:905–913) — fibrin-driven thromboinflammation in COVID-19; relevant to topic:thromboinflammation-and-endothelial-dysfunction.

**Questions this raises:**
- Are GPCR autoantibody prevalences (MAS1, ADRA1, ADRB2, CHRM4) similar between controlled PACVS and PACS cohorts, or do they differ by etiology (infection vs. vaccination)?
- Does persistent spike protein post-vaccination reproduce the same metabolomics signature as post-infection PACS, or are there quantitative differences? No controlled head-to-head metabolomics comparison is cited.
- What is the actual prevalence of PACVS meeting a case definition with required temporal criteria in a large vaccinated population with appropriate unvaccinated controls?
