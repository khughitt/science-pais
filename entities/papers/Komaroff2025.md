# Growing recognition of post-acute infection syndromes

- **Authors:** Anthony L. Komaroff
- **Year:** 2025
- **Journal:** Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, vol. 122, no. 29, e2513877122
- **DOI:** 10.1073/pnas.2513877122
- **BibTeX key:** Komaroff2025
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This PNAS commentary by Anthony Komaroff (Harvard/Brigham and Women's) argues that ME/CFS and long COVID are two examples of a newly recognized, broader category — post-acute infection syndromes (PAIS) — which are themselves a subcategory of infection-associated chronic illnesses (IACIs). Using the survey study by Eckey et al. (nearly 4,000 participants with ME/CFS and/or long COVID) as a launching point, Komaroff makes the case that the striking symptom overlap, shared comorbidities, and similar treatment responses in the two illnesses reflect convergent underlying pathophysiology. He proposes a three-category taxonomy of IACIs (new organ injury, accelerated disease incidence, and PAIS) and argues that insights from studying ME/CFS and long COVID together will apply to an even larger class of post-infectious conditions.

## Methods

This is an invited commentary; it contains no original data collection. The commentary synthesizes evidence from Eckey et al. 2025 (a cross-sectional survey of ~4,000 people with ME/CFS and/or long COVID, self-reported diagnoses and treatment responses), alongside the broader PAIS and ME/CFS mechanistic literature. Key limitations of the Eckey et al. survey acknowledged by Komaroff: diagnoses and treatment responses were self-reported, not confirmed by objective testing or randomized controlled trials.

## Key Findings

**Scope of the burden**
- ME/CFS affects up to 3.1 million people in the United States alone, with direct and indirect expenses of approximately $36 to $51 billion annually.
- The cumulative incidence of long COVID may be as high as 400 million individuals globally; total costs to the US and global economy (excluding direct healthcare costs) may reach several trillion dollars over the next 5–10 years.

**Symptom overlap between ME/CFS and long COVID**
- The Eckey et al. survey (n ≈ 4,000) found the frequency of each of a large set of symptoms to be very similar in both illnesses, providing survey-level cross-validation of clinical impressions.
- Both illnesses share post-exertional malaise (PEM), unrefreshing sleep, cognitive impairment ("brain fog"), and dysautonomia as core features.
- Subgroups within each illness may have distinct predominant symptoms, distinct underlying pathophysiology, and distinct treatment responses.

**Shared comorbidities**
- Both illnesses frequently co-occur with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), migraine, dysautonomia, anxiety and depression, mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS), Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS)/joint hypermobility, and attention deficit disorder (ADD).

**Similar treatment responses**
- Responses to specific treatments were similar across ME/CFS and long COVID at the level of individual symptoms; drugs most effective against particular symptoms would be expected to improve those same symptoms in both illnesses.

**PAIS as a class**
- Over the past century there have been many reports of illnesses with very similar symptoms following multiple different acute bacterial, viral, fungal, and protozoal infections.
- Long COVID is definitively a PAIS; ME/CFS likely often is (the inciting infectious agent has not always been pursued by physicians).

**Infection-associated chronic illnesses (IACIs) taxonomy (Fig. 1)**
Komaroff proposes three categories of IACIs:
1. *New organ injury* — acute tissue damage, smoldering inflammation, autoimmune injury (examples: multiple sclerosis post-EBV, duodenal ulcer post-*H. pylori*).
2. *Accelerated incidence of diseases* — diseases whose natural progression was accelerated by the acute infection (examples: new atherosclerotic disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, dementias post-COVID).
3. *Post-acute infection syndromes (PAIS)* — the core class: fatigue, cognitive impairment, PEM, unrefreshing sleep, orthostatic intolerance, and other symptoms persisting months to years after acute infection (examples: long COVID, ME/CFS, post-Lyme syndrome).

**Candidate pathophysiological mechanisms**
- Nucleic acids and antigens from the infectious agent can persist in host tissues, potentially driving chronic inflammatory response and autoimmunity.
- Living organisms (intact pathogens) may sometimes persist and trigger ongoing immune stimulation.
- Tissue damage from the acute infection can produce a physiological response to ongoing threat, including mitochondrial dysfunction, autoantibodies against neural targets, endothelial dysfunction, proinflammatory dysbiosis of gut microbiome, autonomic dysfunction, redox imbalance, and reactivation of latent infectious agents.
- Symptoms may also arise from orchestrated behavioral and metabolic responses to vital threats ("hunkering down" responses) driven by persistence of infectious agents or tissue damage, which can reinforce each other in physiological vicious cycles.
- Critically, Komaroff notes there is little evidence that treating any single identified abnormality, alone, cures any PAIS — consistent with a multi-mechanism, mutually reinforcing pathophysiology.

**Key open questions identified**
- Are there distinct subgroups with different underlying pathophysiology, treatment responses, and prognosis?
- Are the same frequent comorbidities seen in ME/CFS and long COVID also seen in other PAIS?
- Why are only some infectious agents capable of eliciting PAIS?
- Do the same underlying pathophysiological abnormalities seen in ME/CFS and long COVID also exist in other PAIS?
- Which abnormalities can be targeted by currently available or novel therapies?
- Might people with other PAIS benefit from the same treatments as people with ME/CFS and long COVID — treatments involving single therapeutics or a "cocktail" targeting different underlying physiological abnormalities?

## Relevance

This commentary directly addresses the project's core research question — "Why do some people fail to recover after acute infection, and what shared mechanisms link post-acute infection syndromes?" — on multiple levels:

1. **PAIS as a shared failure mode:** Komaroff explicitly frames ME/CFS, long COVID, and related conditions as members of a coherent class defined by failed recovery of physiological homeostasis after acute infection. This is the most explicit mainstream scientific endorsement to date of the project's central organizing frame.

2. **IACI taxonomy as a project framework contribution:** The three-category taxonomy (new organ injury / accelerated disease incidence / PAIS) provides a structured scaffold for scoping what belongs in this project versus adjacent projects (e.g., accelerated neurodegeneration post-COVID might cross-link to pan-disease or health-meta).

3. **Mechanistic convergence argument:** The observation that both illnesses respond similarly to symptom-targeted treatments — despite being triggered by different pathogens — supports the project's "shared dysregulated attractor" hypothesis and argues that common downstream mechanisms, not pathogen-specific biology, drive chronicity.

4. **Mutual reinforcement / vicious-cycle framing:** The explicit proposal that multiple physiological abnormalities reinforce each other and resist single-target treatment maps directly onto the project's hypothesis that PAIS represents a stable failure-mode attractor rather than a transient homeostatic perturbation.

5. **Broad scope of burden:** The quantitative burden estimates (400M long COVID cases globally, $36–51B US ME/CFS costs annually, potential multi-trillion-dollar global economic burden) underscore the public-health imperative driving the project's research questions.

## Limitations

- This is a commentary, not a primary research paper; no new data are presented and all quantitative claims derive from cited sources.
- The Eckey et al. survey on which the commentary heavily relies is cross-sectional, self-reported (diagnoses and treatment responses unconfirmed by objective testing or RCTs), and subject to selection bias.
- The proposed three-category IACI taxonomy (Fig. 1) is conceptual and lacks operationalized diagnostic criteria; boundary cases (e.g., post-COVID neurodegeneration straddling "accelerated disease" and "PAIS") are not resolved.
- The commentary does not address the full breadth of PAIS beyond ME/CFS and long COVID (e.g., post-Lyme, post-Ebola, post-Q-fever) in any quantitative detail.
- The "physiological vicious cycles" and "hunkering down" mechanistic model is presented as plausible but speculative; no discriminating evidence is cited to test it against competing frameworks.
- The claim that all different PAIS share similar underlying pathophysiology "has not yet been systematically studied in other PAIS" (acknowledged by Komaroff directly).
- Global long COVID cumulative incidence estimate (400 million) is an upper-bound from a cited source and may overestimate ongoing prevalence.

## Follow-up

- Read the companion article by Eckey et al. (Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A. 122, e2426874122, 2025) — the survey of ~4,000 ME/CFS and long COVID patients on which this commentary is directly based.
- Examine primary mechanistic papers cited: Peluso et al. on tissue-based T cell activation and viral RNA persistence up to 2 years after SARS-CoV-2 infection (Sci. Transl. Med. 16, eadk3520, 2024); Stein et al. on SARS-CoV-2 reservoir in the human body and brain at autopsy (Nature 612, 2022); Proal et al. on targeting the SARS-CoV-2 reservoir in long COVID (Lancet Infect. Dis. 25, 2025).
- Assess how the three-category IACI taxonomy affects project scoping: does accelerated atherosclerosis/neurodegeneration post-COVID belong in this project or should it be cross-linked only?
- Evaluate whether the comorbidity cluster (POTS, MCAS, EDS/joint hypermobility, ADD, migraine, anxiety/depression) seen in both ME/CFS and long COVID is also documented in post-Lyme, post-Q-fever, and post-Ebola cohorts.
- Komaroff's "cocktail of therapeutics" framing raises a potential computational question: can multi-target treatment response patterns be predicted from multi-omics or clinical subgroup profiles?
