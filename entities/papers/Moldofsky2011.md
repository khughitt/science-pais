# Chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, depression and disordered sleep in chronic post-SARS syndrome; a case-controlled study

- **Authors:** Harvey Moldofsky, John Patcai
- **Year:** 2011
- **Journal:** BMC Neurology, vol. 11, p. 37
- **DOI:** 10.1186/1471-2377-11-37
- **BibTeX key:** Moldofsky2011
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This is the first report of long-term adverse effects of SARS on sleep physiology and somatic symptoms, demonstrating that a chronic post-SARS syndrome — characterized by persistent fatigue, diffuse myalgia, weakness, depression, and nonrestorative sleep with objective EEG anomalies — develops in a subset of survivors unable to return to work more than a year after acute illness. The study demonstrates strong phenotypic overlap between chronic post-SARS and both fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) and chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS), suggesting a shared post-infectious neuro-sleep dysregulation mechanism. The paper is particularly notable as early evidence that a coronavirus can drive the same persistent failure-to-recover phenotype now recognized as the dominant feature of long COVID.

## Methods

**Design:** Retrospective case-controlled study with two comparison groups (healthy controls and fibromyalgia patients). Ethics approval obtained from University Health Network; all subjects provided informed consent.

**Post-SARS cohort (n=22):** 19 females and 3 males, mean age 46.29 ± 11.02 years, BMI 28.26 ± 6.88. All but one were healthcare workers from the Toronto 2003 SARS outbreak. Subjects were assessed mean 19.8 months after acute SARS onset (range 13–36 months) and were selected from a cohort of 50 chronically ill SARS survivors under care of the Ontario Workers' Safety and Insurance Board (WSIB), unable to return to hospital work.

**Healthy controls (n=7–8):** Younger females (mean age 30 ± 6.7 years, p=0.0002), comparable BMI (24.8 ± 6.1, p=0.242).

**FMS comparison group (n=21):** Drug-free female patients, mean age 42.4 ± 11.8 years, meeting ACR 1990 fibromyalgia criteria; data from a prior double-blind placebo-controlled drug trial (pre-treatment baseline only).

**Instruments:**
- Wahler Physical Symptom Inventory (WPSI) — somatic symptom load
- Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) — depression
- PTSD Checklist Civilian version (PCL-C) — PTSD screening
- Sleep Assessment Questionnaire (SAQc), 17-item — sleep disturbances
- Pre-sleep and post-sleep self-ratings: musculoskeletal pain severity (0–24), fatigue (1–7), sleepiness (1–7)

**Objective sleep physiology:** One overnight polysomnography (PSG) with EEG C3/C4, EOG, EMG, ECG, airflow (oral-nasal thermistors), respiratory impedance plethysmography, pulse oximetry; followed by Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT, ≥4 nap opportunities at 2-hour intervals).

**Sleep EEG anomalies quantified:**
- Alpha EEG sleep anomaly: standardized 1–5 rating (percentage of alpha frequency 7.5–12 Hz in NonREM sleep; 1 = <20%, 5 = >80%)
- Cyclical Alternating Pattern (CAP) rate: automated detection via validated Somnologica software

**Statistics:** Two-tailed t-tests; Bonferroni correction applied to multiple comparisons.

## Key Findings

**Post-SARS vs. healthy controls (Table 1):**
- Physical symptoms (WPSI): post-SARS 10.6 ± 5.0 vs. 0.4 ± 0.5 (p<0.0001)
- Depression (BDI): 13.3 ± 8.0 vs. 0.86 ± 1.5 (p<0.0001); mild-to-moderate range
- Sleep disturbance (SAQc): 30.9 ± 5 vs. 10.9 ± 3.4 (p<0.0001)
- Post-sleep fatigue: more in post-SARS (p<0.05); pre- and post-sleep myalgia (p<0.01)
- PTSD (PCL-C, n=21): mean score 40.75 ± 10.26; only 2 patients scored ≥50 (clinical PTSD threshold)

**Objective sleep findings (PSG):**
- Stage 2 NonREM sleep elevated: 60.22% vs. 49.77% in controls (p=0.006)
- REM onset latency delayed: 136.79 min vs. 87.23 min in controls (p=0.004)
- Alpha EEG sleep anomaly present in ~50% of NonREM sleep (elevated arousal marker)
- REM Apnea-Hypopnea Index (REM AHI): 14.33/hr — elevated REM-related respiratory events in subset with snoring
- 5/22 subjects had variable daytime sleepiness on MSLT (sleep onset <8 min on ≥1 nap); 2 had moderate/mild sleep apnea/hypopnea (RDI 18.8 and 8.4) with oxygen desaturation minimums of 81.2% and 83%

**Post-SARS vs. FMS (Table 2):**
- CAP rate (sleep instability): 71.64 ± 14.25 vs. 70.39 ± 15.64 — no significant difference (both elevated above published norms)
- Alpha EEG sleep anomaly: 3.00 ± 0.63 (post-SARS) vs. 3.50 ± 0.61 (FMS), p=0.014 — FMS significantly higher
- Pre-sleep pain: 6.24 ± 4.01 (post-SARS) vs. 10.95 ± 5.74 (FMS), p=0.005 — post-SARS significantly less pain
- Post-sleep pain: 7.10 ± 3.81 vs. 11.75 ± 6.45, p=0.009 — same direction
- Pre-sleep sleepiness: 2.76 ± 1.14 vs. 4.30 ± 1.08, p=0.0001 — post-SARS less sleepy
- Fatigue (pre/post-sleep) and sleep efficiency: no significant difference — profiles similar

**Broader context from discussion:** In a parallel Toronto cohort of 107 post-SARS survivors at 1 year (Herridge et al.), 87% remained symptomatic; 18 (17%) had not returned to work; 82% of those returning to unmodified work were nonetheless working with ongoing symptoms.

**Mechanistic hypothesis raised:** SARS-CoV neuroinvasion may contribute — viral particles and genome sequences were isolated from neurons in hypothalamus and cortex in autopsy studies; in ACE2-transgenic mice, SARS-CoV enters via olfactory bulb and spreads rapidly via neurons throughout the brain, potentially causing chronic post-inflammatory CNS pathology affecting sleep regulation, pain sensitivity, and energy metabolism.

## Relevance

This paper is directly relevant to the project's research question (research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes) as an early empirical demonstration that SARS-CoV-1 — a coronavirus — produces a well-defined chronic post-infectious syndrome with the precise hallmarks now constituting the PAIS frame: fatigue, pain, cognitive disturbance, depression, and sleep dysregulation persisting >1 year after acute illness resolution.

Within the PAIS frame of **failed homeostatic recovery**, this study is notable because:

1. **Recovery failure is documented objectively, not just subjectively:** PSG-confirmed sleep architecture disruption (elevated CAP rate, alpha EEG anomaly, delayed REM onset) provides neurophysiological evidence that the CNS arousal/sleep homeostasis system has not returned to normal 13–36 months post-acute illness.

2. **The syndrome is phenotypically convergent with ME/CFS and FMS:** The striking overlap in fatigue, sleep instability (matched CAP rates), and somatic symptoms between post-SARS and FMS/CFS patients — two canonical functional somatic syndromes often thought to be triggered by infection — supports the hypothesis that these conditions represent the same homeostatic failure mode triggered by different acute insults.

3. **Pre-COVID precedent:** Published in 2011, this study is a critical historical anchor demonstrating that coronavirus-triggered chronic post-infectious syndromes were documented before COVID-19. It strengthens the biological plausibility of long COVID as PAIS and helps distinguish COVID-19-specific from pan-coronavirus phenomena.

4. **CNS neuroinvasion as mechanism:** The authors' hypothesis that coronaviral CNS invasion causes chronic post-inflammatory CNS pathology affecting sleep, pain, and energy maps directly onto the project's mechanistic interest in how acute infection disrupts CNS homeostatic systems (HPA axis, autonomic regulation, sleep/wake circuitry) with lasting effect.

## Limitations

1. **Small sample size:** n=22 post-SARS subjects limits statistical power; many comparisons are exploratory. The healthy control group was particularly small (n=7–8) and younger (mean age 30 vs. 46), introducing potential age confounding.

2. **Selection bias:** The 22 subjects were drawn from a WSIB-referred cohort of 50 chronically ill SARS survivors — a severity-biased sample of occupationally impaired healthcare workers. The broader post-SARS population at 1 year likely has less severe disability (only 17% of 107 in the parallel cohort had not returned to work).

3. **All but one were healthcare workers:** This occupational homogeneity limits generalizability and may introduce exposure-pattern or psychological stress confounds (SARS exposure on the job, threat of infecting patients, stigma, etc.).

4. **Female predominance (19/22):** The FMS comparison group was all female; the healthy control group was all female; only 3 post-SARS subjects were male. This limits sex-specific interpretation.

5. **Single-night PSG:** One overnight polysomnography is subject to the "first-night effect" (laboratory-induced sleep disruption), which may have inflated stage 2 and REM latency values. The authors acknowledge this but note that comparisons were made under similar conditions.

6. **Cross-sectional design:** No longitudinal follow-up; the study cannot determine whether the sleep anomalies preceded SARS, emerged during acute illness, or developed during the chronic phase.

7. **Retrospective design:** The WSIB cohort was assembled on clinical grounds; no pre-SARS baseline data exist for most subjects.

8. **Pre-existing comorbidities:** 3 subjects with hypertension (a common risk factor for sleep apnea) may have had subclinical sleep-disordered breathing before SARS, potentially inflating the respiratory indices.

9. **FMS comparison group from separate clinical trial:** Not recruited concurrently; from a prior drug trial cohort. While only drug-free baseline data were used, the recruitment context differs.

## Model / Tool Availability

No computational models, tools, or datasets are released. The Sleep Assessment Questionnaire (SAQc) and polysomnographic scoring methods cited (Rechtschaffen & Kales manual; CAP via Somnologica software) are established standardized instruments.

## Follow-up

- **Hickie et al. 2006 (BMJ, Dubbo Cohort):** Post-infective fatigue precipitated by viral and non-viral pathogens — prospective cohort providing the best cross-pathogen comparison.
- **Moldofsky's prior fibromyalgia sleep work:** The alpha EEG sleep anomaly was first described by Moldofsky in fibromyalgia in the 1970s-80s; understanding that lineage helps frame this paper's conceptual contribution.
