# What Makes It Tick: Exploring the Mechanisms of Post-treatment Lyme Disease Syndrome

- **Authors:** Kate E. Wester, Bianca C. Nwokeabia, Rehana Hassan, Taylor Dunphy, Michael Osondu, Carson Wonders, Misbahuddin Khaja
- **Year:** 2024
- **Journal:** Cureus, vol. 16, no. 7, e64987
- **DOI:** 10.7759/cureus.64987
- **BibTeX key:** Wester2024
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This narrative review synthesizes proposed mechanistic pathways underlying post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS), defined by IDSA criteria as fatigue, pain, and/or cognitive complaints with functional impact persisting >6 months after completing antibiotic treatment for Borrelia burgdorferi infection. The authors catalogue four non-exclusive mechanistic hypotheses — tissue damage and persistent inflammation, immune system dysfunction, autoimmune response, and persistent spirochete infection — and additionally cover co-infections as a fifth contributor. The review concludes that PTLDS likely represents an autoimmune response driven by tissue damage and inflammation from viable or nonviable spirochete antigen, and frames it explicitly alongside ME/CFS and long COVID as conditions sharing a common post-infectious pathophysiological signature.

## Methods

Narrative literature review (Cureus open-access; published July 2024). No systematic search protocol or PRISMA reporting is described. The review draws on in vitro studies, mouse models, small human cohort studies, and case reports. It covers epidemiology of Lyme disease and PTLDS, clinical staging, diagnostic criteria (IDSA definition, SF-36 composite T-score <45), and then reviews published mechanistic evidence for each proposed pathway. No meta-analytic re-analysis was performed.

## Key Findings

**Epidemiology and case definition**
- PTLDS affects 10–20% of Lyme disease patients appropriately treated with antibiotics.
- The IDSA-based case definition requires: fatigue, pain, and/or cognitive complaints; functional impact (SF-36 composite T-score <45); symptoms persisting >6 months post-treatment completion.
- Risk factors include delayed diagnosis, incomplete or short-course treatment, increased severity of acute LD, disseminated neurocognitive symptoms at presentation (Bell's palsy, optic Lyme, photophobia, headache), and pre-existing comorbidities (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic pain).
- Approximately 476,000 cases of Lyme disease are diagnosed and treated annually in the US (underreporting is common); ~252,681 confirmed cases were reported 2010–2019.
- RST1 strains of B. burgdorferi generate an increased inflammatory response and are associated with greater symptom severity and higher risk of antibiotic-refractory arthritis.

**Tissue damage and neuroinflammation**
- Tissue damage in PTLDS encompasses nervous tissue, synovial tissue, muscle, and vascular tissue, primarily from the host inflammatory response rather than direct bacterial toxicity.
- Neuroinflammation occurs in both central and peripheral nervous systems. B. burgdorferi infects cranial nerves (notably the vagus nerve), leading to increased cytokines, neuroinflammation, and microglia activation — demonstrated in CNS imaging studies.
- miRNA expression changes in response to B. burgdorferi infection upregulate cell-adhesion pathways, reducing tight junction integrity and compromising the blood–brain barrier.
- In Lyme arthritis, consistent or elevated proinflammatory cytokines persist in joint and synovial fluid despite negative PCR tests for bacterial DNA — suggesting immune-mediated perpetuation independent of viable spirochetes.
- B. burgdorferi DNA can persist in cartilage and bind toll-like receptors, contributing to chronic inflammation even after spirochete clearance.

**Immune system dysfunction**
- A prospective cohort (Aucott et al.; n = 76 Lyme disease patients vs. 26 healthy controls, 12-month follow-up) found that CCL19 levels >111.67 pg/mL one month after treatment predicted a 12-fold higher risk of developing PTLDS within 6–12 months (95% CI not specified in review).
- Post-treatment transcriptome analysis (n not given in review): ~1,000 genes were altered at diagnosis vs. non-infected; after antibiotic treatment, ~700 genes remained altered, with ~50% upregulated — representing a sustained, incompletely resolved transcriptomic shift.
- B. burgdorferi outer surface proteins (OspC, OspA) suppress complement via classical, alternative, and lectin pathways, impairing phagocytosis and formation of antibody-targeted membrane attack complexes.
- Muted plasmablast response during acute infection (a B-cell index) is associated with PTLDS, suggesting inadequate humoral clearance as a predisposing factor.
- Vagus nerve damage from B. burgdorferi may impair the anti-inflammatory cholinergic reflex, contributing to chronic neuroinflammation and dysautonomia.

**Autoimmune response**
- Arvikar et al. identified 30 patients (median age 55 years) who developed systemic autoimmune joint diseases (RA, PsA, SpA) within 4 months of LD diagnosis. Of 27 tested for LD-associated autoantibodies (anti-ECGF, ApoB-100, MMP-10), 10 tested positive for at least one. Anti-ECGF antibody positivity correlated with obliterative microvascular lesions in synovial tissue. Autoantibody positivity was significantly greater in patients with LD history than in healthy controls or RA/PsA/SpA patients without LD history.
- Chandra et al.: Anti-neural antibody levels were elevated in 49% of PTLDS patients vs. 15% of healthy controls and 18.5% of previously infected but recovered individuals — supporting a graded autoimmune response related to Borrelia antigen exposure.
- Two proposed mechanisms for neural autoantibody generation: (1) release of neural autoantigens from tissue damage during active infection, creating post-translational neo-epitopes; (2) polyclonal B-cell activation by potently mitogenic Borrelia antigens (OspA, OspB), increasing antibody-secreting cell output.
- A single case of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) developing post-Lyme treatment is reported (Yehudina and Trypilka, Cureus 2021) — anecdotal level of evidence.

**Persistent infection**
- In vitro: B. burgdorferi forms persister organisms under antibiotic pressure or stationary-phase growth; these retain potential infectivity but replicate at very low copy numbers (confirmed by quantitative PCR — very low spirochetal DNA counts in treated animals, contrasting with the "spirochetal burst" seen after tick feeding).
- Mouse models (Bockenstedt et al.; Hodzic et al.): Spirochetes remained detectable by tissue histopathology and PCR for up to 3 months after ceftriaxone or doxycycline treatment. Surviving spirochetes lacked infectivity genes on plasmids 1p25 and 1p28-1 — attenuated but non-cultivable forms persist. Ticks could acquire and transmit very low-level infectious spirochetes from treated mice.
- Hodzic et al.: Rare spirochete-like forms detected by immunohistochemistry in connective tissue of heart and tibiotarsal joints of antibiotic-treated mice, even when organisms could not be directly cultured. Collagen-rich tissues (ligaments, tendons, tibiotarsal region) identified as preferential survival niches, possibly facilitating immune evasion.
- Limited human evidence: One case study documented B. burgdorferi in flexor retinaculum tendon tissue; a second study (4 subjects with treatment-resistant Lyme arthritis) found positive synovial membrane PCR even when synovial fluid PCR was negative — indicating synovial tissue as a persistent reservoir beyond fluid compartment.
- B. burgdorferi spirochetemia lipoproteins induce prolonged proinflammatory responses even from low-replicating organisms, potentially generating the constitutional PTLDS symptom constellation.

**Co-infections**
- Tick-borne co-infections (Babesia divergens, Anaplasma phagocytophilum, Bartonella, Chlamydophila pneumoniae, Yersinia enterocolitica, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia trachomatis) exacerbate disease expression and impede therapeutic success.
- Trouillas and Franck: 10-patient case series with severe neurological limb paralysis and mixed Borrelia/Babesia/Anaplasma/Bartonella infections — complete motor recovery in 7/10 with long-term combination antibiotics and anti-parasitics.
- Berghoff: Bartonella henselae showed the highest seropositivity among co-infections at 78%; Chlamydia trachomatis showed the highest lymphocyte transformation test (LTT) positivity at 100% (though seropositivity was only 5%), highlighting discordance between serological and functional immune assays.

**Comparators to other PAIS conditions**
- ME/CFS shares PTLDS hallmark symptoms (debilitating fatigue, cognitive difficulties, sleep disturbances) but typically lacks a clear infectious trigger and includes post-exertional malaise.
- Post-viral fatigue syndrome (PVFS) mirrors PTLDS in persistent fatigue and cognitive impairment but typically resolves within 6 months, unlike PTLDS.
- Long COVID shares pathophysiological features with PTLDS.
- Chronic active EBV (CAEBV) can be distinguished by persistent viral reactivation detectable through EBV-DNA or specific antibodies — a potential model for antigen-driven chronic immune activation in PTLDS.

## Relevance

This review is directly relevant to the project research question (research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes) on two levels:

1. **PTLDS as a canonical PAIS instance:** PTLDS is an archetypal post-infectious syndrome with a well-defined infectious trigger (B. burgdorferi), a recognized case definition, and 10–20% incidence after treated Lyme disease. It provides a mechanistically tractable model for studying failed homeostatic recovery after acute infection.

2. **PAIS frame of failed homeostatic recovery:** The review explicitly documents how the normal immune recovery trajectory is disrupted in PTLDS — with transcriptomic evidence (~700 genes still altered 6 months post-treatment, ~50% upregulated), persistent immune activation (CCL19 elevation, anti-neural antibodies in 49% of cases), and ongoing neuroinflammation after bacterial clearance. These are precisely the signatures of failed immune homeostasis that the PAIS frame predicts. The CCL19 → 12-fold PTLDS risk finding is a quantitative biomarker linking acute immune response quality to chronic outcome. The parallel symptom signatures shared with ME/CFS, long COVID, and PVFS reinforce the shared-failure-mode hypothesis that anchors this project.

## Limitations

- **Narrative review without systematic search protocol:** No PRISMA reporting, no explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria, no risk-of-bias assessment. Susceptible to selection bias toward studies supporting proposed mechanisms.
- **Heavy reliance on mouse models:** Persistent infection evidence is predominantly from mouse studies (Bockenstedt, Hodzic). Human data are limited to a few small case studies (single-subject tendon study, 4-subject treatment-resistant arthritis cohort). Mouse–human translation is not established for PTLDS specifically.
- **Small cohort sizes throughout:** The Aucott CCL19 cohort (76 patients, 26 controls) and the Arvikar autoimmune arthritis cohort (30 patients) are too small for definitive conclusions. No power calculations or confidence intervals are consistently reported.
- **No quantitative diagnostic biomarker exists:** As of 2024, PTLDS remains a clinical diagnosis; the review identifies this gap but does not propose validated objective measures.
- **Mechanistic hypotheses remain non-exclusive and unranked:** The review does not attempt to weight or hierarchy the four/five proposed pathways — leaving the dominant driver(s) unresolved.
- **Case definition heterogeneity:** Studies cited use varying definitions of PTLDS and "Lyme disease" (serology-confirmed vs. clinical-only), limiting cross-study comparability.
- **Co-infection section is descriptive:** Presented as a contributing factor but without mechanistic evidence integrated with the other four pathways.
- **Contested causal claims:** Autoimmune SLE post-Lyme is supported only by a single case report. The persistent infection evidence in humans is similarly limited to anecdotal histopathological reports.

## Model / Tool Availability

No computational models, datasets, or tools are released. This is a literature review.

## Follow-up

- Aucott et al. (2016) CCL19 prospective cohort study (Clin Vaccine Immunol, 23:757–66; cited as ref. 17) — primary data source for the 12-fold PTLDS risk finding; warrants direct reading.
- Chandra et al. (2010) anti-neural antibody study (Brain Behav Immun, 24:1018–24; cited as ref. 21) — primary source for 49% anti-neural antibody prevalence in PTLDS.
- Hodzic et al. (2008) persistent spirochete mouse study (Antimicrob Agents Chemother, 52:1728–36; cited as ref. 23) — primary source for non-cultivable persister evidence.
- Choutka et al. (2022; already summarized as paper:Choutka2022) provides the broader PAIS framework within which PTLDS sits.
- Ford and Tufts (2021) Lyme neuroborreliosis review (Brain Sci, 11:789) — mechanistic detail on B. burgdorferi CNS infection.
- Consider systematic review of CCL19/CCL21 as PAIS biomarkers across Lyme, long COVID, and ME/CFS to test cross-condition generalizability.
