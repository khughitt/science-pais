# Autoimmune Features of Post-COVID-19 Vaccination Syndrome and Their Impacts on the Renin–Angiotensin System

- **Authors:** Paolo Bellavite, Giuseppe Di Fede, Mauro Mantovani, Elisabetta Zanolin
- **Year:** 2026
- **Journal:** Vaccines, 14, 354
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/vaccines14040354
- **BibTeX key:** Bellavite2026
- **Source:** Full-text PDF (papers/pdfs/2026_Bellavite_autoimmune-features-post-covid-vaccination-syndrome-ras.pdf), read 2026-06-20.

> **Scope note:** This paper addresses post-acute COVID-19 *vaccination* syndrome (PACVS/PACVS), not post-acute infection syndrome (PAIS) arising from natural SARS-CoV-2 infection. It is read here as a mechanistic comparator because it proposes the same anti-ACE2 / anti-MAS1 / GPCR-autoantibody pathway invoked in long COVID PAIS, and because the review explicitly discusses the partial overlap between the two syndromes. Whether PACVS falls within scope for this project should be adjudicated against `specs/scope-boundaries.md` before committing this paper to primary synthesis.

## Key Contribution

This review (with embedded observational data from a case series of 17 PACVS patients) proposes that persistent post-vaccination symptoms — fatigue, dysautonomia, neurovascular manifestations, and skin changes — are driven at least in part by functional autoantibodies targeting renin–angiotensin system (RAS) proteins, particularly ACE2 and the MAS1 receptor. The authors hypothesize that COVID-19 spike-protein–based vaccines generate anti-idiotypic antibodies (Ab2) whose paratopes structurally mimic the S protein and thereby cross-react with ACE2, and that epitope spreading within the idiotypic network additionally generates anti-MAS1 antibodies. This RAS disruption — impairing the ACE2/Ang-(1–7)/MAS1 protective axis and potentiating the pro-inflammatory ACE/Ang-II/AT1R axis — is posited as a unifying pathophysiological mechanism. The paper is also a narrative review of autoimmunity, diagnostics, and proposed therapeutics in PACVS.

**Evidence maturity:** This is a review combined with observational/hypothesis-generating data; no controlled trial or prospective cohort is presented. The primary empirical basis is a retrospective case series of 17 patients from a single clinic. All causal claims are mechanistic hypotheses or preliminary associations, not established causation.

## Methods

The paper has two components:

1. **Retrospective case series (n = 17):** Patients (13 women, 4 men; mean age 44) were drawn consecutively from the authors' clinic during 2023, selected for symptoms compatible with PACVS and no prior COVID-19 infection. They had received 1–3 doses of adenoviral (n = 2) or mRNA (n = 15) vaccines, with a median observation period of 20 months post-vaccination. Serum was tested for a panel of 16 autoantibodies against RAS-related proteins (ACE2, MAS1) and other GPCRs (adrenergic, muscarinic, chemokine, endothelin, stabilin-1, AT1R) using commercial ELISA kits (CellTrend GmbH–ImBio, Germany). Conventional autoantibody panels (ANA, ANCA, ENA, anti-cardiac muscle) were also run. Symptom frequencies and antibody–symptom associations were assessed; Fisher's exact test and Mann–Whitney tests were used for group comparisons.

2. **Narrative review:** The authors synthesize the broader literature on PACVS immunopathology, RAS biology, molecular mimicry, the idiotypic network, clinical diagnostics, and proposed therapeutic approaches.

**Key design limitations acknowledged by the authors:** Single clinic, small n, no healthy control group for antibody comparisons, retrospective, cross-sectional, observational; "a statistical association does not mean that antibodies are the cause of the syndrome."

## Key Findings

**From the case series (preliminary, n = 17):**

- All patients had multiple symptoms (mean 9.1 ± 3.5); most prevalent: asthenia/chronic fatigue, memory/concentration loss/brain fog, neuralgia/paresthesia, resting or orthostatic tachycardia, musculoskeletal pain, and widespread burning sensation.
- 16/17 patients had below-normal T-regulatory cell (Treg) levels, consistent with autoimmune mechanisms.
- 10/15 patients (66.6%) had plasma Ang-(1–7) above the normal limit (226 ng/mL) — interpreted as possible compensatory upregulation secondary to MAS1 blockade.
- Anti-MAS1 antibodies were elevated in nearly all patients; MAS1 levels were significantly higher in patients with widespread burning sensations (median 51.6 U/mL) versus without (30.9 U/mL; Mann–Whitney p < 0.009).
- Anti-ACE2 antibodies were significantly associated with skin symptoms (bruising, edema, rashes): ACE2-positive patients had higher median anti-ACE2 (26.3 U/mL vs 8.4 U/mL in ACE2-negative; Mann–Whitney p = 0.015); Fisher's exact test p = 0.009 for skin symptom prevalence.
- Conventional autoantibodies (ANA, ANCA, ENA, anti-cardiac muscle) were negative in all cases.
- Anti-SARS-CoV-2 S-RBD IgG, anti-Nucleocapsid IgG and IgM were found in some patients, suggesting ongoing immune stimulation.

**From the review (mechanistic hypotheses, not primary data):**

- The authors propose that the vaccine-elicited anti-S immune response generates anti-idiotypic antibodies (Ab2) with structural similarity to the S protein that cross-react with ACE2 (the S protein's receptor). This is supported by experimental mouse data (Collins et al. 2025: sequential mRNA vaccination of hACE2 transgenic mice induced progressively amplified anti-ACE2 antibodies with each boost). [SPECULATION — mechanistic inference beyond the authors' own patient data]
- Anti-ACE2 antibodies can either block ACE2's carboxypeptidase activity (leading to Ang-II accumulation, AT1R activation, pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic effects) or act as functional agonists mimicking S protein signaling (activating endothelial and platelet Ang-II pathways).
- Anti-MAS1 antibodies are proposed to arise via epitope spreading from anti-ACE2 responses, exploiting structural similarities between the ACE2 binding site for Ang-II and the MAS1 receptor binding site for Ang-(1–7). Blocking MAS1 reduces the vasoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects of the ACE2/Ang-(1–7)/MAS1 axis.
- The lipid nanoparticle adjuvant system, batch heterogeneity, and frameshifted recombinant spike proteins are discussed as additional potential pathogenic contributors.
- Molecular mimicry between the S protein and human proteins (myelin, cardiac proteins, CREB1, adiponectin receptor, thrombopoietin) is reviewed as a complementary mechanism.
- PACVS symptoms overlap substantially with PACS (post-acute COVID-19 syndrome); the two are distinguished primarily by clinical history, and the authors note that distinguishing them is inherently difficult.

**Proposed diagnostics:** A staged panel is described including lymphocyte populations, anti-S/anti-N antibodies, inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α), anti-GPCR antibody panel (AT1R, β-adrenergic, muscarinic, ACE2, MAS1), plasma Ang-(1–7), viral reactivation serology, and gut microbiome assessment. These are proposed for exploratory use in difficult-to-classify cases, not routine screening.

**Proposed therapeutics (no RCT evidence; all observational/theoretical):** Glutathione, hesperidin + quercetin, Ang-(1–7) galenic formulation, nattokinase, bromelain, vitamin D, antihistamines; therapeutic plasmapheresis for refractory cases; IVIG for SFN; methylene blue (MB) as an anti-oxidant and spike-ACE2 interaction blocker.

## Relevance

**Direct relevance to question:0009 (functional autoantibodies drive dysautonomia):** The paper is a detailed mechanistic elaboration of the GPCR-autoantibody hypothesis as applied to PACVS, specifically focusing on the RAS subset of this hypothesis (anti-ACE2, anti-MAS1, anti-AT1R). It provides observational data — albeit small n, uncontrolled — showing that anti-ACE2 levels associate with skin/vascular symptoms and anti-MAS1 levels associate with burning/sensory symptoms. This is a weaker evidence tier than the Wallukat/Scheibenbogen deSa2026 and Stein2025 work: those studies used bioassays confirming functional receptor activity; this paper reports ELISA titers from commercial kits without functional confirmation.

**Relevance to topic:post-infectious-dysautonomia-and-autoimmunity:** The autonomic manifestations (orthostatic tachycardia, hypertension, syncope) are prominent in the symptom catalogue and the proposed mechanism — GPCR autoantibody disruption of autonomic and cardiovascular RAS — is precisely the overlapping mechanism this topic addresses. The paper extends this thread to the vaccine-triggered setting.

**Relevance to topic:thromboinflammation-and-endothelial-dysfunction:** Anti-ACE2 antibodies are proposed to promote Ang-II accumulation → AT1R activation → vasoconstriction, platelet aggregation, coagulation activation, and endothelial dysfunction. Anti-MAS1 blockade removes the counter-regulatory vasoprotective arm of the RAS. The paper's thrombosis-and-embolism symptom frequency (~30% in the case series) and one prior case of plasmapheresis-responsive thrombocytopenia-plus-dysautonomia (Mantovani 2024) situate this in the thromboinflammation context.

**Relevance to hypothesis:0001-shared-dysregulated-attractor:** The paper explicitly argues that PACS and PACVS share pathophysiological mechanisms because both involve the same antigen (S protein): in one case as virus, in the other as vaccine immunogen. This is consistent with the attractor framing of PAIS as a shared failure mode reachable via multiple triggers. However, the paper does not itself test this across conditions.

**Comparator papers:** This paper occupies a lower evidence tier than:
- **deSa2026** and **Stein2025** — which report functional GPCR-autoantibody assays in PACS/ME-CFS cohorts with appropriate controls. Bellavite2026 reports ELISA titers only, in 17 uncontrolled PACVS patients.
- **Wallukat/Scheibenbogen group** studies (cited here) — which initiated the functional anti-GPCR framing in long COVID with bioassay-confirmed functional effects.

## Limitations

**Study-level (acknowledged by authors):**
- n = 17 from a single clinic; no healthy control group or disease comparator group; no power calculation; retrospective and cross-sectional.
- Consecutive inclusion during 2023 partially mitigates referral bias but all patients were already presenting with suspected PACVS, introducing ascertainment bias.
- No functional autoantibody confirmation (only ELISA titers); the commercial CellTrend panel has known variable sensitivity/specificity in this context.
- Causal inference is impossible: antibody levels may be epiphenomenal rather than pathogenic.
- The PACVS case definition used is the authors' own operational criteria (symptoms compatible with PACVS, no prior COVID-19), not a validated or consensus definition.
- Therapeutic suggestions are not evidence-based (no RCT; based on pharmacological rationale and single-clinic observational experience).

**Scope and credibility considerations (project-added):**
- The lead author (Bellavite) is independently affiliated ("Independent Researcher, Verona") and has prior publications expressing vaccine-critical perspectives; this does not automatically invalidate the findings but is context relevant to evidence weighting.
- Conflicts of interest: Bellavite and Di Fede have a consultancy relationship with Vanda Omeopatici s.r.l. (natural medicines/food supplements), disclosed in the paper; this company had no role in study design or analysis.
- This is a contested literature: PACVS itself is not a recognized diagnostic entity by major public health agencies; the anti-RAS autoantibody framing extends a hypothesis under active investigation in PACS to a less well-documented syndrome.
- Several cited supporting studies come from overlapping author groups or are preprints; independent replication of the anti-ACE2/anti-MAS1 associations is sparse.
- Mechanistic claims about idiotypic networks and molecular mimicry are theoretical and based on analogical reasoning rather than direct experimental evidence in these patients.
- The paper does not distinguish mRNA vs adenoviral vaccine responses despite different immunological profiles; n is far too small for subgroup analysis.

## Follow-up

**Scope adjudication needed:** The project should explicitly decide whether PACVS papers belong in this repository (as boundary/comparator cases), in a separate vaccination-adverse-effects project, or are shared via the commons mechanism. This paper is a clear example of the boundary question. Recommend: update `specs/scope-boundaries.md` with a PACVS adjudication note.

**Evidence gap to fill:** The key claim — that anti-ACE2 and anti-MAS1 ELISA positivity associates with specific PACVS symptoms — requires independent replication in a larger, controlled cohort with functional autoantibody validation. Prioritize watching for such studies (e.g., from the Schulze-Forster/Scheibenbogen group, who developed the CellTrend ELISA platform).

**Mechanistic thread:** The anti-idiotype/ACE2 hypothesis is gaining some experimental support (Collins et al. 2025, hACE2 transgenic mouse vaccination model). If that finding replicates in human PACS cohorts, it would elevate the evidence tier considerably and directly inform question:0009.

**Therapeutic signal:** Therapeutic plasmapheresis to clear pathogenic autoantibodies (Mantovani 2024, cited case) is worth tracking — it is also being explored in the Scheibenbogen group context for long COVID dysautonomia.

**Related papers to read next:**
- Mantovani et al. 2024 (*Biomedicines* 12, 2852) — the primary case series publication from this group; more detail on the 17-patient antibody panel.
- Collins et al. 2025 (*Vaccines* 13, 224) — mouse experimental evidence for anti-idiotype anti-ACE2 response post-vaccination.
- Wallukat et al. 2021 (*J. Transl. Autoimmun.* 4, 100100) — functional GPCR autoantibodies in long COVID (cite:deSa2026 companion context).
- Purpura et al. 2026 (*Clin. Infect. Dis.*, ahead of print) — overlapping clinical presentations of long COVID and PACVS with biomarker comparisons.
