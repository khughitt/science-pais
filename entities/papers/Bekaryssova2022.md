# Reactive arthritis before and after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic

- **Authors:** Dana Bekaryssova, Marlen Yessirkepov, Olena Zimba, Armen Yuri Gasparyan, Sakir Ahmed
- **Year:** 2022
- **Journal:** Clinical Rheumatology, vol. 41, no. 6, pp. 1641–1652
- **DOI:** 10.1007/s10067-022-06120-3
- **BibTeX key:** Bekaryssova2022
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This narrative review synthesizes the clinical phenotype, definition controversies, proposed mechanisms, and outcomes of reactive arthritis (ReA), with a focused comparative analysis of how post-COVID-19 ReA and post-vaccination inflammatory arthritis differ from classical (bacterial-triggered, spondyloarthritis-like) ReA. The authors argue that post-COVID-19 ReA is clinically distinct — more transient, occurring at older ages, with equal sex distribution, and frequently affecting small joints — and that the absence of a universal ReA definition is the primary obstacle to systematic research. The review provides the most comprehensive catalog of post-COVID-19 ReA case reports (Tables 1–2) and post-COVID-19-vaccination inflammatory arthritis cases (Table 3) published up to early 2022.

## Methods

Narrative review adhering to recommendations for narrative biomedical reviews. Literature searched in Scopus and LitCovid/PubMed. The MeSH keyword "reactive arthritis" was used, including "post infectious arthritis" for LitCovid/PubMed searches, and "reactive arthritis" OR "post infectious arthritis" for Scopus. Non-English sources, conference abstracts, and non-peer-reviewed sources were excluded. No formal meta-analysis or systematic quality assessment was performed. The review covers the history of ReA, current definitional debates (Braun, ESSG, ASAS criteria), classical vs. post-COVID-19 ReA clinical features, post-COVID-19 vaccination arthritis, broader post-COVID-19 inflammatory arthritis, pathogenic mechanisms, and the need to refine definitions.

## Key Findings

**Case definition and classification disputes**

The most commonly used definition of ReA (Braun criteria) requires monoarthritis or oligoarthritis preceded by symptomatic diarrhoea or urethritis, with a causative organism demonstrable by culture or PCR. Multiple competing frameworks exist (ESSG, ASAS peripheral SpA criteria). No ACR or EULAR practice guidelines exist for ReA, as clinicians in high-income countries rarely see severe presentations. The incidence of classical ReA is apparently declining in most high-income countries, while it remains a major disease burden in low- and middle-income settings (e.g., arthritis is the predominant feature in 95% of ReA cases from India).

**Classical ReA characteristics**

- Typically affects ages 15–40 years, predominantly male.
- Precipitated by gut (Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia) or urogenital (gonococcal) infection; bacterially triggered.
- Spondyloarthritis-like phenotype: axial involvement, lower-limb predominant oligoarthritis; extra-articular features including dactylitis, enthesitis, skin, uveitis, inflammatory bowel disease.
- HLA-B27 confers susceptibility; ~1/3 of cases become chronic (lasting >3 months).
- Only a small percentage of people who have infections with typical organisms actually develop ReA, suggesting host immune susceptibility is the critical variable.

**Post-COVID-19 ReA: clinical phenotype (Table 1, n = ~20 case reports)**

- Predominantly affects ages >45 years; equal male–female distribution (contrast with classical ReA).
- Precipitated by respiratory tract SARS-CoV-2 infection; viral rather than bacterial trigger.
- More commonly has a rheumatoid-like phenotype: wrists, ankles, small joints of hands and feet; large-joint lower-limb oligoarthritis also reported.
- Axial involvement reported in at least one case (bilateral sacroiliitis in a 53-year-old male).
- HLA-B27 was negative in most tested cases; few cases had HLA-B27 positivity.
- Autoantibodies (ANA, RF, anti-CCP): generally negative in most cases; a subgroup with anti-CCP positivity had a more chronic, RA-like course requiring methotrexate.
- Onset: typically 2–4 weeks after acute COVID-19 symptoms.
- Outcome: most resolve within 2 weeks to 3 months; managed with NSAIDs and/or low-dose steroids. Only patients with anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies had a chronic course.
- One case included a 10-year-old boy (pediatric involvement noted); initial reports skewed toward men >50 years.

**Post-COVID-19 vaccination arthritis (Table 3, n = 7 cases)**

- Seven cases identified: CoronaVac (Sinovac), Sinovac, and SPUTNIK-V vaccines.
- Temporal gap: 2 days to 3 weeks after vaccination (for 2nd dose in most cases).
- Clinical features: variable joint pattern — knee monoarthritis, polyarthritis of wrists/hands/spine (including a case with pre-existing ankylosing spondylitis), wrists/hands/knees, bilateral shoulders/hands/feet.
- HLA-B27 positive in at least one case.
- Outcomes: most resolved or markedly improved within 2 weeks to 3 months; one case had persistent pain and swelling at 1-week follow-up; one required methotrexate (pre-existing SpA case).
- The authors note this is likely an undercount and that post-vaccination autoimmune diseases are being reported more broadly (reference to 27 new-onset immune-mediated diseases post-mRNA/DNA COVID-19 vaccination).

**Classical vs. post-COVID-19 ReA comparison (Table 2)**

| Feature | Classical ReA | Post-COVID-19 ReA |
|---|---|---|
| Age | 15–40 years | >45 years (reported in all ages) |
| Sex | Male predominance | Equal male–female |
| Trigger | Gut or urogenital | Respiratory |
| Inciting agent | Bacteria | Virus |
| Phenotype | SpA-like (axial, lower limb) | Multiple phenotypes |
| Joint predilection | Large joints | Small joints |
| Chronicity | 1/3 become chronic (>3 months) | Most resolve within 2 weeks to 3 months |
| Management | As other SpA (limited evidence) | Low-dose steroids ± NSAIDs (limited evidence) |
| Extra-articular | Dactylitis, enthesitis, skin, uveitis, IBD | Unknown/limited |

**Pathogenic mechanisms**

- SARS-CoV-2 activates CD14+ monocytes and PD-L1+ neutrophils via Osteopontin-mediated inhibition of IL-10 (shared pathway with RA synoviocytes; IFN-related pathways also implicated).
- At least 15 different autoantibodies described in COVID-19; 34 human peptides share similarity with SARS-CoV-2 proteins — molecular mimicry is a plausible mechanism.
- One study detected SARS-CoV-2 RNA in synovial fluid from a patient with wrist arthritis 15 days after GI/upper respiratory symptoms; however, post-mortem studies in 5 COVID-19 patients found no viral RNA in synovial fluid or bone tissue. Whether post-COVID-19 arthritis is truly "reactive" (immune-mediated, no direct invasion) or represents late-onset viral arthritis (direct invasion) remains unresolved.
- Chronic post-COVID-19 ReA may evolve into classifiable rheumatic diseases such as RA or lupus in some patients.

## Relevance

This review is directly relevant to the project research question (research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes) at three levels:

1. **ReA as a canonical PAIS model.** Reactive arthritis is the prototypical post-infectious syndrome where the immune system fails to return to homeostasis after the triggering infection resolves. The articular inflammation persists weeks to months after pathogen clearance, representing a failure of post-infectious immune resolution. The observation that only a small fraction of people who contract typical causative organisms (Campylobacter, Salmonella, SARS-CoV-2) develop ReA directly supports the project's framing that host susceptibility — not pathogen alone — determines whether homeostatic recovery succeeds.

2. **Post-COVID-19 ReA as a PAIS exemplar.** Post-COVID-19 ReA is a form of post-acute-infection musculoskeletal dysfunction where SARS-CoV-2 triggers a dysregulated immune response that outlasts acute infection. It fits the project's definition of failed homeostatic recovery: immune activation (autoantibodies, T-cell dysregulation, interferon-pathway upregulation) persisting after pathogen clearance, with most cases self-limiting (successful if slow recovery) but a subgroup progressing to chronic articular disease (persistent homeostatic failure). The anti-CCP-positive subgroup that requires methotrexate represents a shift from transient dysregulation to a stable autoimmune attractor state — a key transition the project seeks to understand.

3. **Mechanistic hypotheses linking to PAIS mechanisms.** The three candidate mechanisms discussed — molecular mimicry (34 human peptides homologous to SARS-CoV-2), antigen/pathogen persistence in synovium, and IFN/autoantibody dysregulation — map directly onto the project's cross-syndrome mechanistic candidates (autoimmunity, antigen persistence, immune dysregulation). The contested finding of SARS-CoV-2 RNA in synovial fluid is directly relevant to the antigen-persistence hypothesis.

## Limitations

- Narrative review without systematic quality assessment, PRISMA compliance, or meta-analysis; susceptible to selection bias in case inclusion.
- Post-COVID-19 ReA evidence base consists entirely of case reports and small case series (no controlled prospective cohorts); effect sizes, prevalence, and incidence cannot be estimated.
- Only 7 post-vaccination inflammatory arthritis cases identified — far too few to characterize the phenomenon or estimate risk.
- Non-English sources excluded, which likely undercounts post-COVID-19 ReA from non-Anglophone low/middle-income countries where ReA burden is highest.
- The central question of whether post-COVID-19 arthritis is truly "reactive" (immune-mediated without direct viral invasion) or viral (direct synovial invasion) remains empirically unresolved; the authors acknowledge conflicting synovial fluid data.
- HLA-B27 testing was absent or not reported in the majority of post-COVID-19 ReA cases summarized in Table 1.
- No long-term follow-up data (>12 months) for post-COVID-19 ReA cases; chronicity rates cannot be compared to classical ReA.
- The review predates validation of post-COVID-19 ReA case definitions and does not propose standardized outcome measures.

## Follow-up

- The Ursini2023 entry in this project covers musculoskeletal manifestations of long COVID more broadly; compare post-COVID-19 ReA to the broader musculoskeletal PAIS phenotype.
- Mechanistic studies directly testing SARS-CoV-2 antigen/RNA persistence in synovial tissue would resolve whether post-COVID-19 arthritis is reactive or directly viral.
- Prospective cohort studies tracking ReA incidence after SARS-CoV-2 infection (using validated case definitions and systematic HLA-B27 genotyping) would establish true prevalence and risk factors.
- The anti-CCP-positive chronic post-COVID-19 arthritis subgroup warrants study as a model for how infection tips susceptible individuals into stable autoimmune states — directly relevant to the PAIS transition-to-chronicity question.
- Definition consensus: the call for a global consensus ReA definition mirrors the need for stable PAIS case definitions across long COVID, ME/CFS, and PTLDS — a cross-cutting problem for the project.
