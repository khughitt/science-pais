# Sex and menopause-based differences in presentation of early Lyme disease

- **Authors:** Alison W. Rebman, Ting Yang, John N. Aucott
- **Year:** 2026
- **Journal:** Clinical and Experimental Medicine
- **DOI:** 10.1007/s10238-026-02063-0
- **BibTeX key:** Rebman2026
- **Source:** PDF: `~/d/health/processes/post-acute-infection/papers/pdfs/2026_Rebman_Sex-and-menopause-based-differences-in-presentation-of-early-Lyme-disease-A-prospective-cohort-study.pdf`

## Key Contribution

Rebman et al. show that sex and menopausal status shape the acute presentation and serologic response of early Lyme disease.
The paper is valuable for PAIS because it separates acute infection severity and test positivity from later post-treatment symptom risk, where females may be overrepresented.

## Methods

The authors analyzed 243 antibiotic-naive adults from the Mid-Atlantic US enrolled in a prospective SLICE cohort with physician-documented erythema migrans rash.
They compared symptom, physical exam, laboratory, and two-tier serology findings by sex and menopausal status at baseline and approximately three weeks later.
Lyme severity was operationalized with six indicators: rash size, number of acute symptoms, disseminated erythema migrans, positive serology, liver-function elevation, and elevated neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio.

## Key Findings

Males had higher adjusted odds of two-tier seropositivity than females (OR 1.77; 95% CI 1.03-3.04).
The difference was stronger when males were compared with premenopausal females (OR 2.93; 95% CI 1.26-6.79) and was not significant compared with postmenopausal females.
Males also had higher odds of a higher severity-score category (OR 1.94; 95% CI 1.20-3.15), again especially compared with premenopausal females.
Symptom count did not differ strongly by sex, but heart palpitations and vomiting were more common among females, while sleep difficulty was more common among males.

## Relevance

This paper complicates a simple "female sex equals worse infection" model.
In early Lyme disease, males looked more objectively severe, while other literature suggests females are more represented in persistent post-treatment symptoms.
For PAIS, this supports the hypothesis that the biology of acute pathogen control and the biology of post-acute non-resolution can diverge by sex and menopausal status.

## Limitations

The cohort is geographically specific and stratified subgroup sizes are modest, especially for premenopausal females.
The paper evaluates early Lyme disease, not post-treatment Lyme disease outcomes in this analysis.
Standard two-tier testing may be replaced by modified two-tier testing, so diagnostic implications need replication.

## Model / Tool Availability

No computational model was released.
The authors state data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and pending approvals.

## Follow-up

This should inform a cross-PAIS sex-differences synthesis: acute severity, immune response, diagnostic sensitivity, and post-acute persistence may have different sex/hormone relationships.
It also motivates reading longitudinal SLICE papers on PTLD by sex and menopausal status.
