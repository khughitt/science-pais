# Vascular Complications of Long COVID—From Endothelial Dysfunction to Systemic Thrombosis: A Systematic Review

- **Authors:** Maja Stojanovic, Marko Djuric, Irina Nenadic, Suzana Bojic, Ana Andrijevic, Aleksa Popovic, Slobodan Pesic
- **Year:** 2026
- **Journal:** International Journal of Molecular Sciences, 27, 433
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms27010433
- **BibTeX key:** Stojanovic2026
- **Source:** Full-text PDF (papers/pdfs/2026_Stojanovic_vascular-complications-long-covid-endothelial-thrombosis.pdf), read 2026-06-20.

## Key Contribution

This systematic narrative review synthesizes evidence from 130 included studies (screened from 4,538 records via PRISMA) on the vascular pathophysiology of Long COVID (LC), positioning endothelial dysfunction as the central nexus linking acute SARS-CoV-2 infection to persistent microvascular and macrovascular complications. The review organizes evidence across four thematic domains — endothelial injury, coagulation/fibrinolytic dysregulation, immunothrombosis/platelet activation, and clinical thromboembolic events — and argues that LC, in a vascular-prominent subset, represents a pathophysiological continuum from acute COVID-19-associated coagulopathy to chronic thromboinflammation.

## Methods

**Review design:** Systematic narrative review (PRISMA-compliant). A formal meta-analysis was considered inappropriate given heterogeneity across study methodologies, populations, and outcome measures.

**Search:** PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science searched from inception through 30 September 2025.

**PICOS eligibility:**
- *Population:* Adults (≥18 years) with LC — defined as symptom persistence >4 weeks post-infection (also capturing WHO ≥12-week definition); included studies using the PASC label when LC was the primary condition.
- *Comparator:* Healthy controls, COVID-recovered individuals without long-term sequelae, or patients with other chronic conditions (e.g., ME/CFS).
- *Outcomes:* (1) Endothelial dysfunction markers (vascular reactivity, flow-mediated dilatation, vWF, ICAM-1, VCAM-1); (2) coagulation/fibrinolytic abnormalities (D-dimer, fibrinogen, PAI-1, hypercoagulability assays); (3) immunothrombosis markers (NETs, MPO-DNA complexes, platelet activation); (4) clinical thromboembolic events (DVT, PE, MI, stroke).
- *Study designs:* Original observational (prospective/retrospective cohorts, case-control, cross-sectional), clinical trials, and mechanistic/laboratory studies in human participants.
- *Exclusions:* Case reports, editorials, narrative opinions, non-peer-reviewed preprints, animal-only studies, non-English publications.

**Selection:** 4,538 records identified → 1,185 screened (after 3,353 duplicates removed) → 297 full texts assessed → 130 studies included (167 full texts excluded).

**Data extraction:** Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form; discrepancies resolved by a third senior reviewer.

**Quality:** No formal risk-of-bias scoring tool is reported; evidence strength within each thematic domain was evaluated qualitatively by consistency and mechanistic plausibility.

## Key Findings

### 1. Endothelial dysfunction

- SARS-CoV-2 enters endothelial cells via ACE2/TMPRSS2, triggering direct cytopathic injury, complement activation (C5b-9 complex), and cytokine-storm-mediated indirect damage.
- Persistent elevation of endothelin-1, ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and soluble thrombomodulin documented months after acute infection in LC patients, indicating sustained endothelial activation even without overt clinical thrombosis.
- vWF and Factor VIII elevated both in acute COVID-19 and in LC; reduced ADAMTS13 activity further amplifies vWF multimer-driven thrombogenicity.
- Glycocalyx degradation (driven by spike-protein-induced inflammation and oxidative stress) contributes to microvascular barrier loss and may explain fatigue and cognitive impairment.
- Reduced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability via impaired eNOS/NOS signalling and IL-6/TNF-α-mediated arginase upregulation leads to increased vascular tone and a prothrombotic milieu.
- ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 remain less well validated as clinical LC biomarkers; current evidence is derived from small, methodologically heterogeneous studies.

### 2. Coagulation and fibrinolytic dysregulation

- COVID-19-associated coagulopathy (CAC) is primarily hypercoagulable (not classic DIC): elevated D-dimer, raised fibrinogen (early phase), moderately prolonged PT, normal/slightly shortened aPTT, reduced antithrombin, elevated vWF and Factor VIII, reduced ADAMTS13.
- PAI-1 elevation drives hypofibrinolysis, promoting fibrin accumulation and microthrombus persistence in LC; PAI-1 also exerts direct pro-inflammatory and pro-senescence effects on the endothelium.
- Antiphospholipid antibodies (aCL, anti-β2GPI, lupus anticoagulant) detected in 30–50% of patients in acute COVID-19; titres are mostly low and transient, but may persist in a subset with more severe LC presentations, potentially sustaining arterial/venous thrombotic risk.
- D-dimer and circulating endothelial cells remain elevated in ~25–30% of LC individuals up to 4 months post-infection.

### 3. Immunothrombosis and platelet activation

- NETs (quantified by MPO-DNA complexes) entrap platelets and fibrin, amplifying microthrombus formation; NETs activity shown to persist beyond viral clearance in LC cohorts.
- Activated platelets release thromboxane A2, serotonin, and CD40L, promoting vasoconstriction, aggregation, and leukocyte recruitment.
- Small fibrin aggregates enriched with α2-antiplasmin and complement components ("microclots") identified in plasma of LC patients months post-infection; these structures appear resistant to fibrinolysis and may contribute to sustained symptoms.
- Persistent microparticles rich in tissue factor documented in LC, maintaining low-grade coagulation pathway activation.

### 4. Clinical thromboembolic events

**Venous thromboembolism (VTE):**
- During acute COVID-19, VTE prevalence up to ~30% in hospitalized patients (DVT ~20%, PE ~18%).
- LC-associated VTE incidence estimated at 0.5–5.4% — approximately fourfold higher than after other infectious conditions.
- Survivors >30 days post-COVID-19 show elevated HRs: DVT HR 1.98 (95% CI 1.94–2.24), PE HR 2.93 (95% CI 2.73–3.15), superficial vein thrombosis HR 1.95 (95% CI 1.80–2.12) [from tLC cohort].
- LC patients have HR 3.16 (95% CI 2.63–3.79, p<0.0001) for incident PE over median 8.5-month follow-up vs non-infected individuals.
- Hypercoagulable profiles on rotational thromboelastometry during acute infection were associated with 17–28× higher odds of developing LC in selected cohorts.

**Arterial and cardiac complications:**
- Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) including MI, stroke, and CV death remain elevated >1 year post-infection, including in previously non-hospitalized individuals.
- Ischaemic stroke: increased risk for up to 1 year post-infection in large cohort studies; multifactorial (endothelial ACE2 injury, sustained procoagulant state, new-onset atrial fibrillation).
- COVID hospitalization associated with 45% increased risk of de novo heart failure within 1 year (HR 1.45; N3C study, N=587,330).
- Myocarditis: cardiac MRI reveals late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and T1/T2 mapping abnormalities even in individuals without severe acute COVID; long-term prognostic significance under investigation.
- Arterial stiffness: central cfPWV may normalize faster than peripheral vascular stiffness, suggesting differential regional vascular recovery trajectories.

**Microvascular / peripheral manifestations:**
- Reduced capillary density per linear mm on nailfold videocapillaroscopy (NVC) in LC patients.
- Microvascular abnormalities (capillary dilatation, microhemorrhages) observed up to 18 months post-infection.
- Chilblain-like lesions ("COVID toes") and livedo reticularis interpreted as cutaneous manifestations of systemic microvascular thromboinflammation, with complement C4c deposition in affected vessels.
- Blood-brain barrier damage, reduced sublingual/retinal capillary density, and impaired flow-mediated skin fluorescence documented.
- Viral persistence proposed as a sustaining mechanism for microvascular dysfunction via chronic proinflammatory signalling and tissue hypoxia.

## Relevance

This review directly supports `topic:thromboinflammation-and-endothelial-dysfunction` and `question:0010-vascular-microclot-subphenotype` by providing the most comprehensive recent synthesis of the endothelial-dysfunction→thrombosis axis in LC. Key relevance points:

1. **Biomarker panel for a vascular LC subphenotype:** The review consolidates the strongest candidate biomarkers — vWF, D-dimer, PAI-1, NETs/MPO-DNA, microclot assays, ICAM-1/VCAM-1, antiphospholipid antibodies — that could operationalize a vascular subphenotype for `question:0010`. ICAM-1/VCAM-1 are flagged as research-grade, not yet validated clinically.

2. **Mechanistic chain supports `hypothesis:0001-shared-dysregulated-attractor`:** The vicious cycle identified here — endothelial injury → cytokine release → platelet/NET activation → thrombin generation → further endothelial activation, sustained by PAI-1-mediated hypofibrinolysis — is precisely the kind of self-reinforcing attractor state described in hypothesis:0001.

3. **Evidence level for clinical thromboembolic events is moderately strong** (large cohort HR data from tLC, N3C, and other population-level studies), while evidence for microclot persistence and NVC abnormalities remains preliminary (small, methodologically heterogeneous studies).

4. **Connection to `topic:biomarkers-and-objective-endpoints`:** The paper explicitly frames the lack of validated clinical biomarkers as a key gap, consistent with this project's ongoing biomarker tracking effort.

### Overlap and what is genuinely new vs. existing project coverage

**vs. paper:CerviaHasler2024** (platelet-driven immunothrombosis): Stojanovic2026 covers substantially overlapping territory on NETs, platelet activation, and microclots. The added value here is the broader clinical thromboembolic event data (VTE incidence, MACE HRs), the complement and glycocalyx discussion, and the peripheral/cutaneous microvascular manifestations. CerviaHasler2024 has more depth on specific platelet transcriptomics.

**vs. paper:Nicolai2023** (platelet hyperreactivity in LC): Stojanovic2026 frames the same platelet activation in a broader vascular context and adds the fibrinolytic arm (PAI-1, microclots) more explicitly. Nicolai2023 has more mechanistic granularity on platelet phenotyping.

**vs. paper:Stahlberg2025** (cardiovascular outcomes): Stojanovic2026 overlaps on MACE/cardiac complication data but situates it mechanistically within the endothelial-dysfunction → thrombosis continuum, whereas Stahlberg2025 focuses more on long-term cardiovascular outcome epidemiology.

**Genuinely new additions:** (1) Comprehensive synthesis of microvascular/cutaneous manifestations (NVC, chilblain lesions, livedo reticularis) as systemic thromboinflammatory phenomena; (2) explicit PRISMA-structured count of 130 studies across all four vascular domains; (3) updated HR estimates from tLC and N3C cohorts (2024–2025 data); (4) glycocalyx degradation as a distinct mechanistic contributor.

## Limitations

1. **Narrative synthesis only.** The review explicitly declines formal meta-analysis due to study heterogeneity; pooled effect estimates are unavailable. Quantitative claims (VTE HR, D-dimer prevalence) are taken from individual cited studies, not re-analysed.

2. **LC case definitions vary.** The review includes studies defining LC as symptom persistence ≥4 weeks (broader) and ≥12 weeks (WHO), which inflates heterogeneity in incidence and biomarker estimates. The review does not stratify findings by case definition.

3. **Cohort design limitations pass through.** Many underlying studies are cross-sectional or lack pre-infection baselines; the review inherits these confounding risks without systematic risk-of-bias scoring.

4. **No systematic quality assessment tool.** Without formal bias scoring (e.g., Newcastle-Ottawa, GRADE), the overall evidence level for individual claims is difficult to grade precisely.

5. **Covariate sensitivity not fully addressed.** The review notes but does not systematically model the effects of acute illness severity, vaccination status, SARS-CoV-2 variant, sex, and age on vascular outcomes — all key confounders flagged by PAIS AGENTS.md guidance.

6. **ICAM-1/VCAM-1 and glycocalyx evidence is described as preliminary** from small, heterogeneous studies — the review itself flags these caveats.

7. **Microclot assay methodology non-standardized.** The evidence base for fibrinolysis-resistant microclots (ref 82, 83 — primarily Pretorius group) relies on non-standardized fluorescence microscopy protocols; reproducibility and clinical significance remain contested.

8. **Antiphospholipid antibody interpretation:** Routine aPL screening is explicitly stated as not currently justified; persistence data come mainly from severe LC subsets and may not generalize.

9. **Authorship/institution context:** Authors are primarily from Serbian clinical anesthesiology and vascular surgery institutes with no external funding; no conflicts of interest declared. The review is comprehensive and well-structured, but mechanistic claims occasionally outrun the evidence quality of cited studies.

## Follow-up

- **question:0010-vascular-microclot-subphenotype** is directly addressed; this review provides the most current synthesis to anchor that question's background. The key unresolved issue is whether microclot burden (Pretorius assay) predicts clinical VTE or symptom severity in prospective studies.
- The PAI-1 / hypofibrinolysis arm deserves a dedicated literature search — PAI-1 appears here as a central self-perpetuating node but is not the primary focus of Nicolai2023 or CerviaHasler2024.
- The NVC data (reduced capillary density) could be extracted and tabulated against symptom phenotypes; this feeds the objective endpoints topic.
- Consider whether a glycocalyx-focused paper is warranted (the topic appears across several reviews but has no dedicated entity in this project).
- Antiphospholipid antibodies in LC: a targeted search on aPL persistence vs. transience in LC longitudinal cohorts would clarify the autoimmunity overlap.
