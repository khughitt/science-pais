# Small fiber neuropathy underlying dysautonomia in COVID-19 and in post-SARS-CoV-2 vaccination and long-COVID syndromes

- **Authors:** Josef Finsterer
- **Year:** 2022
- **Journal:** Muscle & Nerve, vol. 65, no. 3, pp. E31–E32
- **DOI:** 10.1002/mus.27554
- **BibTeX key:** Finsterer2022
- **Source:** PDF
- **Note:** This is a letter to the editor responding to Abrams et al. (DOI 10.1002/mus.27458) and Gemignani's editorial in *Muscle & Nerve* 2022;65:369–370. The user-supplied DOI (10.1002/mus.27549) belongs to a different paper (Nicolau et al., a reply on necrotizing myopathy); the correct DOI for this letter is 10.1002/mus.27554.

## Key Contribution

This letter to the editor argues that the Abrams et al. study of small fiber neuropathy (SFN) in SARS-CoV-2 infection, while valuable, leaves several mechanistically important autonomic pathways under-examined. Finsterer contends that long-COVID syndrome is not synonymous with post-COVID syndrome (acute COVID lasts 1–4 wks; subacute COVID 5–12 wks; only manifestations persisting beyond 12 wks constitute post-COVID syndrome, with long-COVID serving as the umbrella term), and that dysautonomia in this setting likely involves multiple overlapping mechanisms — SFN, Guillain-Barré syndrome/AMSAN, central ANS injury via hypophysitis and HPA-axis impairment, and drug-induced polyneuropathy — all of which require dedicated investigation and objective testing beyond symptom questionnaires.

## Methods

Narrative letter to the editor; no primary data collected. The author performs a literature synthesis drawing on case reports, case series, and small cohort studies to catalogue additional mechanistic pathways for dysautonomia in COVID-19 and long-COVID that were not addressed in the index paper. No formal systematic review methodology is described.

## Key Findings

**Terminology and case-definition clarification:**
- Finsterer distinguishes three temporal phases: acute COVID-19 (1–4 weeks), subacute COVID-19 (5–12 weeks), and post-COVID syndrome (clinical manifestations persisting when acute COVID-19 has resolved; >12 weeks). "Long-COVID syndrome" is the overarching umbrella term covering all phases beyond the acute episode. Differentiating these phases is stated to be critical for management and for assessing long-term outcomes.

**Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) as an under-recognized autonomic mechanism:**
- SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers autoimmune reactions including GBS. Both mRNA and vector-based anti-SARS-CoV-2 vaccines may also trigger GBS.
- GBS affects not only motor and sensory fibers but also peripheral autonomic fibers; the AMSAN (acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy) subtype can exclusively affect autonomic fibers, presenting as pure dysautonomia.
- Because GBS can be mild and recovery prolonged, autonomic manifestations in long-COVID syndrome may represent incomplete recovery from autonomic involvement in abortive GBS — a mechanism distinct from SFN.

**Central ANS injury — pituitary axis:**
- SARS-CoV-2 infection can cause hypophysitis, and patients with pre-existing pituitary micro- or macro-adenoma have increased risk of pituitary apoplexy during infection.
- Impairment of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenergic axis can lead to autonomic dysfunction via central mechanisms, separate from peripheral nerve pathology.

**Under-recognized autonomic symptom spectrum:**
- Patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection are often not investigated sufficiently for autonomic symptoms: insomnia, fatigue, cognitive impairment, hypersensitivity to light, blurred vision, dry eyes/mouth, drooling, palpitations, syncope, orthostatic dizziness, hot flashes, dysphagia, bowel/bladder dysfunction, sexual dysfunction, changes in skin/hair/nails, and abnormal sweating.
- Recommended diagnostic workup for central and peripheral ANS involvement includes: contrast-enhanced MRI of the pituitary, determination of releasing and stimulating hormones, and peripheral endocrine hormone testing. Availability and sensitivity of many peripheral ANS tests are noted to be limited.

**Drug-induced autonomic nerve injury:**
- Anti-COVID-19 drugs (e.g., lopinavir, ritonavir, daptomycin, linezolid) are neurotoxic and can cause polyneuropathy, potentially also damaging autonomic fibers — an under-recognized iatrogenic contributor to post-COVID dysautonomia.

**Recommended objective testing:**
- Finsterer argues that questionnaire-based SFN assessment is insufficient and advocates for: Quantitative Sudomotor Axon Reflex Test (QSART), quantitative sensory testing (QST), micro-neurography of C-fibers of the superficial peroneal nerve, sensory stimulation tests, deep breathing test, Valsalva maneuver, tilt testing, cerebral blood flow velocity measurements, pain-related evoked potentials (PREP), laser speckle contact analysis (LASCA), laser Doppler flowmetry, laser Doppler imaging, contact heat-evoked potentials (CHEP), corneal confocal microscopy (CCM), and skin biopsy with protein gene product (PGP) 9.5. Hormone levels and autopsy-level histological investigation of central and peripheral autonomic pathways in COVID-19 deaths are also recommended.

## Relevance

This letter is directly relevant to the PAIS project's framing of post-acute infection syndromes as reflecting failed homeostatic recovery. Specifically:

1. **Dysautonomia as a shared post-infectious failure mode.** The letter synthesizes multiple pathological mechanisms by which SARS-CoV-2 disrupts autonomic homeostasis — peripheral SFN, immune-mediated nerve injury (GBS/AMSAN), central HPA-axis impairment, and drug toxicity. This multi-mechanism framing is consistent with the PAIS concept that post-infectious chronicity reflects not a single lesion but convergent failure of regulatory systems.

2. **Case definition precision.** The distinction between subacute COVID-19 (<12 wks) and post-COVID syndrome (>12 wks persisting after resolution of acute illness) speaks directly to the research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes framing, where defining the transition from acute to post-acute disease is foundational. The argument that conflating these phases obscures management and prognosis aligns with the project's emphasis on temporal dynamics of recovery failure.

3. **Incomplete autonomic recovery as a mechanism.** Finsterer's hypothesis that long-COVID dysautonomia may represent incomplete recovery from abortive GBS — rather than ongoing SFN — maps onto the PAIS frame of "failed homeostatic recovery": the immune system successfully clears the virus but leaves persistent autonomic dysfunction as a residual. This is a mechanistically distinct model from viral persistence or ongoing inflammation.

4. **Diagnostic gap.** The under-investigation of autonomic symptoms in clinical COVID-19 management is relevant to the project's focus on why PAIS goes unrecognized: objective autonomic testing is recommended but rarely performed, meaning the prevalence of dysautonomia-based PAIS is likely underestimated.

5. **Iatrogenic contribution.** The suggestion that anti-COVID drugs may themselves damage autonomic fibers raises the question of treatment-induced confounding in post-COVID dysautonomia studies — an important source of heterogeneity for any cross-syndrome PAIS analysis.

## Limitations

- **Letter format, no primary data.** This is a commentary/letter; all claims derive from the author's literature synthesis and clinical opinion, not from a new study. No systematic search strategy, no PRISMA, no formal risk-of-bias assessment.
- **No quantitative evidence.** The letter makes no quantitative claims about prevalence, incidence, effect sizes, or proportion of long-COVID dysautonomia attributable to each mechanism. The mechanistic arguments are plausible but largely hypothesis-generating.
- **Terminology disagreement is partly semantic.** The acute/subacute/post-COVID distinction Finsterer insists on (acute: 1–4 wks; subacute: 5–12 wks; post-COVID: >12 wks after resolution) is not universally adopted; the CDC definition (symptoms ≥4 wks after infection) and WHO definition differ. The clinical relevance of these specific cutoffs is not empirically justified in this letter.
- **Mechanistic claims for vaccines are underspecified.** The assertion that mRNA and vector vaccines may trigger GBS is mentioned without quantification of absolute risk or comparison to infection-related risk; this contributes to scientific uncertainty around vaccine-associated neurological adverse events.
- **Recommended test battery is extensive and not widely available.** The 15+ test protocol proposed (CCM, micro-neurography, PREP, LASCA, etc.) is not feasible in routine clinical settings, limiting translational utility.
- **Single author, potential advocacy bias.** Finsterer is a prolific letter writer on this topic; the letter advocates for a broad investigational agenda that reflects the author's research interests.

- Questions for the project: (1) What fraction of long-COVID dysautonomia cases show objective evidence of SFN (skin biopsy PGP9.5, QSART) vs. other autonomic mechanisms? (2) Can the temporal profile of autonomic symptom onset distinguish SFN from post-GBS residual? (3) How does HPA-axis-driven dysautonomia (hypophysitis) compare symptomatically to peripheral autonomic neuropathy in post-COVID patients?
