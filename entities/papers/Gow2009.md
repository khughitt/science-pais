# A gene signature for post-infectious chronic fatigue syndrome

- **Authors:** Gow JW, Hagan S, Herzyk P, Cannon C, Behan PO, Chaudhuri A
- **Year:** 2009
- **Journal:** BMC Medical Genomics, vol. 2, article 38
- **DOI:** 10.1186/1755-8794-2-38
- **PMID:** 19555476
- **PMCID:** PMC2716361
- **BibTeX key:** Gow2009
- **Source:** Full-text via science paper-fetch (DOI 10.1186/1755-8794-2-38), read 2026-06-20

## Key Contribution

This paper generates a whole-genome PBMC transcriptional signature for post-infectious CFS using Affymetrix HG-U133A/B arrays in a small, tightly phenotyped male cohort. The 366-gene signature (286 up-regulated, 80 down-regulated after IPA mapping) implicates three functional clusters: oxidative stress, apoptosis, and viral-like immune modulation. The complete gene list and raw data are publicly deposited as GEO Series GSE14577, making this a candidate input for cross-trigger pathway-level reanalysis.

## Methods

**Study design.** Cross-sectional case-control transcriptomics comparing PBMC gene expression in post-infectious CFS patients versus healthy controls.

**Cohort.** Eight male CFS patients (age 18–54, median 36 years; median illness duration 4.5 years) with a documented history of prior viral or bacterial infection. Seven asymptomatic, physically active male controls (age 22–58 years). Both groups were matched for age, ethnicity, and geographic location (central Scotland). Female subjects were excluded to avoid confounding from reproductive-cycle endocrine variation. Patients were not clinically anxious or depressed and were not on regular medication.

**Case definition.** All patients met the Fukuda 1994 (CDC) international research criteria for CFS diagnosis. The paper uses the phrase "post-infectious CFS" and "non-psychiatric CFS" to describe the phenotype; patients had documented community-acquired infections preceding illness onset. This is a clinically characterised but ungenotyped cohort.

**Sample collection.** Venous blood collected before 11 am in EDTA tubes; PBMCs isolated by Histopaque-1077 density gradient centrifugation within 2 hours and stored as dry pellets under liquid nitrogen.

**RNA preparation.** Total RNA extracted from PBMC pellets using the Promega RNAgents Total Isolation System; quality confirmed with an Agilent RNA BioAnalyzer 2100.

**Microarray platform.** Affymetrix GeneChip HG-U133A (GPL96) and HG-U133B (GPL97) dual-chip set. The combined array covers approximately 39,000 transcripts from more than 33,000 human gene sequences (~45,000 probe sets total). All 15 samples were hybridised to both A and B chips.

**Preprocessing and normalisation.** Raw data generated with GCOS 1.1.1 (Affymetrix). Normalisation by Robust Multichip Average (RMA) implemented in FunAlyse, an automated pipeline from the Sir Henry Wellcome Functional Genomics Facility (SHWFGF), University of Glasgow. A-chip and B-chip probe sets analysed separately, then merged.

**Differential expression.** Rank Products (RP) method applied to identify significantly differentially expressed probe sets, ranking probe sets by geometric mean rank of log fold-change across all pairwise CFS-vs-control comparisons. No explicit FDR threshold stated in the methods; the RP method itself controls error rates via the expected number of false positives.

**Pathway and network analysis.** The 602 significant probe sets were annotated with EntrezGene IDs and submitted to Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA), yielding 366 non-redundant "focus" genes. Network analysis identified 21 significant networks (IPA scores 9–47) encompassing 349 of the 366 genes.

**Validation.** Defensin-α1 (DEFA1) — the second-most up-regulated gene — was validated at RNA and protein level in an independent cohort of 10 CFS patients and 10 age- and sex-matched controls using RT-PCR (288 bp amplicon) and western blotting with an anti-defensin α1–3 monoclonal antibody.

## Key Findings

**Scale of differential expression.** The Rank Products analysis identified 602 significantly altered probe sets: 413 up-regulated and 189 down-regulated. After IPA mapping to unique genes, 366 focus genes: 286 up-regulated (105 at ≥2-fold, 16 at ≥3-fold) and 80 down-regulated (25 at ≥2-fold, 3 at ≥3-fold).

**Three altered bio-systems (Table 2).**

*Oxidative stress.* Up-regulated: prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 1 (COX-1) and 2 (COX-2), haemoglobin gamma A (HBG1), haemoglobin gamma G (HBG2). Down-regulated: glutathione-S-transferase. COX-2 up-regulation is consistent with inflammatory prostaglandin production and peripheral nociceptor sensitisation.

*Apoptosis.* Up-regulated: Annexin-A3, Annexin-A5, serine/threonine kinase 17b, Histones 1 and 2, Protein S (alpha), serum deprivation response (phosphatidylserine binding protein), Caspase 1, TGF-β1, death effector filament-forming CED4-like apoptosis protein, Complement 3a receptor 1 (C3aR1), Early Growth Response 1 (EGR1), TNF-A1P3, TNF-RSF17, TNF-SF4. Also: TLR-4 and TLR-8 up-regulated, indicating macrophage activation and monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation signals.

*Immune modulation (viral-like).* Up-regulated: HLA-DRB4 and HLA-DQB1 (MHC class II β-chains); additional genes consistent with T-cell function disruption, suppression of cytotoxic death receptors, NK receptor inhibition, and dendritic cell modulation. Decay Acceleration Factor (DAF), a complement regulatory protein involved in innate immunity, was also identified.

**Network analysis.** IPA identified 21 significant networks. Top three networks contain five of the ten most up-regulated genes, centring on LTF (network A), CXCR4 (network B), and integrins α2B/β3 plus DEFA1 (network C).

**Defensin-α1 validation.** RT-PCR and western blotting in an independent cohort confirmed DEFA1 over-expression at both mRNA and protein levels in CFS versus controls. Defensins are antimicrobial peptides released by neutrophils and macrophages in response to infection, suggesting persistent or aberrant innate immune activation.

**Cross-study gene overlap (Table 3).** Genes shared with at least one prior CFS microarray study (Vernon 2002, Kaushik 2005, Fang 2006, Kerr 2008): Dynactin1, ARF1, CEACAM family, Defensin family, CXCR4, EGR1, PRKAR1A. Despite partial overlap, the authors note overall poor concordance across CFS gene-expression studies, attributable to differing patient selection criteria, array platforms, and case definitions.

## Relevance

This paper is directly relevant to the project's cross-trigger signature questions (question:0001-shared-molecular-signature-across-triggers, discussion:0002-cross-pathogen-pais-signature-convergence). Key connections:

1. **Oxidative stress as upstream driver.** The COX-1/COX-2 up-regulation and glutathione-S-transferase down-regulation provide transcriptional evidence consistent with the project hypothesis that oxidative stress is an upstream driver of bioenergetic dysfunction in PAIS (question:0016-oxidative-stress-upstream-driver-of-bioenergetic). The paper explicitly endorses this framing, citing a body of prior CFS oxidative-stress literature.

2. **Immune activation and viral-like signalling.** The MHC class II up-regulation, TLR-4/TLR-8 induction, CXCR4 over-expression, and defensin elevation collectively point to persistent innate and adaptive immune activation without a confirmed ongoing infection — a pattern seen across PAIS triggers and consistent with the shared-failure-mode frame (topic:shared-failure-mode-across-pais).

3. **Apoptosis signalling.** Caspase 1 and related apoptosis-associated gene up-regulation aligns with emerging evidence for cell-death dysregulation in long COVID and ME/CFS, though causal direction is unclear.

4. **Public dataset for reanalysis.** GSE14577 (see Model / Tool Availability below) is a candidate reusable input for pathway-level cross-trigger comparison alongside GSE130353 (Raijmakers2019, post-Q-fever fatigue). The post-infectious trigger here is community-acquired viral or bacterial infection, providing trigger diversity relative to Q-fever.

5. **Scope note.** The mechanistic claims (oxidative stress, apoptosis, viral immune evasion) are generated from pathway enrichment of a 366-gene list in a very small cross-sectional cohort. They are hypothesis-generating, not causal or established.

## Limitations

1. **Very small cohort (n=8 cases, n=7 controls).** Severely underpowered for genome-wide analysis; high risk of false positives even with Rank Products. The 366-gene signature should be considered exploratory.

2. **Male-only design.** Female subjects were excluded to remove endocrine confounding, but CFS affects women at approximately three-fold higher rates (~75% female prevalence cited in paper). Signature generalisability to female patients is explicitly unconfirmed, though the authors argue gender effects on symptom severity are small in published studies.

3. **No longitudinal sampling.** Cross-sectional single time-point; mRNA half-lives can be very short, so the snapshot may not reflect chronic disease biology.

4. **Case definition:** Fukuda 1994 (CDC) criteria. This is a broad, symptom-based definition. The cohort is additionally required to have documented prior viral or bacterial infection, narrowing to post-infectious CFS but without specifying the infecting pathogen or confirming pathogen clearance.

5. **No replication cohort for most genes.** Only DEFA1 was independently validated by RT-PCR/western blot. The remaining 365 genes in the signature lack independent replication in this study.

6. **IPA mapping losses.** The 602 significant probe sets collapse to 366 genes after IPA annotation; probe sets without IPA records are dropped, potentially excluding biologically relevant transcripts.

7. **Array generation technology (2009).** Affymetrix U133A/B covers known transcriptome at that time; lacks coverage of non-coding RNAs, novel splice variants, and post-2009 gene annotations. Re-annotation of the raw data with current genome builds may shift gene-level results.

8. **Competing interests.** The University of Glasgow filed a patent on diagnostic/treatment materials related to this work (Patent GB0502042.5), which is a potential conflict of interest for the biomarker claims.

9. **Cross-study concordance is low.** Table 3 shows only partial gene overlap with prior CFS microarray studies, and the authors acknowledge that definitional and cohort differences likely explain much of the discordance — reinforcing that the signature is not yet robust.

## Model / Tool Availability

The complete list of 366 differentially expressed genes and the underlying raw microarray data are publicly available through NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO):

- **GEO Series accession:** GSE14577
- **Array platforms:** GPL96 (Affymetrix Human Genome U133A) and GPL97 (Affymetrix Human Genome U133B)
- **Data type:** Raw CEL files and processed expression values for 8 CFS cases and 7 male healthy controls (PBMC)
- **Access:** Fully open; no restrictions

This dataset is a candidate input for a future pathway-level cross-trigger reanalysis of PAIS transcriptomes, alongside GSE130353 (Raijmakers2019, post-Q-fever fatigue PBMC data). The cross-trigger comparison would require re-normalisation and re-annotation of the CEL files against a current genome build (hg38) to harmonise with more recent datasets, since the original analysis used the RMA/RP pipeline with the 2009-era IPA database.

## Follow-up

**Papers to read next.**
- Kaushik 2005 (Gene expression in PBMC from CFS patients, J Clin Pathol) — directly compared in Table 3; partial signature overlap.
- Kerr 2008 (CFS microarray, cited for CXCR4 overlap) — another candidate cross-trigger comparator.
- Raijmakers2019 / GSE130353 — post-Q-fever fatigue PBMC transcriptome; planned cross-trigger comparator dataset.

**Questions this raises.**
- How much of the Gow2009 366-gene signature survives re-analysis with a current genome annotation and a modern normalisation pipeline (e.g., oligo/limma or DESeq2-style)?
- Do the COX-1/COX-2 and GST expression changes in GSE14577 replicate in the post-Q-fever (GSE130353) or post-COVID datasets, or are they trigger-specific?
- The male-only design limits immediate generalisability: are there other post-infectious CFS PBMC datasets with female-majority cohorts that could serve as a complementary reanalysis input?
- The Rank Products method does not report FDR values in the paper; re-running with a defined FDR cutoff (e.g., <0.05) may substantially reduce the gene list size — worth assessing before treating GSE14577 as a fixed input.
