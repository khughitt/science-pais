# Unexplained post-acute infection syndromes

- **Authors:** Jan Choutka, Viraj Jansari, Mady Hornig, Akiko Iwasaki
- **Year:** 2022
- **Journal:** Nature Medicine, vol. 28, pp. 911–923
- **DOI:** 10.1038/s41591-022-01810-6
- **BibTeX key:** Choutka2022
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This landmark review establishes that post-acute infection syndromes (PAISs) — chronic, unexplained disability following diverse acute infections — are not unique to SARS-CoV-2 and instead form a coherent class of conditions with overlapping symptomatology and likely shared pathophysiological mechanisms. The authors synthesize epidemiological and mechanistic evidence across more than a dozen pathogens (SARS-CoV-2, Ebola, dengue, chikungunya, EBV, Borrelia, Coxiella burnetii, West Nile virus, Giardia, and others) and propose four non-exclusive mechanistic hypotheses: (1) persistent pathogen or remnant antigens, (2) autoimmunity triggered by the infection, (3) dysbiosis or reactivation of latent viruses, and (4) unrepaired tissue damage. The review calls explicitly for a unified conceptual framework, shared nomenclature, and dedicated basic biomedical research into PAISs as a class — making it the foundational framing paper for this project.

## Methods

This is a narrative/systematic review. The authors conducted a comprehensive literature search covering epidemiological cohort studies, case-control studies, and mechanistic reports on PAISs across multiple pathogens. ## Key Findings

**Epidemiology and prevalence**
- PAIS is not severity-dependent: it commonly follows mild or even asymptomatic acute infection, and has predominantly been identified in females (notably for PASC/long COVID).
- After infectious mononucleosis (EBV), 30–40% of cases have persistent symptoms for several weeks; prevalence drops to ~8–14% at 6 months and 7–9% at 12 months, with 4% still debilitated at 2-year follow-up (Katz et al.).
- Post-Q-fever fatigue syndrome: 19.4% met ME/CFS criteria at 5 years post-outbreak; 28% of a UK slaughterhouse cohort met ME/CFS criteria 14–15 years after infection.
- Post-West Nile virus: an 8-year Texas cohort showed persistent symptoms in 40–70% of participants across three diagnostic subgroups, with a plateau after ~2 years. 64% of those with post-infection fatigue for ≥6 months met ME/CFS criteria at 5 years.
- Post-Giardia: IBS prevalence stagnated at 40% at 6 years and 43% at 10 years in a Norwegian outbreak cohort; 41.5% met ME/CFS diagnostic criteria 5 years after outbreak.
- SARS (2002–2004): 10–20% post-SARS sequelae prevalence at 2 months to 12 years follow-up (meta-analysis); 27.1% met ME/CFS criteria 4 years after SARS in a Hong Kong long-term study.
- Post-PASC (long COVID): ONS data show ~10% self-reported long COVID with activity limitation persisting beyond 168 days post-acute illness.
- An Australian primary-care surveillance study (EBV, Coxiella, Ross River, n=253 prospective) showed time-dependent decreases in persistent symptoms: 35%, 27%, 12%, 9% unrecovered at 1.4, 3, 6, and 12 months; 11% met ME/CFS criteria at 6 months.

**Symptom profile (Box 2)**
Core shared symptoms across PAISs: exertion intolerance and fatigue (the cardinal feature), unrefreshing sleep, neurocognitive impairments ("brain fog"), flu-like symptoms (feverishness, malaise, muscle pain), dysautonomia, musculoskeletal complaints, and a broad range of neurological and immunological symptoms. Trigger-specific features (e.g., anosmia/ageusia post-COVID, eye problems post-Ebola, motor disturbances post-polio and post-West Nile) are superimposed on the shared profile. Up to 75% of ME/CFS cases report an infection-like episode preceding illness onset.

**Mechanistic hypotheses (Fig. 2)**
1. *Pathogen reservoir or remnants (Fig. 2a):* Persistent viable pathogen or non-infectious fragments in deep tissues drive chronic PAMP-mediated innate activation and chronic lymphocyte stimulation. Evidence: SARS-CoV-2 RNA detected in gut, appendix, ileum, liver, gallbladder, and lymph nodes 9–180 days after negative nasopharyngeal swab; 5/14 COVID endoscopy biopsies positive for spike antigen 3 months post-diagnosis; West Nile virus RNA in urine 1.6–6.7 years post-infection; Ebola RNA in semen >2 years post-resolution; periodic restimulation of Ebola-specific antibody levels observed.
2. *Autoimmunity (Fig. 2b):* Infection can activate autoreactive T and B cells via impaired Treg function or molecular mimicry. Autoantibodies against G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) found in all 31 PASC patients examined; EBV EBNA1 antibodies cross-reacting with CNS protein GlialCAM linked to multiple sclerosis (32-fold MS risk after EBV); autoantibodies to type I IFNs, annexin A2, and other self-antigens documented in severe COVID-19.
3. *Dysbiosis/latent virus reactivation (Fig. 2c):* Microbiome disruption and reactivation of latent DNA viruses (EBV, CMV, HSV). EBV viremia at diagnosis is a risk factor for PASC development. HSV-2 reactivation can induce transient BBB opening. Gut-to-systemic translocation of pathobionts can drive autoimmunity.
4. *Tissue damage (Fig. 2d):* Unrepaired acute damage (lung fibrosis, brain atrophy, vascular damage, villous atrophy, kidney dysfunction, heart damage) locks in chronic dysfunction. Micro-clotting / thromboinflammation noted as an additional candidate.

**ME/CFS overlap**
Up to 75% of ME/CFS cases report an infection-like onset. Post-exertional malaise (PEM) / post-exertional symptom exacerbation is a defining feature and can last days to weeks. Multiple PAISs fulfill ME/CFS diagnostic criteria (1994 CDC, Canadian Consensus, NAM 2015) in a substantial minority of patients.

**Sex and other predictors**
Female sex is the most consistently reported predictor of PAIS across all syndromes reviewed. Severity of initial infection is often cited as a risk factor but PAISs occur after mild illness, and the association is inconsistent. Immunological abnormalities (cytokine levels before or during acute illness) have been reported as predisposing but mostly unconfirmed.

## Relevance

This paper is the direct conceptual foundation of this project. It addresses the core research question — "Why do some people fail to recover after acute infection, and what shared mechanisms link the post-acute infection syndromes?" — by:

1. Establishing the empirical case that PAISs are a **class**: diverse pathogens converge on a remarkably similar symptom cluster, suggesting shared pathophysiology rather than pathogen-specific idiosyncracy.
2. Framing PAISs as a **failed-recovery problem**: the acute infection resolves (or appears to) but the system does not return to baseline — directly instantiating the project's "failed homeostatic recovery" frame.
3. Cataloging the four mechanistic hypotheses (antigen/pathogen persistence, autoimmunity, dysbiosis/reactivation, tissue damage) that constitute the project's mechanistic hypothesis space.
4. Identifying ME/CFS as the convergent phenotypic attractor reachable from multiple triggers — consistent with the project's "shared dysregulated attractor" framing.
5. Flagging the methodological obstacles (lack of objective biomarkers, absence of appropriate controls, self-report bias, variable case definitions) that any future computational or clinical study in this project must address.

The paper explicitly calls for unified nomenclature, shared diagnostic markers, and basic biomedical research — tasks the project is positioned to advance through literature synthesis and future computational analyses.

## Limitations

- Narrative review structure: heterogeneous study designs, variable follow-up, and different case definitions across pathogens make formal meta-analytic comparison impossible; quantitative prevalence estimates are indicative, not definitive.
- Most prevalence data rely on self-reported symptom presence at a single time point, missing trajectory and post-exertional exacerbation dynamics that are diagnostic for ME/CFS-like states.
- Publication bias toward more severe or well-characterized outbreaks (Q fever, Ebola) means sporadic-infection PAISs are likely under-represented.
- No prospective biomarker studies: no study in the review links a mechanistic hypothesis to a validated objective marker in a controlled cohort — acknowledged explicitly by the authors.
- Predisposing factors (cytokine levels, immunogenetic background, prior infection history) remain mostly unconfirmed.
- The review does not address children/adolescents (pediatric cohorts excluded from Table 2), limiting generalizability across the lifespan.
- All four mechanistic hypotheses are non-exclusive and the paper does not propose a priority ordering or testable discriminating predictions between them.

- Primary mechanistic papers to read: Cheung et al. 2022 (residual SARS-CoV-2 antigens in GI tissue); Fischer et al. 2017 (Ebola RNA >2 years post-resolution); Davis et al. 2021 (long COVID characterization); Lanz et al. 2022 (EBV EBNA1 / GlialCAM MS link); Wallukat et al. 2021 (functional autoantibodies against GPCRs in long COVID).
- Review the ME/CFS case-definition literature (1994 CDC / Fukuda, Canadian Consensus Criteria, NAM 2015) to clarify which PAISs in the project's scope formally overlap.
- Assess whether the four mechanistic hypotheses can be operationalized as discriminating computational signatures (e.g., transcriptomic, proteomic, or microbiome features) in available PAIS datasets.
- Examine sex-disaggregated cohort data for each PAIS to characterize the female-sex effect more precisely.
