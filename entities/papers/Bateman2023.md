# Fibromyalgia and myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome

- **Authors:** Lucinda Bateman
- **Year:** 2023
- **Journal/Book:** Neurobiology of Brain Disorders (2nd ed.), Chapter 32, pp. 559–570
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **DOI:** 10.1016/B978-0-323-85654-6.00061-7
- **BibTeX key:** Bateman2023
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This clinically authoritative textbook chapter by Lucinda Bateman (Bateman Horne Center) provides a paired neurobiology-framed overview of fibromyalgia (FM) and ME/CFS, arguing both conditions are best understood through the lens of neurobiology — specifically neuroimmunology, central sensitization, and dysautonomia — rather than as psychosomatic or purely functional disorders. The chapter traces the evolution of case definitions for both conditions (1990 ACR tender-point criteria through 2019 AAPT criteria for FM; Holmes/Fukuda through Canadian Consensus Criteria and 2015 NAM criteria for ME/CFS), reviews the multisystem pathophysiology including infections as triggers, immune and neuroinflammatory dysfunction, orthostatic intolerance, and impaired energy production, and surveys comorbid conditions that form a coherent illness cluster. A key clinical observation is that ME/CFS frequently follows a viral or flu-like acute infection — establishing it explicitly as a post-infectious syndrome within the broader PAIS frame.

## Methods

Narrative textbook chapter review drawing on the published clinical and research literature up to approximately 2022. Covers epidemiological studies (CDC population surveys, Chicago-based community study, CDC Wichita study), clinical trials (rituximab RCT, antiviral studies), neuroimaging (fMRI, MRS), polysomnography cohorts, cytokine/immunological studies (PBMC stimulation assays), and small fiber neuropathy (SFN) skin biopsy series. No original data are presented. The approach is to survey the evidence landscape organized by condition, then by comorbid overlaps, then by treatment options.

## Key Findings

**Fibromyalgia — epidemiology and diagnosis**
- Population prevalence: 2–3% overall; 3–5% of women, rising to 7–8% in women age 60+; 0.5–1.5% of men. Second most common rheumatologic condition after osteoarthritis.
- Diagnostic criteria have evolved substantially: 1990 ACR criteria (tender-point exam, widespread pain >3 months) → 2010 ACR revision (WPI + SSS, eliminating tender points; sex ratio narrowed from 13:1 to 2:1 female-to-male) → 2016 ACR revision → 2019 AAPT criteria (pain in ≥6 of 9 sites + moderate-to-severe sleep problems or fatigue, all ≥3 months).
- Prevalence changed substantially across criteria: 1.7% (1990 criteria in clinic populations) to 5.4% (2010 criteria).

**Fibromyalgia — pathophysiology**
- Central sensitization is the physiological hallmark: augmented afferent pain signaling, decreased descending analgesic pathway activity, global hyperalgesia.
- Elevated substance P in cerebrospinal fluid, elevated glutamate in brain regions (insula), functional MRI evidence of augmented central pain processing.
- Alpha-wave intrusion in deep sleep documented by polysomnography.
- Small fiber neuropathy (SFN): Oaklander proposed up to 50% of adult FM patients and an even higher proportion of pediatric FM patients have skin biopsy evidence of SFN — a finding that, if replicated, would reconceptualize FM as involving a peripheral neuropathic component driving central amplification. The finding is contested given SFN's nonspecificity across >50 conditions.
- Genetic predisposition: first-degree relatives of FM patients are 8× more likely to have the condition; ~50% of FM risk estimated as genetic; twin studies implicate same risk factors for IBS and headache.
- Cytokine signature: a 2015 PBMC stimulation study (FM N=160, SLE N=100, RA N=98, controls N=119) found a 4-cytokine/chemokine panel (IL-6, IL-8, MIP-1a, MIP-1b) 93% sensitive and 89.4% specific for FM vs. controls; RA and SLE patients also elevated (31% and 29% respectively), complicating specificity claims.

**ME/CFS — epidemiology and diagnosis**
- Community-based prevalence: 0.42% (420/100,000) in a 1999 Chicago study; a CDC study based in Wichita (published 2003) estimated 235/100,000; a 2007 Georgia population study found 2.54% of persons aged 18–59 years — 8–10-fold higher than prior estimates.
- More than 75% of patients took longer than 1 year to get a diagnosis; >25% took longer than 5 years.
- Case definition evolution: Holmes/CDC criteria (1988) → Fukuda/CDC criteria (1994, required fatigue + 4 of 8 minor symptoms ≥6 months, all other causes ruled out) → Canadian Consensus Criteria / CCC (2003, required fatigue + PEM + unrefreshing sleep + pain + ≥2 neurocognitive + ≥1 autonomic/neuroendocrine/immune) → NAM/IOM clinical criteria (2015, three core symptoms: substantial functional impairment + PEM + unrefreshing sleep, plus ≥1 of cognitive impairment or orthostatic intolerance).
- The 2015 NAM criteria notably removed the exclusion-based structure and stated ME/CFS is valid irrespective of other diagnoses.

**ME/CFS — infections as triggers**
- Prospective studies have demonstrated postinfection fatigue syndromes following numerous infections, sometimes in more than 10% of those infected. Pathogens documented include: Epstein-Barr Virus, Ross River virus, Dengue virus, enteroviruses, human herpesvirus-6, Parvovirus, West Nile Virus, SARS-CoV-1, SARS-CoV-2, Borrelia burgdorferi, Coxiella burnetii, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Giardia lamblia.
- Typical clinical course: follows a seemingly ordinary respiratory or GI infection; 10–14 days of antibiotic/steroid treatment fails; persistent fatigue and malaise emerge; push-crash cycle begins at 8–12 weeks; diagnosis delayed months to years.
- COVID-19: by 1 year into the pandemic, at least 10% of infected patients developed symptoms reminiscent of ME/CFS for months after the acute infection resolved.

**ME/CFS — immunity, inflammation, and neuroinflammation**
- Growing consensus that immune factors drive a postinfection syndrome in ME/CFS. Persistent viral antigen, reactivated infection, and abnormally persistent inflammation are all candidate mechanisms.
- Abnormal serum cytokine profiles correlate with disease severity or duration in some studies; abnormal proteins in cerebrospinal fluid also reported.
- B-cell subsets, T-cell activation, potential autoantibodies against nuclear and membrane structures and neurotransmitter receptors, abnormal circulating cytokines, and increased HLA prevalence all reported.
- Neuroinflammation: glial cell activation throughout the brain correlating with ME/CFS symptoms and severity demonstrated in a small influential study; whole brain MRS showed increased temperature in right insula, putamen, frontal cortex, thalamus, and cerebellum (all P < 0.05) in ME/CFS subjects vs. controls, not attributable to body temperature or cerebral perfusion differences.
- Elevated lactate in brain detected; reduced cerebral blood flow on head-up tilt table testing even without tachycardia or hypotension; the most severely ill ME/CFS patients experienced reduced cerebral blood flow just by sitting up.

**ME/CFS — orthostatic intolerance**
- Both CCC and NAM criteria place orthostatic intolerance as a major illness manifestation. Invasive cardiopulmonary exercise test (iCPET) studies in ME/CFS patients with exercise intolerance documented: low atrial and ventricular filling pressures (impaired venous return), impaired peripheral tissue oxygen extraction. 30% of ME/CFS patients studied with iCPET had biopsy-proven SFN; neuropathic microvascular dilation may cause shunting of oxygenated blood, reduced venous return, lower cardiac output.

**ME/CFS — impaired energy production**
- Markedly impaired tolerance for physical or cognitive activity + PEM is the pathognomonic feature. Multiple metabolomic studies provide support for impaired mitochondrial function. In a cognitive + exercise fMRI study, ME/CFS subjects (unlike controls) showed a gradually *increasing* error rate on the paced auditory serial addition task (PASAT) while completing serial cognitive tests alongside exercise — indicating increasing cognitive failure, not learning.

**Comorbid and overlapping conditions**
- FM co-occurs in: 25% of RA patients, 30% of SLE patients, 50% of Sjogren's syndrome patients (one study); 6.6% RA, 13.4% SLE, 12.6% AS, 10.1% OA, 12% SS, 25% vasculitis, 1.4% gout, 6.9% polymyalgia rheumatica/PMR in another study.
- Shared comorbid cluster: mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS), POTS, small fiber neuropathy, sleep disorders, IBS, interstitial cystitis, migraine, hypermobile Ehler-Danlos syndrome (hEDS).
- MCAS symptom overlap with ME/CFS/FM is significant: fatigue (83%), FM-like pain (75%), presyncope/syncope (71%), headache (63%), paresthesia (58%), nausea/vomiting (57%), chills (56%), dyspnea (53%), cognitive dysfunction (49%), abdominal pain (48%), palpitations (47%), sweats (47%), etc.
- Four ME/CFS sleep phenotypes identified by polysomnography; intrinsic sleep disruption present in both FM and ME/CFS beyond what primary sleep disorders can explain.

**Treatments**
- FM: Three FDA-approved drugs (pregabalin, duloxetine, milnacipran); low-dose naltrexone (widely available, well-tolerated, evidence for FM symptom modulation); low-dose aripiprazole (0.2–2 mg/day, retrospective Stanford ME/CFS clinic series: 75/101 improved in fatigue, brain fog, or unrefreshing sleep).
- ME/CFS: Activity pacing (not graded exercise therapy) is the key management strategy. PEM is worsened by cognitive, physical, or upright activity; pacing and stress management are central. Rituximab: initial open-label enthusiasm (improvement in ME/CFS patients treated for lymphoma) → small double-blind RCT suggested autoimmunity role → randomized double-blind placebo-controlled RCT (2019) showed no difference between rituximab and placebo over 12 months.
- COVID-19 as a silver lining: provides unprecedented prospective study of postviral sequelae, real-time scientific scrutiny, and opportunity to track postinfectious PAIS almost like a prospective study.

## Relevance

This chapter directly addresses the project research question (research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes) by situating ME/CFS unambiguously within the PAIS frame: a substantial proportion of ME/CFS cases arise acutely after an identified infection (EBV, Ross River, dengue, SARS-CoV-1/2, Borrelia, Coxiella, and others), and the clinical presentation — persistent immune activation, neuroinflammation, orthostatic intolerance, and impaired energy metabolism — maps directly onto the PAIS model of failed homeostatic recovery after acute infection.

Key connections to the PAIS frame of shared post-infectious failure mode:

1. **Infection as proximate trigger:** The chapter explicitly documents that ME/CFS follows a wide spectrum of pathogens in >10% of infected individuals, consistent with a pathogen-agnostic homeostatic failure mechanism rather than pathogen-specific damage.
2. **Persistent immune activation:** Immune dysregulation (cytokine abnormalities, autoantibodies, neuroinflammation with glial activation) persists long after the acute infection resolves — the defining characteristic of PAIS in this project's frame.
3. **Orthostatic intolerance and dysautonomia:** The autonomic nervous system failure documented via POTS, orthostatic hypotension, and abnormal iCPET findings in ME/CFS parallels dysautonomia as a shared PAIS mechanism.
4. **Impaired energy metabolism:** Metabolomic and functional evidence for mitochondrial dysfunction aligns with the metabolic failure axis of the PAIS shared failure mode.
5. **Overlap with long COVID:** The chapter explicitly notes that 10% of COVID-19 patients develop ME/CFS-like symptoms by 1 year — directly linking ME/CFS to the long COVID arm of this project.
6. **FM as a related but distinct entity:** FM shares comorbid features (central sensitization, SFN, dysautonomia, sleep disruption, MCAS) but is triggered more by diverse stressors than by infection alone, making it a partially-overlapping condition worth tracking in this project's illness-cluster mapping.
7. **Diagnostic heterogeneity as a confound:** The chapter's detailed account of shifting case definitions (Fukuda vs. CCC vs. NAM) is directly relevant to cross-study comparability in this project — a key methodological challenge for meta-analysis of PAIS incidence and mechanisms.

## Limitations

- Textbook chapter format: narrative review without systematic search protocol, PRISMA flow, or formal quality assessment of cited studies. Selection of cited evidence reflects author perspective.
- No original data; quantitative claims derived from a range of studies with heterogeneous methods, case definitions, and follow-up periods.
- The chapter covers FM and ME/CFS jointly, which risks blurring their distinct pathophysiologies. The author acknowledges case definition overlap and heterogeneity create confounds throughout.
- Cytokine biomarker findings (EpicGenetics blood test, 93%/89.4% sensitivity/specificity) cited without adequate discussion of independent replication; author notes that further validation is needed.
- The rituximab RCT null result is reported but the mechanistic implications (autoimmunity hypothesis weakened?) are not deeply explored.
- SFN prevalence estimate (up to 50% of adult FM; even higher in pediatric FM) noted as challenged by the nonspecificity of SFN across conditions; the chapter does not fully resolve this debate.
- Long COVID section (published 2023, data through ~2022) is necessarily incomplete relative to subsequent research; the 10% ME/CFS-like sequelae figure is an early estimate.
- No discussion of sex/gender differences in ME/CFS pathophysiology despite noting female predominance in epidemiology.
- Treatment section reflects clinical practice state circa 2022; does not include more recent developments in low-dose naltrexone, BC007, or vagal nerve stimulation trials.

## Follow-up

- The 2015 NAM/IOM report on ME/CFS diagnostic criteria is the primary reference for the case definition used in most current research; read for detailed methodology.
- The Canadian Consensus Criteria paper by Carruthers and colleagues (2003) established the PEM-centric ME/CFS definition most used by ME/CFS specialists; relevant for understanding divergence from Fukuda criteria in mechanistic studies.
- The cross-PAIS epidemiological and mechanistic synthesis in [cite:Choutka2022] contextualizes ME/CFS within the broader PAIS class (already in this project).
- The iCPET studies documenting impaired venous return and peripheral oxygen extraction in ME/CFS patients with SFN warrant follow-up — these are key mechanistic links between SFN, dysautonomia, and exercise intolerance in PAIS.
- Rituximab null RCT (2019) is worth reading for what the negative result implies about B-cell depletion and autoimmunity as treatable mechanisms in ME/CFS.
- The COVID-19 prospective sequelae literature (ONS long COVID data, post-COVID ME/CFS incidence studies) should be followed to update the 10% figure.
