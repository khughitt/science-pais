# Dysautonomia following Lyme disease: a key component of post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome?

- **Authors:** Brittany L. Adler, Tae Chung, Peter C. Rowe, John Aucott
- **Year:** 2024
- **Journal:** Frontiers in Neurology, vol. 15, article 1344862
- **DOI:** 10.3389/fneur.2024.1344862
- **BibTeX key:** Adler2024
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This narrative review makes the case that dysautonomia — and specifically POTS — is an under-recognized but biologically plausible complication of *Borrelia burgdorferi* infection that contributes to post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS). Drawing on case reports, small case series, animal/autopsy data, and mechanistic parallels with PASC and ME/CFS, the authors propose five non-exclusive pathogenic mechanisms (neuronal/glial apoptosis during acute infection, chronic *B. burgdorferi* persistence, chronic neuroinflammation, infection-triggered autoimmunity, and PNS/vagal dysfunction including small-fiber neuropathy) and provide a comprehensive clinical guide to evaluation and treatment. The paper calls for controlled prospective studies using objective autonomic testing in well-defined PTLDS cohorts.

## Methods

Narrative review. Literature was synthesized from: (1) case reports and small case series documenting autonomic symptoms after acute Lyme disease or in PTLDS; (2) autopsy and primate studies on *B. burgdorferi* neurotropism; (3) mechanistic literature on PASC, ME/CFS, and dysautonomia in other infection-associated chronic illnesses; (4) clinical dysautonomia evaluation and treatment literature. No systematic search protocol or PRISMA flow is reported. A mechanistic framework is presented as Figure 1 (four-domain Venn: neuroinflammation, microbial mechanisms, dysregulated immune response, PNS dysfunction). Two tables summarize diagnostic tests (Table 1) and available treatments (Table 2) for dysautonomia.

## Key Findings

**Epidemiology and prevalence**
- Approximately 10–20% of patients with Lyme disease develop chronic symptoms after adequate antibiotic treatment — the PTLDS population this review targets.
- POTS affects ~1–3 million people in the United States; the figure is likely an underestimate given underdiagnosis. Over half of POTS patients report a preceding infection.
- A small but notable subset of PTLDS patients have symptoms pointing toward peripheral autonomic neuropathy (difficulty urinating, nausea), though very few studies have formally examined this population with objective autonomic testing.

**Clinical evidence linking Lyme disease to dysautonomia**
- 18 patients with serologically positive acute Lyme disease (IgM positive) had lower cardiac vagal tone in response to deep breathing compared to 15 healthy controls.
- Painful small-fiber neuropathy with reduced intraepidermal nerve fiber density (EINFD) and reduced sweat gland nerve fiber density (SGNFD) documented during acute *B. burgdorferi* infection; symptoms improved with antibiotics in one report.
- Two separate case series totaling 7 patients treated for Lyme disease later developed new POTS symptoms 6 months to 12 years after the acute infection.
- A study of 10 patients with well-defined PTLDS (meeting IDSA case definition) on tilt table test showed reduced cerebral blood flow velocity and either sympathetic or parasympathetic dysfunction in all 10 participants.
- All 10 PTLDS patients in that same series had evidence of abnormal IENFD and/or SGNFD on skin biopsy.
- 49% of PTLDS patients had anti-neural antibodies vs. 15% of healthy controls and 18.5% of Lyme-recovered individuals (Rebman et al.) — though specificity of these antibodies remains unknown.

**Mechanistic hypotheses (Figure 1)**

1. *Neuronal and glial cell apoptosis during acute infection:* *B. burgdorferi* directly infects dorsal root ganglia in rhesus monkeys (Ramesh et al.) causing neuronal apoptosis, satellite glial cell apoptosis, and ganglionic lipoprotein staining. Rhesus DRG tissue explants exposed to live spirochetes induce inflammatory response and neural apoptosis. Injury to ANS during acute infection may be irreversible or take months to recover.

2. *Chronic B. burgdorferi infection in the nervous system:* Persistent infection after antibiotic therapy is anecdotal and limited to case reports/case series. A recent study found *B. burgdorferi* peptidoglycan in synovial fluid of antibiotic-refractory late Lyme arthritis patients. Re-treatment with IV ceftriaxone + doxycycline does not provide dramatic or sustained improvement in PTLDS. Chronic/persistent infection remains mechanistically plausible but unproven in humans (evidence rated: anecdotal and limited).

3. *Chronic neuroinflammation:* Imaging studies in PTLDS show microglial activation in CNS, similar to ME/CFS. Proposed pathway: infection → cytokine/chemokine release → BBB disruption → microglial and astrocyte activation → ROS and further neuroinflammation. Non-viable spirochete residues after antibiotics may retain ability to induce inflammation. Vagal nerve dysfunction may contribute via reduced anti-inflammatory cholinergic signaling.

4. *Autoimmunity triggered by the pathogen:* Molecular mimicry proposed as mechanism for chronic symptoms (lag of 6+ months between infection and symptom onset is supportive). Anti-neural antibodies elevated in PTLDS (49% vs. 15% healthy controls). Autoantibodies against ganglionic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (identified in other dysautonomia syndromes) found in PASC; prevalence unknown in PTLDS. A single case report of a PTLDS patient with polyneuropathy achieved full recovery with subcutaneous immunoglobulin.

5. *PNS dysfunction — vagal nerve and small-fiber neuropathy:* *B. burgdorferi* known to infect cranial nerves including vagus nerve (CN X). Vagal nerve dysfunction impairs anti-inflammatory cholinergic signaling, promoting neuroinflammation and microglial activation. Small-fiber neuropathy in a non-length-dependent pattern (autonomic/dorsal root ganglia as target) observed across POTS etiologies; hypothesized as final common pathway for nerve damage.

**Additional contributing mechanisms discussed**
- Vascular/endothelial damage: *B. burgdorferi* spirochetes adhere to and penetrate endothelial cells in vitro, activating inflammatory transendothelial migration of neutrophils. Fibrin amyloid microclots and platelet hyperactivation reported in PASC. No studies in PTLDS to date.
- Gut microbiota dysregulation: PTLDS patients have altered gut microbiome vs. controls with/without antibiotic history. GI dysmotility in dysautonomia leads to SIBO, further disrupting microbiome.
- Genetic contribution: Dysautonomia runs in families; co-occurrence with joint hypermobility/Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome suggests genetic predisposition. Pathogenic mutations in norepinephrine transporter gene and sodium channel gain-of-function mutations documented.
- Cardiovascular deconditioning: Over 90% of POTS patients have reduced VO2 max. However, deconditioning does not explain orthostatic intolerance (no relationship to cerebral blood flow reduction on tilt in ME/CFS patients; orthostatic intolerance in elite athletes argues against deconditioning as primary driver).

**Diagnostic evaluation guidance (Table 1)**
- Head-up tilt table test or 10-min active/passive stand test: defines POTS (sustained HR increase ≥30 bpm in adults, or ≥40 bpm in 12–19 year olds, within 10 min of upright posture).
- Additional tests: heart rate variability on deep breathing, Valsalva maneuver, transcranial/extra-cranial Doppler ultrasound with tilt, QSART, thermoregulatory sweat test (TST), GI motility testing, skin biopsy for IENFD/SGNFD.
- Validated questionnaires: COMPASS-31, Survey of Autonomic Symptoms (SAS), Malmo POTS Symptom Score.

**Treatment overview (Table 2)**
- Non-pharmacologic: increased fluid and sodium intake, compression stockings/abdominal binder, physical therapy (cardiovascular/strength training).
- Pharmacologic: vasoconstrictors (midodrine, droxidopa, stimulants); beta-blockers or ivabradine (HR reduction); fludrocortisone (mineralocorticoid, blood volume); pyridostigmine (cholinesterase inhibitor, improves hemodynamics and GI motility); clonidine/guanfacine/methyldopa (sympatholytics for hyperadrenergic POTS); prokinetic agents for GI dysmotility; stimulants/modafinil for cognitive deficits and fatigue.
- Emerging/investigational: IVIG (single PTLDS case report of full recovery; multiple PASC case series; randomized trials ongoing for POTS/PASC); efgartigimod (neonatal Fc receptor inhibitor, trials for POTS after COVID-19); vagal nerve stimulation (ameliorates microglial activation and cytokine production; being studied for PASC and ME/CFS); antivirals for PASC.

## Relevance

This paper directly addresses the project research question (research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes) on two levels:

1. **PTLDS as a canonical PAIS:** PTLDS is one of the longest-studied post-acute infection syndromes. By documenting that ~10–20% of adequately treated Lyme disease patients fail to recover fully, and by characterizing the symptom overlap with PASC and ME/CFS, the review reinforces the "shared failure mode" framing central to this project.

2. **Dysautonomia as a cross-PAIS convergence mechanism:** The paper explicitly situates PTLDS dysautonomia within a cross-syndrome framework: the same mechanisms proposed for PTLDS (small-fiber neuropathy, neuroinflammation, autoimmunity, vagal dysfunction) are active in PASC and ME/CFS. This makes dysautonomia — and specifically POTS — a candidate shared mechanistic node linking multiple PAISs. The project's homeostatic-recovery frame maps directly: ANS dysfunction represents a failure of autonomic homeostasis that persists after the infectious trigger has resolved.

3. **Mechanistic specificity:** The five-mechanism Venn framework (Figure 1) provides the most pathogen-specific mechanistic account for PTLDS in the project's dataset, adding *B. burgdorferi*-specific biology (spirochete neurotropism, direct ganglionic infection, spirochete peptidoglycan persistence) to the generic PAIS mechanism palette.

4. **Diagnostic and therapeutic translation:** The paper's Tables 1 and 2 are directly actionable: they define objective tests for autonomic dysfunction and enumerate treatments. For the project, this represents a case study of how clinical characterization of a PAIS complication proceeds — and reveals the methodological gap (no controlled prospective autonomic testing studies in PTLDS) that marks the failed homeostatic recovery as under-measured.

## Limitations

- **Narrative (not systematic) review:** No PRISMA flow, no formal quality appraisal of included studies. Evidence base is dominated by small case series (n=7–18), single case reports, and primate/autopsy studies; no prospective cohort data with objective autonomic testing in well-defined PTLDS cohorts exists.
- **Causal direction unestablished:** Without temporal studies demonstrating higher incidence of dysautonomia closer to the time of *B. burgdorferi* infection, or prospective controlled data showing higher dysautonomia rates in post-Lyme vs. non-Lyme controls, the association remains suggestive rather than demonstrated.
- **Case definition heterogeneity:** Not all cited case reports used strict diagnostic criteria for Lyme disease (erythema migrans rash, confirmed serology, objective manifestations). Several relied solely on serology, which is not diagnostic of active infection.
- **Confounding by co-infections:** Up to two-thirds of erythema migrans patients also have *Anaplasma phagocytophilum* or *Babesia microti* co-infections; human studies of PTLDS rarely assess complete tick-borne co-infection profiles. Co-infections (including EBV) may confound mechanistic attribution to *B. burgdorferi*.
- **Antibiotic confounding:** Most patients in reported case series were treated concurrently with multiple medications in addition to antibiotics, making it impossible to attribute improvement to antibiotics alone.
- **No histopathological studies in humans:** No studies to date have examined the autonomic ganglia or other neural tissue of PTLDS patients; the neuronal apoptosis and ganglionic inflammation evidence comes entirely from autopsy reports and primate models.
- **Genetic/host factors not systematically accounted for:** Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome and joint hypermobility co-occur with dysautonomia and may be unmasked by infection rather than caused by it; existing PTLDS studies do not routinely screen for these predispositions.
- **Treatment evidence in PTLDS is largely by extrapolation from PASC/ME/CFS:** Only one case report of IVIG in PTLDS; all pharmacologic recommendations are imported from other dysautonomia etiologies.

## Model / Tool Availability

Not applicable. This is a narrative review. No computational model, dataset, or software tool is released. Tables 1 and 2 provide clinical reference material for dysautonomia evaluation and treatment.

## Follow-up

- Primary data needed: controlled prospective study using objective autonomic testing (tilt table, QSART, skin biopsy) in a well-defined PTLDS cohort (meeting IDSA case definition) vs. Lyme-recovered and healthy controls.
- Key papers to read: Rebman et al. 2017 (PTLDS characterization, Front Med); Novak et al. 2019 (small-fiber neuropathy in PTLDS, PLoS ONE); Ramesh et al. 2013 (B. burgdorferi ganglionic apoptosis, J Neuroinflammation); Aucott et al. 2022 (PTLDS risk, J Infect Dis).
- Examine whether the 10-patient PTLDS tilt table study (reference 47) with universal cerebral blood flow reduction and autonomic dysfunction is published in a peer-reviewed form with full methods.
- Assess overlap between PTLDS dysautonomia and PASC dysautonomia using validated instruments (COMPASS-31) across cohorts — are questionnaire-based profiles distinguishable?
- Vagal nerve stimulation is being studied in PASC and ME/CFS (references 108–110); monitor trial results for applicability to PTLDS.
- Efgartigimod (neonatal Fc receptor inhibitor, NCT05633407) trial for POTS post-COVID-19 — results will test the autoimmune mechanism and may be translatable to PTLDS.
