# Chronic inflammation in post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 modulates gut microbiome: a review of literature on COVID-19 sequelae and gut dysbiosis

## Key Contribution

This narrative review synthesises mechanistic and epidemiological evidence linking gut dysbiosis to the persistence and multi-system symptom burden of Long COVID (PASC), with a focus on two convergent pathways: (1) SARS-CoV-2-mediated ACE2 dysfunction that directly disrupts gut homeostasis via impaired mTOR signalling, reduced antimicrobial peptide secretion, and leaky gut; and (2) dysbiosis-driven microbiota-gut-brain (MGB) axis dysregulation that propagates neuroinflammation, HPA axis dysfunction, and neuropsychiatric sequelae. The review catalogues dysbiotic signatures across the acute, 3-6 month, and 12-month post-COVID phases, identifies risk factors (age, sex, obesity, T2DM, acute severity, ethnicity) that predispose to both severe dysbiosis and Long COVID, and surveys dietary, prebiotic, and probiotic interventions with potential to restore microbiome balance.

## Methods

**Study type:** Narrative review (not systematic meta-analysis); UWARN COVID Supplement 3 (NIAID/CREID-funded).

**Scope:** Literature on Long COVID epidemiology, case definitions, gut microbiome changes in SARS-CoV-2 infection, gut-brain axis biology, inflammation biomarkers, immune cell changes, and microbiome-targeted therapeutics (diet, prebiotics, probiotics). English-language publications through approximately late 2023/early 2024.

**Case definitions reviewed (Table 1):**
- CDC: persistent symptoms ≥4 weeks post-acute infection
- WHO: symptoms at 3 months post-infection, persisting ≥2 months, no alternative explanation
- NICE: 4-12 weeks (ongoing symptomatic COVID) or >12 weeks (post-COVID-19 syndrome)
- RECOVER cohort: used WHO's PASC definition

**Key cohort data cited:**
- RECOVER cohort (n=8,646): most common symptoms at >6 months — PEM 87%, fatigue 85%, brain fog 64%, dizziness 62%, GI manifestations 57%
- Meta-analysis of 10,945 cases at 6-12 months: muscle weakness 54%, fatigue 30.94%, dyspnea 27.06%, anxiety 25.19%
- Systematic review: 22% of Long COVID patients had GI-related symptoms vs. 12% in acute phase
- 486,149-person cohort (US): ethnicity significantly associated with Long COVID risk; Black, South Asian, and other ethnic minority groups more susceptible than white populations

**No original data were generated.** All findings are synthesised from cited primary literature.

## Key Findings

### Epidemiology and Risk Factors
- Cumulative prevalence of COVID-19 sequelae is approximately 6× higher than for comparable viral illnesses (mononucleosis, pneumonia)
- ~13% of COVID-19 patients experience persistent symptoms at 1 month; 2.5% at 3 months; >30% among hospitalised patients with severe acute disease
- Risk factors: older age (>40% of those >65 years experience prolonged symptoms vs. 26-30% aged 18-64); female sex (post-menopausal oestrogen decline linked to higher inflammatory burden); obesity (BMI >25.9 kg/m² associated with higher Long COVID risk in a health-care worker cohort); Type 2 diabetes (via CD8+ T cell suppression and inflammatory baseline state); severe acute infection (ICU/mechanical ventilation → 3.6× higher persistent symptom risk); ethnicity; nutrient deficiency

### ACE2-Mediated Gut Disruption (Mechanism 1)
- SARS-CoV-2 uses ACE2 as an entry receptor; ACE2 is abundant in GI tract (small intestine) and co-localises with TMPRSS2 and B⁰AT1 (tryptophan/glutamine transporter)
- ACE2 internalisation reduces tryptophan absorption → impairs mTOR signalling in Paneth cells → reduced antimicrobial peptide (AMP) production → impaired tight junction (TJ) protein expression (Occludin, Claudin-4, ZO-1, ZO-2) → increased gut permeability
- Reduced ACE2 also impairs Ang II → Ang 1-7 conversion → pro-inflammatory shift and reduced vasodilation
- SARS-CoV-2 RNA detected in feces of 12.7% of patients at 4 months post-positive test; 3.8% at 7 months, indicating ongoing GI viral persistence

### Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs) as Central Homeostatic Mediators
- SCFAs (butyrate, acetate, propionate) are reduced in Long COVID, produced primarily by Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, Dorea, Bifidobacterium — all depleted in PASC
- SCFAs maintain: intestinal epithelial TJ integrity, blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity via Occludin/Claudin-5, FOXP3+ Treg cell levels, IgA production by B cells, NLRP3 inflammasome modulation (anti-inflammatory), GPR43/41/109A signalling (anti-inflammatory)
- Reduced SCFAs → ↓ BBB integrity → LPS, pathogens, and cytokines leak into CNS → microglial activation → neuroinflammation

### Dysbiotic Signatures by Phase (Table 3)
**Acute COVID-19:** ↑ Streptococcus, Enterococcus, Corynebacterium; ↓ Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium Roseburia, Lachnospira, Prevotella

**3-6 months post-COVID (n=133 study):** Long COVID patients had less diverse microbiome; ↓ Bifidobacterium adolescentis, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Lachnospiraceae; ↑ Candida albicans, Pseudomonas phages Pf1, Klebsiella spp.; ↓ SCFAs and L-isoleucine; microbiome impact persisted up to 6 months

**12 months post-COVID (n=84 cohort):** ↓ Eubacterium hallii, Subdoligranulum, Ruminococcus, Dorea, Coprococcus, Eubacterium ventriosum (all SCFA producers; negative correlation with CRP); these deficits correlated inversely with lymphocyte counts and positively with CRP

**Symptom-specific microbiome changes:**
- Respiratory symptoms: ↑ Streptococcus anginosus, S. vestibularis, S. gordonii
- Neurological symptoms: ↑ Clostridium innocuum, Actinomyces naeslundii
- Depression/mental stress: ↓ Lactobacillus, alterations in Clostridia
- Hair loss: ↓ Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii

### Microbiota-Gut-Brain (MGB) Axis Dysregulation (Mechanism 2)
- Reduced SCFAs → ↓ enteroendocrine cell (EEC) neurotransmitter synthesis → ↓ vagal nerve stimulation → ↑ macrophage TNF-α production → neuroinflammation
- Increased gut permeability → LPS + opportunistic pathogen products cross BBB → macrophage activation → ↑ IL-17, IL-6, IL-1 → neuroinflammation → HPA axis dysregulation
- HPA dysregulation: hypocortisolism in Long COVID (dysregulated cortisol, ↑ ACTH) → disrupted glucocorticoid signalling in prefrontal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus → synaptic and neuronal integrity compromise
- Three MGB pathways described: neuroinflammation, neuroendocrine, and direct vagus nerve pathway

### Inflammatory Biomarker Profile
- Elevated in Long COVID: IL-17, IL-2 (via TH17-mediated response); low IL-4, IL-10 (anti-inflammatory)
- Meta-analysis of 23 studies (18 markers): CRP, LDH, D-dimer, leucocytes remain elevated vs. recovered patients
- Inflammatory prediction model for Long COVID: 78.5-81.6% accuracy based on set of biomarkers
- Short-term PASC (2 months post-discharge): fatigue correlated with ↑ ESR, LDH, CRP, D-dimer, ↓ haemoglobin, albumin, ferritin

### Immune Cell Changes
- ↑ CD8+ T lymphocytes (cytotoxic, especially effector CD8+); ↑ PD-1, TIM-3 expression on CD3+ cells → exhaustion phenotype
- ↑ CD38⁺HLA-DR⁺ myeloid cells, ↑ active CD56+ NK cells, ↑ plasmacytoid dendritic cells
- ↑ memory B cells and plasmablasts producing IgA, IgG; anti-RBD antibody titres correlated with severity
- Autoantibodies (including anti-ACE2 IgG) elevated; suppression of ACE2 expression by anti-ACE2 IgG blunts anti-inflammatory effects

### Therapeutic Interventions
**Probiotics:** 51% reduction in symptom severity (meta-analysis) with Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacillus spp. administration; improvements in headache, cough, diarrhea, fatigue, cognition; probiotic/prebiotic combination (Bifidobacteria strains) reduced viral load and IL-6, MCP-1, M-CSF, IL-1RA, TNF-α in COVID-19 patients at 5 weeks

**Diet:** Mediterranean diet (n=612): ↓ CRP, IL-17; ↑ Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Eubacterium; plant-based diet → ↓ risk of severe COVID-19

**Vitamin D:** RCT in hospitalised patients — calcifediol on admission → 2% ICU admission (treated) vs. 50% (untreated); cross-sectional study showed lower vitamin D at admission → higher risk of neurocognitive symptoms, dysgeusia, headache at 6 months

**SCFAs/prebiotics:** Prebiotic inulin → ↑ GLP-2, ↓ serum zonulin (improved barrier function); cranberry oligosaccharides → ↑ SCFA production from Lactobacillus strains

## Relevance

This review is directly relevant to the project research question (research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes) and the PAIS frame of **failed homeostatic recovery after acute infection** in the following ways:

1. **Gut dysbiosis as a persistor of the PAIS state:** The paper provides mechanistic grounding for why post-acute GI and systemic symptoms fail to resolve. SARS-CoV-2-mediated ACE2 downregulation disrupts the tryptophan-mTOR-AMP axis, initiating a self-sustaining dysbiotic state (depleted SCFA producers, increased opportunistic pathogens) that amplifies inflammation and impairs barrier function rather than allowing homeostatic recovery. This is a concrete molecular pathway by which the acute insult transitions into the chronic PAIS state.

2. **Multi-system failure propagated via the gut:** The MGB axis dysregulation described here — reduced SCFAs → impaired BBB → neuroinflammation → HPA dysregulation — explains how a localised gut perturbation propagates into neuropsychiatric, neuroendocrine, and immune system failure. This is paradigmatic of the project's frame of PAIS as multisystem failed recovery rather than organ-specific pathology.

3. **Quantified temporal dynamics of dysbiosis:** The review documents that gut microbial perturbation persists and evolves through the acute, 3-6 month, and 12-month post-infection phases, with distinct dysbiotic signatures at each stage. This temporal evidence supports the idea that dysbiosis is not merely a consequence of acute inflammation but an ongoing driver of PAIS maintenance — a key distinction for the project's mechanistic models.

4. **Identifies potentially modifiable nodes:** The probiotic, prebiotic, and dietary data (albeit preliminary) point to gut microbiome restoration as a tractable intervention target for PAIS recovery — directly relevant to the project's interest in factors that enable vs. impede homeostatic recovery.

5. **Cross-PAIS relevance:** While focused on Long COVID, the gut-brain axis disruption and SCFA-depletion mechanisms reviewed here are not COVID-specific; similar microbiome perturbations have been documented in ME/CFS and post-Lyme disease, suggesting this may be a shared failure mode across PAIS conditions.

## Limitations

1. **Narrative, not systematic review:** No formal systematic search protocol, PRISMA, or GRADE assessment described. Risk of selection bias in cited studies; unclear how conflicting evidence was resolved.

2. **Heterogeneous case definitions:** Studies cited use different Long COVID definitions (CDC, WHO, NICE, RECOVER), making quantitative comparisons across cited cohorts difficult. The review acknowledges global variation in phenotype but does not stratify findings by case definition.

3. **Causality cannot be established:** Most cited microbiome studies are observational (cross-sectional or longitudinal cohort). Whether gut dysbiosis is a driver, consequence, or bystander of Long COVID symptoms cannot be determined from this evidence base. The mechanistic pathways proposed (ACE2 → mTOR → dysbiosis → MGB → neuroinflammation) are plausible but insufficiently validated by interventional data in Long COVID specifically.

4. **Cohort size and demographic limitations:** Many cited primary studies have small cohorts (n=14, n=84, n=133). The populations are often geographically limited; the review notes global variation in Long COVID phenotype but many of the mechanistic microbiome studies are from East/South Asian cohorts (particularly Hong Kong) with uncertain generalisability.

5. **Intervention evidence is preliminary:** The probiotic and dietary data cited are largely from COVID-19 patients during acute infection, not from confirmed Long COVID patients. Standardised guidelines for probiotic use in Long COVID are explicitly acknowledged to be absent; adverse effects data are limited.

6. **No distinction between resolved vs. persisting dysbiosis:** The review does not clearly delineate whether patients who recover from Long COVID also show microbiome normalisation, which would be important evidence that dysbiosis is mechanistically relevant rather than merely co-occurring.

7. **Confounding by prior antibiotic use:** Antibiotic exposure during acute COVID-19 severely disrupts the microbiome, and the review notes elevated antibiotic resistance genes in Long COVID patients who received antibiotics. This confound is acknowledged but not addressed in the individual microbiome studies reviewed.

## Follow-up

**Papers to read/cross-reference:**
- Liu et al. 2022a — key primary study on microbiome composition changes at 3 and 6 months post-COVID in Long COVID vs. recovered patients (n=133); extensively cited here.
- Zhan et al. 2023 — 1-year post-COVID dysbiosis cohort (n=84); provides the 12-month SCFA-producer depletion data in Table 3.
- Gareau and Barrett 2023 — MGB axis signalling in Long COVID; cited as the mechanistic framework for the gut-brain connection.
- Zhang et al. 2022a — SCFA levels longitudinally in PASC; documents persistent SCFA reduction.
- Su et al. 2022a — immune profiling and autoantibody data in Long COVID (Klebsiella, autoantibodies, CD8+ cytotoxics).
- Komaroff2023 (this project) — cross-reference the MGB/neuroendocrine dysfunction described here with the broader ME/CFS/PAIS comparison in that paper.

**Questions this raises for the project:**
1. Is gut dysbiosis (specifically SCFA producer depletion) a shared feature across non-COVID PAIS conditions (ME/CFS, post-Lyme, post-Q-fever), and does it track with symptom severity in those conditions as it does in Long COVID?
2. Does microbiome restoration (via probiotics or dietary intervention) accelerate functional recovery metrics in Long COVID, and can the timing of intervention (acute vs. convalescent phase) predict efficacy?
3. Does the anti-ACE2 autoantibody response described here represent a self-sustaining loop (virus downregulates ACE2 → autoantibody further suppresses ACE2 → dysbiosis persists) that could be a targetable node for breaking the PAIS maintenance cycle?
