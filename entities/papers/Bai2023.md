# Posttreatment Lyme disease syndrome and myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome: A systematic review and comparison of pathogenesis

- **Authors:** Natalie A. Bai, Christie S. Richardson
- **Year:** 2023
- **Journal:** Chronic Diseases and Translational Medicine, vol. 9, no. 3, pp. 183–190
- **DOI:** 10.1002/cdt3.74
- **BibTeX key:** Bai2023
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This systematic review provides the first direct qualitative comparison of the documented symptom profiles of posttreatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS) and myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), using 18 published PTLDS studies (total N ranging from 10 to 280 per study). The central finding is that 26 of the 29 canonical ME/CFS symptoms were reported in at least one PTLDS study, and in the majority of PTLDS studies at least four of the six major ME/CFS diagnostic symptoms were present — including fatigue >6 months, reduced activity, and post-exertional malaise. The authors argue that PTLDS and ME/CFS have convergent, likely shared pathogenesis despite being triggered by different pathogens (Borrelia burgdorferi vs. diverse infections), positioning both as post-active-phase infection syndromes.

## Methods

- **Design:** Systematic review following PRISMA 2020 guidelines.
- **Databases:** PubMed and MEDLINE/Ovid searched through November 15, 2022.
- **Search terms:** "chronic Lyme disease" and "posttreatment Lyme disease" (both platforms); yielded 1415 / 327 and 231 / 50 results respectively across the two platforms.
- **Initial pool → final:** 2023 initial articles → 52 after deduplication and relevance filtering → 18 final articles after full-text review.
- **Inclusion criteria:** Patients with confirmed Lyme disease treated with antibiotics per standard of care; symptoms persisting ≥3 months post-treatment (ideally ≥6 months to align with ME/CFS inclusion criteria); PTLDS diagnosed by exclusion.
- **Exclusion criteria:** Articles not documenting individual patient symptoms; patients with antibiotic-refractory Lyme arthritis or late-stage Lyme disease; studies without antibiotic treatment.
- **Outcome measure:** Qualitative comparison of PTLDS symptom frequencies to the 29 canonical ME/CFS symptoms listed in Wong and Weitzer (2021) [@WongWeitzer2021] and the Institute of Medicine (IOM/NAM) case-definition criteria. A symptom chart format adapted from Lim and Son (2020) was used.
- **Quantitative data extracted:** Sample size, location, time since antibiotic treatment (range: >4 months to >12 months), methodology (questionnaires, interviews, chart review, neurological evaluation), percentage of patients reporting each symptom, and risk-of-bias assessment.

## Key Findings

**Included studies (Table 1)**
The 18 included studies spanned n = 10 (Novak et al.) to n = 280 (Berende et al.), almost all US-based (16/18), with two from Sweden and the Netherlands. Fifteen of the 18 studies documented symptoms lasting >6 months; the remainder required >3–4 months.

**Symptom prevalence across PTLDS studies**
- Fatigue / reduced daily activity: reported in 15/18 studies, typically in 64–95% of participants within each study.
- Neurocognitive symptoms (brain fog, memory difficulties, cognitive slowing): 15/18 studies; 53–90% of participants.
- Sleep difficulties: 15/18 studies; 33–77% of participants.
- Arthralgias / joint pain: 11/18 studies; 44–74% of participants.
- Myalgia: 11/18 studies; 43–80% of participants.
- Headaches: 11/18 studies; 20–70% of participants.
- Post-exertional malaise: documented in at least 3/18 studies; may be underreported because many studies coded it within fatigue/malaise.
- Paresthesias: reported in several studies (16–43%); noted as a symptom potentially unique to PTLDS vs. ME/CFS.
- In the Nilsson et al. study of 85 Swedish patients (median follow-up >6 months, survey/interview methodology), all participants satisfied the diagnostic criteria of ME/CFS if not for their documented PTLDS.
- In a Coyle et al. study of 12 patients (interview, >6 months), all 12 met ME/CFS criteria.

**Symptom overlap with ME/CFS (Table 2)**
Of the 29 canonical ME/CFS symptoms:
- 26/29 were reported in at least one PTLDS article.
- Three ME/CFS symptoms not reported in any PTLDS study: lymphadenopathy, susceptibility to viruses, food sensitivities.
- Three symptoms reported in PTLDS but classified as non-ME/CFS criteria: paresthesias, stiff neck, tremor.
- The most commonly overlapping symptoms (reported in ≥11 studies): fatigue/reduced activity, brain fog, sleep difficulties, arthralgia, myalgia, headaches.

**Shared biomarker signals**
The authors note (drawing on cited primary literature):
- Elevated C-reactive protein in both PTLDS and ME/CFS.
- Elevated IL-23 in ME/CFS (children) and in PTLDS patients.
- Elevated CCL19 in PTLDS.
- Anti-neural antibodies in PTLDS patients.
- Metabolic differences (glycerophospholipid, aromatic and branched-chain amino acid, carnitine, bile acid, fatty acid, sphingolipid metabolism) shared across both syndromes, documented by Fitzgerald et al. (PTLDS) and Germain et al. (ME/CFS).
- HLA-DR alleles linked to autoimmune diseases are more common in patients who develop antibiotic-refractory Lyme arthritis.

**Treatment context**
- Standard of care: 10 days Doxycycline (early stage) or 28-day IV Ceftriaxone (neurological manifestations); no further antibiotics recommended.
- Two RCTs published in NEJM (2001 and 2016) showed no significant benefit of additional antibiotics over placebo.
- No FDA-approved treatment or standardized diagnostic test exists for PTLDS; ~1 million US patients are estimated to be suffering.
- Improvement in severity of eight major PTLDS symptoms was shown with 6-month Dapsone combination treatment in a retrospective chart review of 200 patients (Horowitz & Freeman), but this remains investigational.

## Relevance

This paper directly addresses the project research question `research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes` by:

1. **Demonstrating symptom convergence across distinct infectious triggers.** PTLDS follows *Borrelia burgdorferi* infection; ME/CFS follows diverse infections (EBV, enteroviruses, Coxiella, and others). Yet 26/29 canonical ME/CFS symptoms emerge in PTLDS cohorts — a powerful empirical argument that the post-infectious failure mode is triggered-infection-agnostic and likely reflects a common downstream dysregulation rather than pathogen-specific pathology.

2. **Instantiating the "failed homeostatic recovery" frame.** PTLDS is defined as the persistence of symptoms after antibiotic treatment has cleared the acute infection — i.e., the homeostatic baseline is not restored despite pathogen elimination. The ~10% of appropriately treated Lyme patients who develop PTLDS are a direct instance of failed recovery from acute infection.

3. **Expanding the PAIS class beyond viruses.** Choutka et al. (2022) reviewed PAIS across pathogens but placed greater emphasis on viral syndromes. This paper adds a well-characterized bacterial trigger (Borrelia) with a defined treatment endpoint, strengthening the case that the shared failure mode operates across kingdoms of pathogens.

4. **Identifying ME/CFS diagnostic overlap as a quantifiable endpoint.** The finding that some PTLDS cohorts fully satisfy ME/CFS criteria (Nilsson et al.; Coyle et al.) supports using ME/CFS as a convergent phenotypic marker in multi-PAIS comparative analyses planned for this project.

5. **Flagging shared biomarker candidates** (IL-23, CCL19, CRP, anti-neural antibodies, shared metabolic signatures) as measurable correlates of the shared failure mode — directly relevant to the project's goal of identifying pan-PAIS biomarker signatures.

## Limitations

- **Qualitative design; no meta-analysis.** Symptom frequencies across studies cannot be pooled because study designs (questionnaires vs. interviews vs. chart review), patient selection, and time since treatment vary substantially. All comparisons are qualitative.
- **Small and homogeneous cohorts.** Fifty percent of included articles had N < 50; the majority of patients are White and of higher socioeconomic status (volunteered or were referred), likely introducing ascertainment bias.
- **Variable case definitions and time thresholds.** Inclusion varied from >3 months to >12 months post-treatment; some studies did not require the 6-month duration mandated by ME/CFS criteria, potentially including transient cases.
- **Post-exertional malaise likely underreported.** PEM is a defining ME/CFS criterion and is exertion-triggered; cross-sectional questionnaires that do not specifically probe for PEM will miss it by conflating it with baseline fatigue.
- **Selection bias / symptom exaggeration risk.** PTLDS carries medical stigma; patients seeking care may amplify symptom reporting. Studies relying on self-referral or clinician referral are not population-representative.
- **No prospective biomarker studies included.** The shared inflammatory/metabolic findings are drawn from separately published primary studies, not from the 18 included articles themselves; the review does not directly measure biomarkers.
- **ME/CFS comparison dataset is itself heterogeneous.** The comparison symptom list is drawn from a single prior review (Wong & Weitzer 2021) [@WongWeitzer2021], which itself compiled ME/CFS symptoms across variable case definitions (Fukuda 1994, Canadian Consensus, NAM 2015).
- **No control group within the review.** The studies document PTLDS patients only; direct comparison to healthy controls or to other PAIS cohorts is inferred, not measured.

## Follow-up

- Read Nilsson et al. (PLoS One 2021, ref 20 in this paper): the only study in which all PTLDS participants satisfied ME/CFS diagnostic criteria — examine methodology and cohort details.
- Read Fitzgerald et al. (Clin Infect Dis 2021): metabolic differences between healthy controls and PTLDS patients; compare to Germain et al. (ME/CFS metabolomics) for shared signatures.
- Examine Coyle et al. (ref 34): 12/12 PTLDS patients meeting ME/CFS criteria by interview — what were the specific ME/CFS criteria used?
- Follow the RCT literature on PTLDS treatment (NEJM 2001 / 2016 refs 22–23) to understand the null antibiotic result — this supports the hypothesis that continued Borrelia activity is not the primary driver of PTLDS, pointing toward immune dysregulation.
- Compare PTLDS biomarker panel (IL-23, CCL19, CRP, anti-neural Abs) to biomarker panels from long COVID and post-Q-fever ME/CFS cohorts for convergence.
- Assess whether PTLDS datasets (e.g., from the included studies) have publicly available individual-level data usable for computational PAIS comparisons.
