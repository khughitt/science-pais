# Mitochondrial Dysfunction in Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome

- **Authors:** Abu Mohammad Syed, Alexander K. Karius, Jin Ma, Ping-yuan Wang, Paul M. Hwang
- **Year:** 2025
- **Journal:** Physiology, vol. 40, pp. 319–328
- **DOI:** 10.1152/physiol.00056.2024
- **BibTeX key:** Syed2025
- **Source:** Full-text PDF (papers/pdfs/2025_Syed_mitochondrial-dysfunction-mecfs-review.pdf), read 2026-06-20.

## Key Contribution

This narrative review from the Hwang lab (NHLBI/NIH) synthesizes the evidence for mitochondrial dysfunction as a recurring pathophysiological theme in ME/CFS and extends it to long COVID, framing both conditions as disorders of energy production triggered by exertion. The review's primary mechanistic focus is the ER stress → WASF3 → mitochondrial respiratory supercomplex disruption axis identified in the lab's own prior work (Wang2023/Wang et al., PNAS 2023), positioning WASF3 as a candidate molecular mediator that bridges viral ER disruption, immune metabolic reprogramming, and impaired oxidative phosphorylation. The paper explicitly declines to resolve whether mitochondrial dysfunction is a primary cause or secondary consequence of ME/CFS, framing it instead as a tractable entry point into upstream etiology.

## Methods

This is a narrative review (not a systematic review or meta-analysis). It synthesizes:

- In vivo phosphorus-31 MRS studies of skeletal muscle during exercise recovery
- Plasma and blood metabolomics studies comparing ME/CFS patients to healthy controls
- Brain MRS studies reporting metabolite abnormalities
- PBMC and isolated immune-cell bioenergetics studies
- The Hwang lab's primary mechanistic study: fibroblasts from a fatigue patient, skeletal muscle biopsy cohort (Walitt et al. 2024, Nat Commun), and WASF3 transgenic mouse model
- Mitochondrial genome (mtDNA) sequencing studies
- Clinical trials of mitochondrial-targeting supplements (Table 1, 9 representative studies)

The review does not perform new data analysis. The underlying primary data for the WASF3 mechanism is Wang et al. 2023 (PNAS), which used patient fibroblasts, a well-characterized ME/CFS biopsy cohort, and transgenic mice — reviewed here without methodological detail.

Case definition note: The review does not specify which ME/CFS case definition(s) were used in the cited primary studies; cohort heterogeneity across cited studies is acknowledged but not modelled.

## Key Findings

**Evidence for mitochondrial dysfunction (pre-exertion / resting state):**
- Multiple 31P-MRS studies report lower ATP synthesis rates and increased intracellular acidosis in ME/CFS skeletal muscle during exercise recovery, consistent with excess glycolytic reliance.
- Decreased oxygen extraction during cardiopulmonary exercise testing implies mitochondrial deficiency not attributable to deconditioning.
- Plasma metabolomics and brain MRS reveal elevated lactate levels (ventricular CSF in CFS) and metabolite profiles consistent with impaired pyruvate dehydrogenase function and oxidative stress.
- PBMC bioenergetics and isolated neutrophil studies (n=138 in one study) document impaired mitochondrial ATP synthesis correlated with illness severity.
- Increased oxidative stress markers (lipid peroxidation products, protein carbonyls, oxidized DNA bases) and reduced CoQ10 levels reported across multiple studies.
- Peroxisomal dysfunction (plasma metabolomics) may compound mitochondrial ROS dysregulation.

**Direction-of-effect (resting vs. provoked):**
The review frames mitochondrial dysfunction as present at rest but worsened by exertion. PEM is interpreted bioenergetically as the consequence of rapid ATP depletion combined with slow regenerative capacity and increased oxidative stress — not merely deconditioning. Appelman et al. 2024 (Nat Commun) is cited to show that long COVID patients have an increased fraction of fatigue-prone glycolytic muscle fibers and that mitochondrial function worsens specifically with PEM. The review does not present a formal resting-vs-provoked comparison but the implicit model is: baseline mitochondrial compromise is present at rest; exertion exposes and acutely worsens it, producing PEM through slow energy regeneration and ROS accumulation.

**Primary vs. secondary defect:**
The review explicitly states this remains unresolved: "Whether mitochondrial dysfunction in ME/CFS, reported by numerous groups, is a cause or simply an effect of the underlying pathogenesis is currently unclear." The WASF3 pathway is presented as a mechanistic candidate that could be primary (if viral ER stress is upstream), but the paper is careful not to claim this is established.

**WASF3 / ER stress mechanism (core Hwang-lab contribution):**
- ER stress (induced by viral infection or other stimuli) upregulates WASF3 protein via a posttranscriptional mechanism.
- WASF3 localizes to mitochondria (posttranslationally phosphorylated) at ER–mitochondria contact sites and disrupts assembly of respiratory supercomplexes, specifically destabilizing Complex IV (CIV) subunits by interfering with SC III₂+IV formation.
- WASF3 acts independently of its canonical actin-polymerization (VCA domain) function — the COOH-terminal truncated form (lacking VCA) retains full mitochondrial inhibitory activity.
- In parallel, WASF3 overexpression promotes actin polymerization, which drives glycolysis, creating a dual metabolic switch: suppressed OxPhos + activated glycolysis.
- Transgenic mice overexpressing human WASF3 show significantly reduced exercise capacity and higher blood lactate vs. wild-type.
- Skeletal muscle biopsies from a well-characterized ME/CFS cohort (Walitt et al. 2024) show elevated WASF3 protein, elevated ER stress markers, and decreased CIV subunits vs. healthy volunteers, consistent with the mechanism.
- Elevated WASF3 activates p38 MAPK (possibly as a feedback signal for mitochondrial biogenesis) and NF-κB; p38 MAPK is proposed to mediate central fatigue via IFN-α induction.

**Immune–metabolism interface:**
- Mitochondria regulate immune cell function, particularly T-cell metabolic reprogramming; T-cell mitochondrial abnormalities have been reported in ME/CFS.
- WASF3's potential role in immune cells is unexplored but hypothesized: JAK2/STAT3 and NF-κB regulation of WASF3 suggests it could be part of an immune/host-defense metabolic switch that becomes pathologically sustained in ME/CFS. [SPECULATION — authors acknowledge this is speculative]
- WASF3 is highly expressed in brain tissue; disruption of WASF3 homeostasis is speculated to contribute to neuroinflammation. [SPECULATION]

**Long COVID parallel:**
- Long COVID shares the mitochondrial dysfunction pattern. SARS-CoV-2 viral persistence may sustain ER stress → WASF3 elevation, linking antigen persistence to bioenergetic impairment.
- Appelman et al. 2024 specifically documents mitochondrial impairment worsened by PEM in long COVID skeletal muscle.

**Therapeutic trials (Table 1 summary):**
- CoQ10 (±NADH, ±selenium), NADH, D-ribose, DCA, acetyl-L-carnitine, KPAX002, quercetin — all targeting mitochondrial function or oxidative stress.
- Results: modest, variable, and inconsistent across individuals; no treatment has significantly changed clinical management.
- Salubrinal (PP1 inhibitor; reduces ER stress → decreases WASF3 → restores mitochondrial function in patient cells) is highlighted as a mechanistically targeted candidate, but is not approved for human use and inhibits general protein translation.

**ME/CFS is not classified as a mitochondrial disease:** No specific mtDNA mutations or variants robustly explain the dysfunction; mtDNA haplogroups J, U, H and some SNPs associate with individual symptoms but without mechanistic clarity.

## Relevance

**question:0011-mitochondrial-basis-of-pem:** This is the most direct feed. The review synthesizes the multi-level evidence (31P-MRS, metabolomics, PBMC bioenergetics, muscle biopsy) that mitochondrial dysfunction underlies PEM specifically, and provides the current best mechanistic model: ER stress → WASF3 → supercomplex disruption → OxPhos failure → rapid ATP depletion and slow recovery after exertion. The unresolved primary-vs-secondary question is the central open issue the question is designed to probe.

**question:0001-shared-molecular-signature-across-triggers:** The ER stress → WASF3 axis is explicitly framed as a convergence point across viral triggers (EBV, SARS-CoV-2, others) — viral infections usurp the ER, causing ER stress that induces WASF3, regardless of the specific pathogen. This supports a shared post-infectious mechanism across ME/CFS and long COVID at the molecular level.

**hypothesis:0001-shared-dysregulated-attractor:** The WASF3-mediated metabolic switch (OxPhos suppression + glycolysis activation) sustained by persistent ER stress from unresolved antigen/viral presence is consistent with a stable dysregulated cellular-energy attractor state that is not self-correcting without external intervention.

**paper:Wang2023:** This review is the Hwang lab's own narrative synthesis of the primary findings published in Wang2023 (PNAS). It extends the WASF3 mechanism to immune cells (speculative), neuroinflammation (speculative), and therapeutic targeting (salubrinal). Reading Syed2025 alongside Wang2023 covers both the primary experimental evidence and the broader integrative framing.

**paper:Appelman2024:** Cited specifically for the long COVID PEM–mitochondrial worsening finding (glycolytic fiber shift + mitochondrial impairment worsened post-exertion), which is the strongest current evidence for the direction-of-effect: mitochondrial dysfunction is provoked/worsened by exercise, not merely present at baseline.

**paper:Naviaux2016:** The Naviaux metabolomics paper (hypometabolic signature in ME/CFS) is cited as one of the metabolic profiling studies (ref 31) supporting the evidence base reviewed here. Syed2025 does not add new metabolomics interpretation but situates the Naviaux findings within the broader mitochondrial-dysfunction frame.

**topic:biomarkers-and-objective-endpoints:** WASF3 protein levels, ER stress markers, and CIV subunit levels (from muscle biopsy) are proposed as potential biomarkers of treatment efficacy. Salubrinal's ability to decrease WASF3 and restore mitochondrial function in patient cells provides a candidate pharmacodynamic endpoint.

## Limitations

1. **Narrative review, not systematic:** No explicit search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or risk-of-bias assessment. The selection of cited studies may reflect the authors' prior work and perspective.

2. **Primary-vs-secondary causality unresolved:** The review explicitly acknowledges this but does not resolve it. The WASF3 mechanism, while compelling mechanistically, is demonstrated in fibroblasts and a muscle biopsy cohort — it does not establish whether ER stress / WASF3 elevation is upstream or a consequence of some other primary defect.

3. **Case definition heterogeneity not addressed:** The cited primary studies span multiple ME/CFS case definitions (Fukuda, CCC, ICC, IOM/SEID). The review does not stratify findings by definition or flag how definitional differences may explain inconsistencies (e.g., negative or contrasting mitochondrial findings in some studies).

4. **Single-center / single-group mechanistic data:** The WASF3 findings (Wang2023, the backbone of this review) come from one laboratory; independent replication of WASF3 elevation in ME/CFS skeletal muscle and its causal relationship to symptoms has not yet been published by independent groups.

5. **Biopsy cohort size not stated in this review:** The review references the Walitt et al. 2024 (Nat Commun) deep phenotyping cohort for the muscle biopsy WASF3 data but does not repeat cohort size here; readers must consult the primary paper for statistical power details.

6. **Therapeutic trial quality is low:** Table 1 includes open-label pilot studies and small RCTs (n=10–207). No treatment has produced reliable, clinically meaningful benefit. The salubrinal candidate is not approved for humans and broadly inhibits protein translation.

7. **Immune and neurological mechanistic claims are speculative:** The role of WASF3 in immune cells and its potential contribution to neuroinflammation are explicitly framed as hypotheses, not supported by primary data in this review.

8. **Sex, age, severity covariates:** These are not systematically addressed despite their known importance for ME/CFS phenotype. The epidemiological note that women are affected 3× more than men is stated but not mechanistically explored.

## Model / Tool Availability

This is a review paper; no new model, dataset, or computational tool is released. The WASF3 transgenic mouse model and patient fibroblast lines are described in the primary paper (Wang et al. 2023, PNAS). Salubrinal is a commercially available research compound (not approved for human use).

## Follow-up

- **Wang2023 (paper:Wang2023):** The primary experimental paper underpinning this review. Should be read for full mechanistic and statistical detail on the WASF3–ME/CFS link.
- **Appelman2024 (paper:Appelman2024):** The long COVID muscle biopsy paper (PEM worsens mitochondrial function) that provides the most direct evidence linking direction-of-effect to exertion.
- **Walitt et al. 2024 (Nat Commun 15:907):** The NIH deep-phenotyping ME/CFS cohort paper; provides the biopsy sample underlying the WASF3 muscle findings in this review. Consider adding as a separate entity.
- **Key open question:** Is WASF3 elevation measurable non-invasively (e.g., in plasma exosomes or PBMCs) and does it track with PEM severity or treatment response? This is the biomarker gap for question:0011.
- **Independent replication:** Has WASF3 elevation in ME/CFS skeletal muscle been confirmed by groups outside NIH/Hwang lab? A targeted literature check would establish replication status.
- **Salubrinal clinical path:** Warrayat et al. (Trends Mol Med, in press at time of publication) reviews salubrinal's therapeutic potential for ME/CFS and long COVID — worth tracking.
- **WASF3 in immune cells:** No primary data yet on WASF3 function in T cells, monocytes, or B cells in ME/CFS. This is the next logical experimental step flagged by the authors.
