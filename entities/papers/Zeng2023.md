# A systematic review and meta-analysis of long term physical and mental sequelae of COVID-19 pandemic: call for research priority and action

- **Authors:** Na Zeng, Yi-Miao Zhao, Wei Yan, et al. (23 authors; Peking University, University of Washington, Baylor College of Medicine, Imperial College London, and others)
- **Year:** 2023
- **Journal:** Molecular Psychiatry
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-022-01614-7
- **BibTeX key:** Zeng2023
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This is the largest and most wide-ranging systematic review and meta-analysis of long COVID sequelae to date (as of 2022), pooling 151 studies involving 1,285,407 participants from 32 countries. The study quantifies the pooled prevalence of physical and neuropsychiatric sequelae across seven organ systems up to 12 months post-discharge, and identifies demographic and clinical predictors of persistent sequelae. The central finding is that approximately half of COVID-19 survivors carry a burden of at least one long-term physical or mental sequela at 12 months post-infection.

## Methods

**Study design:** PRISMA-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis; registered on PROSPERO (CRD42021228197).

**Search strategy:** PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library searched through September 30, 2021; medRxiv searched for preprints. Terms: "COVID-19" and "sequelae" in title or abstract.

**Inclusion criteria:** Original observational research; follow-up studies reporting at least one physical symptom, laboratory, or imaging result from COVID-19 survivors; minimum 4-week follow-up after initial infection.

**Exclusion criteria:** Review articles, abstracts only, case reports, studies reporting only acute-phase symptoms, and single-patient case reports. Studies covering the acute phase (defined as within the first 22 days on average) were excluded.

**Quality assessment:** Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS); four independent investigators. High quality = >7 stars; medium = 5–7; low = <5. Among included studies, 12 (7.9%) were high quality, 124 (82.2%) medium, 15 (9.9%) low.

**Statistical synthesis:** Random-effects model when I² > 50% or chi-squared p < 0.05; fixed-effects otherwise. Subgroup analyses by follow-up duration (<180 vs. >180 days), acute infection severity (asymptomatic/community, mild/moderate hospitalized, severely/critically ill hospitalized), mean age (<60 vs. ≥60 years), sex proportion (male <50% vs. ≥50%), and income level (high vs. middle/low, World Bank criteria). Meta-regression on continuous covariates. Publication bias assessed via funnel plots and Egger's test. All analyses in R 4.0.3.

**Studies included:** 151 studies; 35 in China, 23 in Italy, 12 Spain, 9 UK, 4 France, 4 Germany, 4 India, and others. N = 110 prospective, 21 cross-sectional, 20 retrospective. Follow-up up to 12 months post-discharge.

## Key Findings

**Overall burden:**
- At least one sequela: 50.1% (95% CI 45.4–54.8), from 37 studies with 659,454 participants, up to 12 months follow-up.
- Sequelae prevalence declined over time: 56.0% at 1–3 months, 47.8% at 3–6 months, 37.8% at 6–12 months.

**Respiratory system (most affected):**
- Any objective respiratory finding: 55.6% (95% CI 46.8–64.2), 32 studies, 6,292 participants.
- Lung CT abnormalities: 56.9% (95% CI 46.2–67.3), including CT with ground-glass opacity 40.8%.
- Abnormal pulmonary function tests: 45.6% (95% CI 36.3–55.0); DLCO <80%: 42.3%.
- Subjective respiratory symptoms: 25.7% (95% CI 21.4–30.2), 75 studies, 550,319 participants.

**Generalized symptoms:**
- Fatigue: 28.3% (95% CI 23.9–32.9), 69 studies, 53,315 participants; ~one-third reported persistent fatigue within 18–40 months.
- Joint pain: 11.0%; body aches: 10.9%; myalgia: 8.2%.
- Any generalized symptom: 28.7% (95% CI 21.0–37.0), 76 studies, 502,540 participants.

**Psychiatric sequelae:**
- Any psychiatric symptom: 19.7% (95% CI 16.1–23.6), 59 studies, 737,901 participants.
- Depression: 18.3% (95% CI 13.3–23.8), 30 studies, 484,106 participants.
- PTSD: 17.9% (95% CI 11.6–25.3), 13 studies, 3,014 participants.
- Anxiety: 16.2% (95% CI 12.0–20.8), 32 studies, 721,541 participants.
- Sleep disturbance: 13.5% (95% CI 8.7–19.2), 34 studies, 731,679 participants.
- Prevalence at 6–12 months: anxiety 14.9%, depression 12.4%, sleep disturbance 12.3%.
- Mild/moderate anxiety was far more common (41.2%) than severe/very severe anxiety (6.0%).

**Neurological sequelae:**
- Any neurological symptom: 18.7% (95% CI 16.2–21.4), 68 studies, 738,430 participants.
- Cognitive deficits: 19.7% (95% CI 8.8–33.4).
- Memory impairment: 17.5% (95% CI 8.1–29.6).
- Loss of taste or smell: 15.7% (95% CI 6.6–27.8); olfactory alteration 15.1%; gustatory dysfunction 10.6%.
- Concentration impairment: 12.5%.

**Cardiovascular sequelae:** 14.8% (95% CI 10.0–20.3); palpitation most common (9.9%).

**Dermatological:** 10.2%; digestive: 9.6%.

**Re-positivity and readmission:** PCR re-positivity 9.9% (95% CI 6.1–14.4); readmission 13.6% (95% CI 7.3–21.5).

**Subgroup: Severity of acute infection**
- Higher severity → higher prevalence of all sequelae categories.
- Sleep disturbance: severely/critically ill 21.8% vs. asymptomatic/community 0.7%.
- Anxiety: more frequent in asymptomatic/community patients (28.5%) than in severely ill (12.9%), suggesting different mechanisms.

**Subgroup: Age**
- Older age (≥60 years): higher dizziness (73.7% vs. 6.8%, p < 0.01), concentration impairment.
- Younger adults (<60): higher olfactory dysfunction (17.4% vs. 8.4%, p = 0.04).

**Subgroup: Sex**
- Male ≥50%: higher PTSD (20.5% vs. 10.6%), concentration impairment (30.6% vs. 8.9%), cardiovascular symptoms.
- Female/lower male: higher anxiety, depression, sleep disturbance.

**Subgroup: Income level**
- High-income countries: higher prevalence of respiratory, generalized, and neurological symptoms.
- Middle/low-income: higher cognitive deficits (38.4% vs. 14.3%), likely reflecting access to neuroimaging and diagnosis rather than true prevalence differences.

**Subgroup: Follow-up duration**
- Longer follow-up consistently associated with lower prevalence across all symptom categories.
- Neurological symptoms declined from 22.0% (<3 months) to 22.5% (3–6 months) to 15.8% (6–12 months).
- Psychiatric symptoms: 23.6% (<3 months) to 20.1% (3–6 months) to 18.0% (6–12 months) — no significant trend across time points.

**Predictors and biomarkers (pre-illness, acute, convalescent):**
- Pre-illness: older age, male sex, pre-existing COPD, history of psychiatric illness, cigarette smoking.
- Acute illness: higher COVID-19 severity, peak CT pneumonia score, longer hospital stay, no oxygen supplementation (paradoxically associated with more sequelae, possibly selection), abnormal objective test results (lower albumin, higher CRP, higher D-dimer, higher glucose, higher ALT, lymphopenia), SARS-CoV-2 cytokines IL-6, IL-1b, TNF, IL-17 disrupting the blood-brain barrier.
- Convalescent: no oxygen supplementation, abnormal pulmonary function (especially DLCO), decreasing D-dimer, abnormal CMR findings, troponin-T elevation.

## Relevance

This paper is directly relevant to the project research question (research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes) and the PAIS frame in two ways:

1. **Prevalence and persistence of failed recovery:** The 50.1% rate of any sequela at up to 12 months quantifies the magnitude of homeostatic failure post-COVID at population scale. The temporal trend (56% at 1–3 months falling to 37.8% at 6–12 months) shows partial but incomplete recovery, exactly the arc predicted by the PAIS model of incomplete homeostatic restoration.

2. **Multi-system failure mode:** The simultaneous involvement of respiratory, neuropsychiatric, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and neurological systems in COVID-19 survivors parallels what is seen in ME/CFS and post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS), supporting the project hypothesis that PAIS reflects a shared, multi-system homeostatic failure rather than organ-specific residual damage. The persistence of psychiatric symptoms (no significant decline 3–12 months) even as neurological and physical symptoms partially resolve suggests that neuroinflammatory and psychosocial mechanisms sustain dysfunction independently of the initial viral burden.

3. **Predictors as homeostasis modulators:** Pre-existing conditions (COPD, psychiatric illness), acute inflammatory biomarkers (IL-6, CRP, D-dimer), and convalescent pulmonary function are all factors that modulate whether homeostasis is restored. This aligns with the project's focus on predicting who fails to recover and identifying intervention windows.

## Limitations

1. **High heterogeneity:** Most I² values >50% (many approaching 97–100%), driven by diverse case definitions, follow-up durations, and severity mixes. Pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution.
2. **Self-reported outcomes dominate:** Neuropsychiatric symptoms are almost entirely self-reported; objectively detected cardiovascular and pulmonary data are sparser. The objective cardiac inflammation evidence is very limited (only one study).
3. **No control groups in many studies:** Cannot definitively attribute self-reported long-term symptoms to COVID-19 rather than pandemic-era psychosocial stressors, concurrent illness, or recall bias.
4. **Literature cutoff September 2021:** Excludes Delta and Omicron variant-era data; vaccination status not incorporated. Omicron-era long COVID burden may differ substantially.
5. **China-dominant sample:** 35 of 151 studies from China; results may not generalize globally.
6. **Short follow-up bias:** Only 10 studies followed participants beyond 6 months from infection; most data reflect the <6-month window.
7. **Quality distribution:** 82.2% of studies were medium quality; low quality studies may inflate or deflate prevalence estimates.
8. **PCR re-positivity interpretation:** Whether 9.9% re-positivity represents true reinfection, viral persistence, or false-positive testing is unresolved.

## Follow-up

- Compare sequelae spectrum and prevalence rates with PTLDS (Bai2023) and post-dengue fatigue (Kalimuddin2022) to evaluate overlap supporting a shared PAIS failure mode.
- Examine whether the IL-6/IL-1b/TNF/IL-17 cytokine signature identified as predictor maps onto ME/CFS neuroinflammation literature (Bateman2023, Komaroff2023).
- Investigate the paradox of mild infection producing high anxiety prevalence (28.5%) — does this reflect subclinical neuroinflammation or psychosocial sensitization?
- Delta/Omicron era meta-analyses needed to update prevalence estimates; vaccination modulation of PAIS rates is an open gap.
- The finding that D-dimer decline during convalescence predicts sequelae warrants connection to microclot/endothelial dysfunction hypotheses.
