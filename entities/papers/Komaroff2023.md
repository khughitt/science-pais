# ME/CFS and Long COVID share similar symptoms and biological abnormalities: road map to the literature

- **Authors:** Anthony L. Komaroff, W. Ian Lipkin
- **Year:** 2023
- **Journal:** Frontiers in Medicine, vol. 10, p. 1187163
- **DOI:** 10.3389/fmed.2023.1187163
- **PMC:** PMC10278546
- **BibTeX key:** Komaroff2023
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This comprehensive review by Komaroff (Harvard/Brigham and Women's) and Lipkin (Columbia Center for Infection and Immunity) systematically compares the symptoms and objective biological abnormalities of ME/CFS and Long COVID across seven biological domains: central and autonomic nervous system, immune system and infectious agents, metabolism and redox balance, and cardiopulmonary/vascular function. The paper's central argument is that both illnesses are biologically real, share the majority of their symptoms and many of the same measurable pathophysiological abnormalities, and these shared abnormalities suggest common mechanisms — meaning advances in understanding one illness have direct implications for the other. The review serves as a "road map" to over 550 citations and proposes that ME/CFS and Long COVID, together with other post-infectious syndromes, may represent a conserved, multi-system "post-infection/post-injury syndrome" driven by stereotyped responses whose evolutionary analogs are dauer, hibernation, and torpor.

## Methods

This is a narrative review. The authors searched and synthesized the ME/CFS and Long COVID/PASC literature (predominately 2020–2023 for Long COVID, decades of prior literature for ME/CFS), organizing findings into seven parallel summary tables (Tables 4–7) that cite both "positive" studies (finding an abnormality) and "negative" studies (not finding it) to enable readers to judge evidence strength. Three broad PASC categories are distinguished in Table 1: (1) tissue injury to multiple organs visible on imaging; (2) new onset of major diseases (diabetes, cardiovascular disease — incidence increased 150–400% in first year post-COVID); (3) Long COVID proper — persistent symptoms not explained by major organ injury. The review uses the U.S. National Academy of Medicine 2015 case definition for ME/CFS (Table 2), which requires ≥6 months of substantially impaired function, profound fatigue, post-exertional malaise, unrefreshing sleep, and at least one of cognitive impairment or orthostatic intolerance.

## Key Findings

**Prevalence and burden**
- A meta-analysis of 57 studies (>250,000 people) found that impairing ongoing symptoms persisted in 43% of people with acute COVID-19 for at least 6 months.
- A study of ~100,000 cases plus matched never-infected controls found >40% of cases had persistent symptoms/impairment across all daily activities at 18 months.
- Among 6,000 hospitalized patients vs. uninfected matched controls, persistent symptoms were found in ~30% at least 2 years after acute infection.
- An estimated 13–45% of people with persistent debilitating Long COVID symptoms meet the NAM case definition for ME/CFS.
- ME/CFS affects up to 2.5 million people in the U.S., generating ~$17–24 billion in direct and indirect annual expenses. Post-COVID conditions may affect 65 million globally; the aggregate cost of Long COVID is projected at $3.7 trillion over 5 years.

**Symptom comparison (Table 3)**
- Most symptoms are shared: fatigue, post-exertional malaise, headaches, sleep disorder, impaired reasoning/memory/attention, secondary depression/anxiety, reduced activity, myalgia/arthralgia, muscle weakness, orthostatic intolerance, palpitations, breathlessness, nausea/diarrhea, chills/cough. Post-exertional malaise is the cardinal feature of ME/CFS and is also reported by the vast majority of people with Long COVID.
- Syndrome-specific symptoms: decreased smell/taste, rash, and hair loss are frequent in Long COVID but rarely in ME/CFS; painful lymph nodes, chemical sensitivities, and tinnitus are frequent in ME/CFS but rare in Long COVID.

**Central and autonomic nervous system (Table 4)**
- Cognitive deficits: objective psychometric deficits in attention and information-processing speed documented in both. One study estimated the measured cognitive deficit in Long COVID patients was equivalent to 10 years of aging. Cognitive impairment correlates with EEG and MRI abnormalities in both illnesses.
- Reduced cerebral blood flow (CBF): documented by SPECT, arterial spin labeling MRI, and transcranial Doppler in ME/CFS; reduced from time of acute infection, persisting ≥10 months in Long COVID.
- HPA axis down-regulation: reduced CRH, ACTH, and cortisol confirmed in ME/CFS by two meta-analyses; similar abnormalities reported in Long COVID.
- Brain hypometabolism: documented by PET and MR spectroscopy in multiple brain areas in ME/CFS; in Long COVID lasts 10–12 months, may be accompanied by amyloid-beta deposition.
- Autonomic dysfunction: confirmed by multiple testing modalities in ME/CFS (meta-analysis) and Long COVID; objective autonomic dysfunction is common in the first 6–12 months post-COVID, becoming less common by 24 months.
- MRI abnormalities: increased white matter signal intensity, reduced white matter volume (progressive in ME/CFS), reduced gray matter volume in ME/CFS; dynamic changes in gray matter in Long COVID (initial increase in limbic areas followed by atrophy in more severe cases).
- Small fiber neuropathy: positive biopsy studies in both illnesses.
- Neuroinflammation (PET/MRS/pathology): widespread activation of astrocytes and microglia in ME/CFS; positive studies in Long COVID.
- Autoantibodies against neural/autonomic targets: correlate with symptoms in ME/CFS (multiple studies); also found in Long COVID.

**Immune system and infectious agents (Table 5)**
- Cytokine dysregulation: increased pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1A, IL-17a, TNF-α) and certain "anti-inflammatory" cytokines (IL-1 receptor antagonist, IL-4, IL-13) in ME/CFS; similar cytokine elevations in Long COVID (IL-18, IL-6, TNF), though reduced levels of some cytokines/receptors also seen.
- T cell exhaustion: positive studies in both illnesses.
- Increased autoantibodies: multiple polymorphisms linked to autoimmunity, antinuclear antibodies, antiphospholipid antibodies, antineuronal antibodies, antiganglioside antibodies, antiserotonin antibodies in ME/CFS; autoantibodies to neural/autonomic targets also found in Long COVID.
- Reactivation of latent herpesviruses: positive studies in ME/CFS (EBV, HHV-6, others); EBV reactivation frequent in acute COVID-19, and HHV-6, HHV-7, CMV reactivation also documented; reactivation also found in Long COVID specifically.
- SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid/antigen persistence: evidence of viral reservoirs in multiple tissues (gut, lymph nodes, brain) in Long COVID.
- Gut microbiome dysbiosis: proinflammatory gut and oral microbiome with dysbiosis documented in both illnesses.
- Accelerated senescence: various markers of cellular senescence (including SASP and shortened telomere length) in both illnesses.

**Metabolism and redox balance (Table 6)**
- Reduced ATP generation from fatty acids: acylcarnitine levels and beta-oxidation impaired in ME/CFS; impairment of beta-oxidation of fatty acids at rest and during exercise in Long COVID.
- Reduced ATP from amino acids: reduced levels of multiple amino acids in ME/CFS; elevated taurine, glutamine/glutamate ratio, kynurenine/tryptophan ratio in Long COVID.
- Reduced ATP via TCA cycle and glycolysis: positive studies in both.
- Mitochondrial/oxidative phosphorylation abnormalities: positive studies in ME/CFS.
- Hypometabolic state: reduced metabolite levels in blood in ME/CFS; multiple reports of brain hypometabolism on imaging in Long COVID.
- Redox imbalance: increased peroxides/superoxides (correlating with symptom severity in ME/CFS); SARS-CoV-2 spike protein induces oxidative stress in microglial cells; an RCT of vitamin C improved Long COVID symptoms.
- Kynurenine pathway: L-tryptophan levels elevated in ME/CFS, reduced in Long COVID; kynurenine/tryptophan ratio inversely related — reduced in ME/CFS-like interferon-therapy state, increased in Long COVID.

**Cardiopulmonary and vascular abnormalities (Table 7)**
- Reduced peak VO2 on cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET): documented in ME/CFS, confirmed by several meta-analyses; worsened when CPET is repeated 24 hours later (a finding used as a diagnostic test). Also found in Long COVID.
- Reduced ventilatory efficiency (increased VE/VCO2 slope): positive studies in both.
- Endothelial dysfunction: well-documented in ME/CFS (macrovascular and microvascular); in Long COVID, microvascular injury is a cardinal feature; markers of accelerated angiogenesis accurately distinguish Long COVID from acute COVID and from healthy controls. Transcranial Doppler demonstrates impaired cerebral perfusion 300 days after initial COVID-19. An RCT of L-arginine + vitamin C directed at improving endothelial function significantly improved flow-mediated dilation and fatigue.
- Platelet abnormalities and coagulopathies: hyperactivated platelets and microclots in ME/CFS; fibrin amyloid microclots impairing microcirculation in Long COVID; differences in platelet-function genes.
- Reduced blood volume: positive studies in ME/CFS.
- Cardiac chamber volume reductions at rest: MRI-documented reduction in stroke volume and end-systolic/end-diastolic volumes in ME/CFS.

**Post-infection/post-injury syndrome framing**
The authors propose that ME/CFS and Long COVID — and similar syndromes following multiple infectious and non-infectious injuries (post-critical illness syndrome, heat stroke, major trauma) — represent a conserved, stereotyped multi-system response that evolved to facilitate recovery by metabolic reprogramming: reducing energy-consuming activities and redirecting energy stores to eradicate infection and heal tissue. The evolutionary analogs are dauer (C. elegans), hibernation, and torpor in higher animals, all of which involve "abnormal" innate immune responses, redox imbalance, increased glycolysis, decreased aerobic respiration, gut microbiome alteration, and autonomic nervous system orchestration — mirroring the emerging pathophysiology of ME/CFS and Long COVID.

## Relevance

This review is directly central to the project research question — "Why do some people fail to recover after acute infection, and what shared mechanisms link the post-acute infection syndromes?" — in several respects:

1. **Evidence for a shared failure mode:** The paper's central empirical claim is that ME/CFS and Long COVID share the same objective biological abnormalities across seven domains. This is the strongest published evidence base for the project's framing of PAIS as a convergent biological state rather than a collection of unrelated syndrome-specific pathologies.

2. **Mapping the failure mode mechanistically:** The parallel tables give the project a structured, evidence-graded map of exactly which homeostatic systems fail to recover: hypothalamic-pituitary axes, autonomic nervous system tone, cerebral perfusion, mitochondrial energy generation, redox balance, immune activation and exhaustion, vascular/endothelial function. Each of these represents a homeostatic system that in PAIS fails to return to pre-infection set points.

3. **The "conserved stereotyped response" hypothesis:** The dauer/hibernation/torpor framing explicitly positions PAIS not as random organ damage but as a dysregulated, evolutionarily conserved response — offering a testable mechanistic hypothesis that the project can use to organize future computational analyses. If the post-infectious state is a conserved attractor, transcriptomic and metabolomic signatures should reflect known dauer/torpor biology.

4. **Evidence asymmetry between ME/CFS and Long COVID:** The review identifies numerous ME/CFS findings not yet studied in Long COVID (prolactin axis, thyroid axis, CSF proteins, muscle bioenergetics, small-fiber neuropathy). These gaps constitute a prioritized reading list for the project and point to where ME/CFS research can directly inform Long COVID investigation.

5. **Bidirectional "vicious cycle" framing:** The observation that many abnormalities reinforce each other (autonomic dysfunction → reduced CBF → neuroinflammation → immune dysregulation → metabolic failure → worsened autonomic dysfunction) supports the project's hypothesis that PAIS represents a stable dysregulated attractor maintained by positive feedback — distinct from simple "damage" that would heal over time.

## Limitations

- Narrative review: no formal meta-analysis or systematic search protocol reported; inclusion criteria for studies are not explicit. Evidence quality varies widely across the hundreds of cited studies.
- The review compares ME/CFS (studied over decades, heterogeneous case definitions, varying durations) with Long COVID (studied for only ~3 years at time of writing, evolving case definitions, predominantly post-SARS-CoV-2). Temporal mismatch in evidence maturity means the two illness literatures are not symmetrically developed.
- "Positive" and "negative" citations in the tables are presented without formal quality weighting; a small high-quality negative study and a large positive study are both listed as "negative"/"positive" respectively.
- Causal directionality among the seven domains is largely unresolved; the paper acknowledges bidirectional interactions but cannot assign a primary driver.
- The dauer/hibernation/torpor evolutionary hypothesis, while provocative and testable, is speculative; the paper explicitly labels it as such.
- No new data are presented; the paper synthesizes and frames rather than testing any mechanism.
- Case definition heterogeneity: 13–45% of Long COVID patients meeting ME/CFS criteria is a very wide range, reflecting instability in both Long COVID operationalization and ME/CFS diagnostic criteria across the cited studies.
- The review does not address pediatric PAIS, which may have distinct biology and epidemiology.

## Follow-up

- The parallel evidence tables (Tables 4–7) constitute a prioritized reading list: ME/CFS findings not yet studied in Long COVID (HPA prolactin axis, thyroid axis, CSF protein profiles, small-fiber neuropathy biopsies, muscle bioenergetics, microRNA profiles, methylome changes) represent the highest-yield ME/CFS-to-Long COVID inference targets.
- The dauer/hibernation/torpor hypothesis should be evaluated computationally: do available ME/CFS or Long COVID transcriptomic datasets show enrichment for known dauer/torpor gene signatures?
- The "vicious cycle" framing warrants a causal diagram: map the bidirectional reinforcing loops (autonomic → CBF → neuroinflammation → immune → metabolic → autonomic) to identify which nodes, if interrupted, would most efficiently resolve the attractor.
- Compare with Choutka 2022 (already in project): that paper frames the four PAIS mechanisms; Komaroff 2023 provides the detailed mechanistic evidence base across two major PAIS conditions. Together they form the conceptual backbone for this project.
- The RCT data (vitamin C in Long COVID redox; L-arginine + vitamin C for endothelial function) are modest but point to interventional implications worth tracking as the trial literature matures.
- Investigate whether the accelerated senescence (SASP) finding in both illnesses is causally linked to the persistent low-grade cytokine elevation — senolytic interventions are an emerging therapeutic hypothesis.
