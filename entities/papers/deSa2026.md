# A causal link between autoantibodies and neurological symptoms in long COVID

- **Authors:** Keyla Santos Guedes de Sá, Julio Silva, Rafael Bayarri-Olmos, et al. (Akiko Iwasaki lab, Yale; David Putrino lab, Mount Sinai)
- **Year:** 2026
- **Journal:** Cell
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.cell.2026.04.042
- **PMID:** 42208499
- **BibTeX key:** deSa2026
- **Tier:** Core now
- **Source:** PDF (medRxiv preprint OA copy via Unpaywall; preprint doi:10.1101/2024.06.18.24309100 — note the Cell publication is the peer-reviewed version of this preprint)

## Key Contribution

This paper provides the first direct experimental (causal) demonstration that IgG autoantibodies (AABs) purified from long COVID (LC) patients are sufficient to recapitulate neurological symptoms in healthy mice. Using a passive IgG-transfer model — injecting purified total IgG intraperitoneally into C57BL/6 female mice — the authors show that LC patient antibodies alone can induce heightened pain sensitivity, loss of balance and coordination, and muscle weakness in a donor-symptom-matched pattern. This elevates the autoantibody hypothesis for LC from association to in-vivo functional causation, and identifies MED20 and USP5 as candidate autoantigens. The study also provides mechanistic evidence for small fiber neuropathy (SFN) as one downstream effector pathway.

**Scope note (for cross-pathogen track):** This is a single-trigger study (SARS-CoV-2 / long COVID only). It establishes causality on the autoimmunity axis for LC, which is strong evidence in the context of hypothesis:0001 and question:0009, but does not by itself demonstrate a shared autoantibody signature across other PAIS triggers. That cross-trigger inference requires parallel evidence from ME/CFS, PTLDS, post-dengue, etc.

## Methods

**Cohort:** Mount Sinai–Yale Long COVID (MY-LC) cohort. Study subset: n=55 LC participants with high neurological symptom burden, n=42 convalescent controls (CC), n=39 healthy controls (HC). LC case definition: persistent symptoms >6 weeks post-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, evaluated by clinicians to rule out alternative etiologies. Predominantly female (average age 46.3 years); 63.6% had five or more neurological symptoms; 52.7% had mild LC by propensity score. No participants had a known autoimmune diagnosis at enrollment.

**IgG purification:** Total IgG purified from serum by Protein-G Sepharose bead chromatography; purity and homogeneity confirmed by FPLC size-exclusion chromatography.

**Immunofluorescence (IF) tissue reactivity:** Purified IgG applied to a panel of human and mouse tissues including pons, sciatic nerve, spinal cord, meninges, cerebellum, and a broad set of non-neural tissues. Meninges co-stained with NG2-dsRED (pericyte marker) and CD31 (endothelial marker) to characterize binding specificity.

**Autoantigen discovery (HuProt microarray):** Plasma screened on HuProt human proteome microarray (>21,000 full-length human proteins; CDI Laboratories). Positive hits defined at >3× mean of controls by negative binomial regression with longlink function. Top hits (MED20, USP5) validated orthogonally by (1) autoantigen pull-down from mouse brain homogenate followed by DIA mass spectrometry, and (2) ELISA at two concentrations (25 µg/mL and 10 µg/mL IgG).

**Passive transfer model (causal arm):** C57BL/6J female mice (6–8 weeks old) received a single intraperitoneal injection of 38 mg/kg total IgG from HC, CC, or LC donors (one mouse per donor). IgG BBB penetrance confirmed by ELISA of brain homogenate at day 5 (~5% of injected IgG crossed the BBB). Behavioral battery:
- Hot plate test (day 3): thermal nociception / pain sensitivity
- Grip strength test (day 3): muscle weakness
- Rotarod test (day 2): motor coordination / balance
- Open field + elevated zero maze: anxiety and locomotion
- Blood pressure / heart rate (CODA tail-cuff): autonomic function

**Small fiber neuropathy readout:** Scn10a-Cre::tdTomato reporter mice used for two-photon imaging; quantified intraepidermal nerve fiber (IENF) volume and density after passive transfer.

**Statistical analysis:** One-way ANOVA with Tukey correction for multiple groups; Student's t-test for two-group comparisons; Chi-square for frequency comparisons. Symptom-stratified analyses used donor-reported symptom phenotype as grouping variable. GraphPad PRISM 10.2.3.

## Key Findings

**IgG cross-reactivity with neural tissues:**
- LC patient IgG showed significantly increased immunofluorescence reactivity against human pons, mouse sciatic nerve, mouse meninges, and mouse cerebellum relative to HC and CC.
- Reactivity was neural-tissue selective: no significant staining of a broad panel of non-neural tissues (adrenal gland, breast, liver, kidney, etc.).
- Meningeal staining co-localized predominantly with pericytes (NG2-dsRED+) and to a lesser degree endothelial cells (CD31+), radiating into surrounding tissue.

**Symptom correlations (IF):**
- Meningeal AAB reactivity correlated with headache, memory loss, weakness, and disorientation.
- Sciatic nerve reactivity showed a trend toward association with tinnitus (not significant after correction).

**Autoantigen landscape (HuProt):**
- LC participants had significantly more autoreactive antibody hits than controls.
- Unsupervised clustering identified a LC-enriched cluster with increased reactivity to neurological proteins.
- Top validated autoantigens: **MED20** (Mediator Complex subunit; family members MED12/MED23 linked to neurological disease) and **USP5** (ubiquitin-specific protease; deubiquitinase that stabilizes Cav3.2 T-type calcium channels in nociceptive neurons — knockdown induces analgesia in mice).
- Autoantigen diversity is high: MED20 and USP5 reactivity found in a subset of LC patients only, consistent with multiple LC endotypes.

**Passive transfer — causal evidence:**
- **Pain (hot plate):** 85% of mice showing the pain phenotype received IgG from LC participants who self-reported new-onset pain since LC diagnosis (Fig. 4D–E). Hot plate phenotype also associated with donor-reported pins-and-needles and burning pain, and with dysautonomia (90% of mice positive for pain received IgG from dysautonomia-reporting donors).
- **Motor coordination (rotarod):** 88.88% of mice showing loss of balance/coordination received IgG from LC donors who reported dizziness (Fig. 4O).
- **Muscle weakness (grip strength):** Trend toward association with tinnitus (54.5%) and headache (90.9%) in donor (Figs. 4L–M).
- **Small fiber neuropathy:** LC IgG transfer reduced intraepidermal nerve fiber (IENF) volume and fiber count in Scn10a-Cre::tdTomato reporter mice relative to HC IgG controls — consistent with SFN as a mechanism for chronic pain and burning/pins-and-needles symptoms.
- **Negative findings:** Open field, elevated zero maze, and blood pressure/heart rate measurements did not significantly differ across groups, indicating the autonomic cardiovascular axis was not recapitulated in this short-term model.

**Overall conclusion:** Passive total IgG transfer from LC donors reproduces neurological phenotypes (pain, balance/coordination loss, muscle weakness) in a donor-symptom-matched pattern, demonstrating that IgG AABs are sufficient to cause these symptoms.

## Relevance

This paper is a landmark result for the **autoimmunity axis** of the PAIS signature matrix. It is directly relevant to:

- **hypothesis:0001 (shared dysregulated attractor):** Establishes that autoantibody-mediated neuro-inflammation is a bona fide driver of LC phenotype, not merely an epiphenomenon. Strengthens the mechanistic plausibility of the autoimmunity cell of the attractor model.
- **hypothesis:0009 / question:0009 (functional autoantibodies → dysautonomia and neurological symptoms):** Provides direct in-vivo functional causal evidence that LC IgG drives nociceptive, motor, and (partially) autonomic phenotypes. The donor-symptom matching (85–90% concordance for pain/dizziness phenotypes) is particularly strong — it links the antibody source to the recipient phenotype at the individual level, going beyond group-level statistics.
- **question:0001 (shared molecular signature across triggers):** This paper is a **single-trigger** study (SARS-CoV-2 / long COVID only). It does not test whether similar functional autoantibodies exist in ME/CFS, PTLDS, post-dengue, or other PAIS. Its cross-pathogen significance is indirect: if AAB-mediated neurological disease is a mechanism in LC, it becomes a candidate mechanism to look for in parallel PAIS, but the shared signature hypothesis requires orthogonal data from those other conditions. This study provides the LC anchor point for that comparison.

**Position in the cross-pathogen track (t001):** Strong evidence that functional autoantibodies are a causal neurological mechanism in LC; does not by itself establish cross-trigger sharing. Pair with studies showing functional AABs in ME/CFS (e.g., anti-GPCR work), PTLDS, or post-Q-fever to test hypothesis:0001 across triggers.

**Therapeutic implications noted by authors:** IVIg (neutralizes circulating AABs), FcRn inhibitors, plasmapheresis, anti-CD20 (rituximab), and CAR-T against antibody-secreting cells are all identified as candidate interventions for the AAB-driven LC endotype. Biomarker development (optimized autoantigen panel for diagnosis) is flagged as urgent.

## Limitations

1. **Preprint version accessed:** The PDF retrieved via Unpaywall is the medRxiv preprint (June 2024), not the final Cell peer-reviewed article (2026). The Cell version may contain additional data, revised analyses, or peer-review-mandated changes not reflected here. Methods and numbers should be treated as from the preprint unless the final article is consulted.
2. **Passive transfer of total IgG, not specific AABs:** Transfer uses bulk purified IgG, not affinity-purified antigen-specific fractions. The specific antibody(ies) responsible for each phenotype are not isolated. This limits mechanistic specificity — the effector AABs and their targets are inferred, not proven for each phenotype.
3. **Short observation window:** Behavioral phenotypes assessed at days 2–3 post-injection; longer-term consequences and reversibility are unknown.
4. **Autoantigen completeness:** The study does not identify the full complement of autoantigens sufficient to explain all phenotypes. MED20 and USP5 are validated hits in a subset of patients; the majority of causal autoantigens remain unknown.
5. **No cardiovascular autonomic recapitulation:** Blood pressure, heart rate, and anxiety/locomotion measures were not significantly affected in mice, despite dysautonomia being a major LC symptom. The passive transfer model may not capture cardiovascular autonomic dysregulation in a short time frame.
6. **Single sex (female mice):** All passive transfer experiments used female C57BL/6 mice. Sex-specific effects on autoantibody pathogenicity are not addressed, though the LC cohort itself was predominantly female.
7. **Cohort design:** Cross-sectional at enrollment; no pre-infection baseline samples available. LC case definition relies on clinician exclusion of alternative etiologies; no biomarker-confirmed controls for active infection or alternative autoimmune disease at enrollment.
8. **BBB penetrance is partial:** Only ~5% of injected IgG crossed the BBB at day 5. Central vs. peripheral contributions to the behavioral phenotypes are not cleanly separated.
9. **Endotype heterogeneity:** High diversity in AAB targets across patients; MED20/USP5 reactivity present in only a subset. The model describes one LC endotype, not LC as a whole.

## Model / Tool Availability

None reported. HuProt microarray is a commercial platform (CDI Laboratories). GraphPad PRISM 10.2.3 used for statistics. No code, data repository, or computational model released as of the preprint version.

## Follow-up

- Identify whether functional AABs against neural targets have been demonstrated in other PAIS (ME/CFS, PTLDS, post-dengue) using comparable passive transfer designs — needed to move from "LC mechanism" to "shared PAIS mechanism" (question:0001).
- Determine whether the USP5 / Cav3.2 nociceptor pathway is the principal effector of the pain phenotype — could be tested by depletion of anti-USP5 IgG fraction prior to transfer.
- Assess whether cardiovascular autonomic phenotypes (POTS-like) can be recapitulated with longer observation windows or by targeting the GPCR-directed AAB subset separately.
- Track whether the Cell (2026) peer-reviewed version introduces substantive changes vs. the preprint used here; update if so.
- Consider pairing with anti-GPCR autoantibody studies (Wallukat et al. 2021; Szewczykowski et al. 2022) for the dysautonomia–autoantibody intersection (question:0009).
