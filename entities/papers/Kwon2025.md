# Cytokine profiles associated with persisting symptoms of post-acute sequelae of COVID-19

- **Authors:** Ji-Soo Kwon, Euijin Chang, Hyeon Mu Jang, Ji Yeun Kim, Woori Kim, Ju Yeon Son, Junho Cha, Choi Young Jang, Seongman Bae, Jiwon Jung, Min Jae Kim, Yong Pil Chong, Sang-Oh Lee, Sang-Ho Choi, Yang Soo Kim, Sung-Han Kim
- **Year:** 2025
- **Journal:** Korean Journal of Internal Medicine
- **Volume/Pages:** 40:667–675
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.3904/kjim.2024.217
- **BibTeX key:** Kwon2025
- **Source:** Full-text PDF (papers/pdfs/2025_Kwon_cytokine-profiles-persisting-pasc-symptoms.pdf), read 2026-06-20.

## Key Contribution

This prospective single-centre study from Asan Medical Center (Seoul, Korea) characterised plasma cytokine profiles associated with 16 specific persisting PASC symptoms across 79 COVID-19-recovered adults sampled at 1, 3, and 6 months post-diagnosis. By clustering symptoms according to shared cytokine elevation patterns rather than traditional organ-system categories, the authors demonstrate that multiple symptom categories — including gastrointestinal symptoms, post-exertional malaise (PEM), fatigue, brain fog, chest pain, smell/taste change, palpitation, and abnormal movement — co-elevate a core set of inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, VEGF, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, MIG/CXCL9, MCP-1, granzyme A, CX3CL1), suggesting shared pathophysiological mechanisms centred on T-cell recruitment, thrombosis, and increased vascular permeability. Importantly, anti-spike S1-IgG titres were not associated with any individual symptom, whereas cytokine profiles differed markedly across symptoms, implicating ongoing immune/inflammatory activity rather than humoral antibody burden as the driver of heterogeneous PASC presentations.

## Methods

**Study design:** Prospective observational cohort.

**Setting and funding:** Department of Infectious Diseases, Asan Medical Center, University of Ulsan College of Medicine, Seoul, Korea. Funded by the Korea Health Technology R&D Project / Korea Health Industry Development Institute (KHIDI) under NIH Republic of Korea grant HD22C2045.

**Participants:** 79 adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with COVID-19 between August 2022 and September 2023 (predominantly Omicron sub-variants: BA.5 27.8%, BA.2.75 21.6%, XBB 45.6%). Mean age 57.9 ± 13.5 years; 59.5% male. 41/79 (51.9%) met PASC criteria; 38/79 (48.1%) non-PASC. High comorbidity burden: diabetes 36.4%, cardiovascular disease 50.6%, malignancy 26.0%, transplant 35.1%. COVID-19 severity at diagnosis: asymptomatic/presymptomatic 9.9%, mild 46.5%, moderate 19.7%, severe 12.7%, critical 11.3%.

**Case definition:** PASC defined by PASC Index ≥ 12, using a scoring instrument developed by Thaweethai et al. (RECOVER Consortium, JAMA 2023). This is a symptom-burden composite score rather than a symptom-persistence or symptom-presence criterion, and applies to adults with prior confirmed COVID-19 diagnosis.

**Timepoints:** Blood sampling and symptom questionnaire conducted concurrently at clinic visits scheduled at 1, 3, and 6 months post-COVID-19 diagnosis. Total 156 paired blood-symptom records analysed (not all 79 participants contributed records at every timepoint).

**Immunological assays:**
- 33-plex cytokine multiplex (cytometric bead array, BD Bioscience): IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL-10, IL-12p40, IL-12p70, IL-13, IL-17A, IL-21, granzyme A, granzyme B, MIG (CXCL9), IP-10 (CXCL10), I-TAC (CXCL11), TNF-α, sCD40L, CX3CL1, FasL, MCP-1, MIP-1α, RANTES, G-CSF, GM-CSF, VEGF, bFGF. Data acquisition via FACS Diva / FlowJo.
- SARS-CoV-2 spike subunit 1-specific IgG (S1-IgG): in-house ELISA, reported in IU/mL per WHO international standards.

**Statistical analyses:** Fisher's exact test or χ² test for categorical variables; Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Two-tailed p < 0.05 = significant. GraphPad Prism 9.4.1. Symptoms clustered post hoc by inspection of cytokine expression heatmap (Fig. 2); no formal clustering algorithm is described.

**Symptom survey:** Questionnaire covering 16 representative PASC symptoms drawn from Thaweethai et al. (2023): smell/taste change, PEM, chronic cough, brain fog, thirst, chest pain, dyspnea, palpitation, fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, changes in sexual desire/capacity, gastrointestinal symptoms, dizziness, hair loss, abnormal movement.

## Key Findings

**Symptom prevalence:** Most frequent symptom among all participants with symptoms was fatigue (65.7%), followed by PEM (53.7%), chronic cough (41.9%), thirst (41.5%), brain fog (40.3%), and chest pain (40.0%). Median of 4 symptoms (IQR 2–7) per symptomatic individual. ~20% retained more than 8 symptoms at 6 months.

**Symptom trajectory over time:** Cardiopulmonary symptoms (PEM, chronic cough, chest pain) showed significant decline over 1–6 months (p = 0.003, 0.098, 0.061 respectively). Fatigue (p = 0.083) and most other symptoms did not show significant temporal change over 6 months, indicating persistence.

**Antibody levels:** S1-IgG titres showed no statistically significant difference between participants with vs. without any of the 16 symptoms tested (Supplementary Fig. 1). Humoral response is dissociated from symptom burden.

**Cytokine-symptom associations — Symptom Cluster 1 (the inflammatory cluster):**
Symptoms: gastrointestinal, chest pain, PEM, smell/taste change, fatigue, brain fog, abnormal movement, palpitation.
These symptom contexts were accompanied by significantly elevated:
- **IL-10** (p < 0.001 across gastrointestinal, brain fog, fatigue, chest pain, PEM, smell/taste change, palpitation contexts)
- **VEGF** (p < 0.001 to 0.05 across gastrointestinal, brain fog, fatigue, chest pain, PEM)
- **IL-1β** (p < 0.05 across PEM, chest pain, gastrointestinal)
- **IL-6** (p < 0.05 for smell/taste change; trend for chest pain and gastrointestinal)
- **IL-8** (p < 0.05 for PEM, brain fog, smell/taste change)
- **MIG/CXCL9** (p < 0.05 for smell/taste change, chest pain, MIG shown in Fig. 2 matrix; abnormal movement)
- **MCP-1** (p < 0.05 for gastrointestinal, chest pain)
- **Granzyme A** (p < 0.05 for gastrointestinal)
- **CX3CL1** (p < 0.05 for brain fog, palpitation)
- **sCD40L** (elevated for cognitive dysfunction and brain fog contexts)
- **FasL, I-TAC, IFN-α** elevated for PEM and fatigue (FasL and I-TAC: p = 0.003 and 0.0003 for PEM; IFN-α: p < 0.0001 for PEM and fatigue)

Consistent IL-10 and VEGF elevation was observed across many symptoms in cluster 1. IL-10, VEGF, and VEGF + IL-1β patterns are consistently present across these symptoms.

**Symptom Cluster 2 (thirst, cognitive dysfunction, changes in sexual capacity):** Did not show significant elevation of the cluster 1 inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, VEGF, IL-1β). Associated instead with differences in other cytokines (sCD40L for cognitive dysfunction and thirst; G-CSF for thirst; RANTES for cognitive dysfunction). Distinct mechanism implied.

**Symptom Cluster 3 (chronic cough, dizziness, dyspnea, hair loss):** No significant differences in cytokine levels relative to their absence. These symptoms may be driven by non-cytokine mechanisms.

**Gastrointestinal symptoms show the widest cytokine perturbation:** Elevated IL-10, VEGF, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, MIG, MCP-1, granzyme A, IL-9 levels all significantly higher vs. those without gastrointestinal symptoms (p < 0.05).

**IL-6 specifically:** Significantly elevated for smell/taste change (p < 0.05 in Figure 2 matrix); shown elevated for gastrointestinal and chest pain contexts in Supplementary Fig. 2, though with less consistent statistical significance across symptom categories than IL-10 or VEGF. IL-6 is part of the shared inflammatory signature but is not the sole or dominant driver in this dataset.

**Authors' mechanistic interpretation:** Elevated VEGF (vascular permeability factor) + sCD40L (T-cell/B-cell/platelet co-stimulatory molecule) + CX3CL1 and RANTES (ligands for CX3CR1 and CCR5 on monocytes) suggests vessel damage, T-cell recruitment, and thrombotic/inflammatory vascular pathology as cross-cutting mechanisms. This converges with prior reports on VEGF and sCD40L from activated platelets interacting with CX3CR1+CCR5+ monocytes.

**IL-10 interpretation:** Authors note IL-10's dual role — both pro-inflammatory (in pathological contexts) and anti-inflammatory — and cite conflicting data from other PASC cohorts (some showing elevated IL-10, one showing diminished IL-10). They regard IL-10 as playing a pro-inflammatory role in both acute COVID-19 and recovery phases, consistent with their previous severe COVID-19 work.

## Relevance

**question:0006 (JAK-STAT / IL-6 driver vs marker):** IL-6 is elevated in association with specific symptoms (smell/taste change, gastrointestinal, chest pain) but is not the most consistently elevated cytokine across the full PASC symptom spectrum in this cohort. IL-10 and VEGF show broader elevation across cluster 1 symptoms. This evidence supports IL-6 as one member of a broader shared inflammatory signature rather than the singular driver. The study does not resolve whether IL-6 elevation is causal or a downstream marker; tocilizumab use was higher in the PASC group (p = 0.019 in Supplementary Table 1), but the study is not powered to address treatment effects.

**question:0001 (shared molecular signature across PAIS triggers):** This study confirms that diverse PASC symptoms — spanning gastrointestinal, neurological (brain fog), cardiopulmonary (PEM, chest pain), and sensory (smell/taste) domains — share a core elevated cytokine set (IL-10, VEGF, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, MIG, MCP-1, granzyme A, CX3CL1). This empirically grounds the shared-failure-mode frame within PASC itself, though it does not extend to other PAIS triggers.

**hypothesis:0003 (immune exhaustion feedback):** Granzyme A (cytotoxic T/NK marker) and FasL, I-TAC, IFN-α elevations in PEM and fatigue contexts are consistent with ongoing cytotoxic T-cell activity and exhaustion-proximal signalling. The maintained elevation of multiple inflammatory mediators 6 months post-infection without SARS-CoV-2 antibody signal supports persistent immune activation rather than acute-phase spillover.

**hypothesis:0001 (shared dysregulated attractor):** The symptom clustering result — showing that symptoms traditionally classified into distinct organ systems share cytokine elevation patterns — is directly supportive of the shared attractor hypothesis: different clinical presentations may represent the same underlying immune-inflammatory state, not distinct pathologies.

**topic:long-covid-immune-dysregulation:** This paper adds prospective, symptom-linked cytokine data from an Omicron-era Korean tertiary-care cohort (high comorbidity load), extending the immune dysregulation evidence base beyond Western/North American cohorts. The FasL and I-TAC (CXCL11) elevation for PEM and fatigue — previously less consistently reported in PASC — warrants follow-up attention.

**topic:biomarkers-and-objective-endpoints:** The 33-plex multiplex panel is a candidate objective biomarker framework for PASC stratification by symptom cluster. The absence of S1-IgG association with any symptom reinforces that humoral titres alone are not useful PASC symptom biomarkers.

## Limitations

1. **Small cohort (n = 79, 156 paired records):** Underpowered for subgroup analyses (e.g., by sex, severity, variant) and for detecting modest cytokine-symptom associations after multiple testing correction. No formal correction for multiple comparisons across 33 analytes × 16 symptoms is described.

2. **High-comorbidity tertiary-care sample:** Cardiovascular disease (50.6%), malignancy (26.0%), and transplant (35.1%) prevalence is much higher than general population PASC cohorts; comorbidities independently alter cytokine baselines and may confound PASC-symptom cytokine associations. Findings may not generalise to milder, community-acquired PASC.

3. **Omicron-era only:** All participants infected with BA.5, BA.2.75, or XBB; risk and cytokine patterns may differ from wild-type or delta PASC, limiting generalisability to earlier pandemic cohorts.

4. **Cross-sectional per timepoint:** The 156 records come from a maximum of 3 visits per participant; not all 79 contributed all visits. Longitudinal within-person cytokine dynamics are not fully characterised, and causal direction (cytokines drive symptoms vs. symptoms reflect ongoing inflammation) cannot be determined.

5. **No cellular source attribution:** The study does not examine which cell types produce the elevated cytokines. Authors acknowledge that monocytes and SARS-CoV-2-specific T cells are likely important but this was not measured.

6. **Symptom self-report:** PASC symptom status assessed by questionnaire, not clinician examination or objective testing (e.g., cardiopulmonary exercise testing for PEM). Symptom clustering is post hoc and descriptive, not derived from a pre-specified algorithmic clustering method.

7. **PASC definition specificity:** The Thaweethai PASC Index was developed in a North American RECOVER cohort; its validity for Korean adults with different baseline comorbidities, variant exposure, and healthcare access is not validated here.

8. **Cytokine causality unknown:** IL-6 and other elevated cytokines could be consequences of underlying pathology (antigen persistence, autoimmunity) rather than proximate drivers. The study is explicitly designed to be associative.

9. **Missing healthy controls for some analytes:** Supplementary Fig. 2 includes a healthy control (HC) comparator for some cytokine plots, confirming that both symptomatic and asymptomatic post-COVID participants differ from HC for several cytokines, but the HC sample is not fully described.

## Model / Tool Availability

No computational model, tool, or dataset released. The 33-plex cytokine panel (BD Bioscience cytometric bead array) and in-house S1-IgG ELISA are described in Methods; protocols were previously published in Kwon et al. 2020 (Am J Trop Med Hyg) and Jung et al. 2022 (Immune Netw).

## Follow-up

**Papers to read next:**
- Talla et al. 2023 (Nat Commun 14:3417; paper:Talla2023 if summarised): serum protein signatures defining an inflammatory PASC subcategory — directly comparable cytokine/proteomics approach, Western cohort; would allow cross-cohort comparison of the IL-10/VEGF/IL-6 signature.
- Schultheiß et al. 2022 (Cell Rep Med 3:100663): IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF cytokine triad in post-acute COVID-19 — cited by authors as a key comparator.
- Patterson et al. 2021 (Front Immunol 12:700782): immune-based prediction of COVID-19 severity/chronicity by machine learning — cited by authors.
- Queiroz et al. 2022 (Front Cell Infect Microbiol): cytokine profiles in acute vs. long COVID — authors cite as comparator with conflicting IL-10 findings.
- Wang et al. 2023 (Cell Rep Med 4:101254): sequential multi-omics for long COVID clinical phenotypes — authors cite as complementary clustering approach.

**Questions raised for this project:**
- Does the IL-10/VEGF co-elevation pattern in PASC replicate in other PAIS cohorts (ME/CFS post-infection, PTLDS)? If so, it strengthens the shared attractor hypothesis.
- Are FasL, I-TAC (CXCL11), and IFN-α elevations in PEM/fatigue reproducible in larger cohorts? These are less commonly reported than IL-6/IL-10 in PASC and may represent distinct T-cell activation signatures.
- Given tocilizumab use was significantly higher in the PASC group (p = 0.019), is there a confounding effect on cytokine profiles, and what does this imply for JAK-STAT / IL-6 receptor blockade as potential intervention (question:0006)?
- Does the cytokine-uncoupled symptom cluster 3 (cough, dizziness, hair loss) implicate structural/mechanical mechanisms, and do these symptoms track different biomarker classes (e.g., autonomic, complement)?
