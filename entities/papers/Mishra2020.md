# COVID-19 and Menstrual Status

- **Authors:** Neha Mishra, Ritu Sharma, Pinky Mishra, Monika Singh, Shikha Seth, Trideep Deori, Payal Jain
- **Year:** 2020
- **Journal:** Journal of Mid-life Health
- **DOI:** 10.4103/jmh.JMH_288_20
- **BibTeX key:** Mishra2020
- **Source:** PDF: `~/d/health/processes/post-acute-infection/papers/pdfs/2020_Mishra_COVID-19-and-Menstrual-Status-Is-Menopause-an-Independent-Risk-Factor-for-SARS-Cov-2.pdf`

## Key Contribution

Mishra et al. compare acute COVID-19 presentation and short follow-up symptoms in premenopausal and menopausal hospitalized women.
The paper is useful mainly as an early clinical attempt to test whether menopausal status independently predicts acute COVID severity.

## Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study of 147 RT-PCR-confirmed women aged 18-70 years admitted with mild or moderate COVID-19 between May and August 2020.
The authors compared 96 premenopausal women with 51 menopausal women, excluding severe disease at admission, pregnancy/lactation, current estrogen therapy, uncontrolled major disease, steroids, and cancer therapy.
Primary outcome was hospital stay duration; secondary outcomes included admission severity, progression, oxygen or ventilation requirement, mortality, residual symptoms at 8-12 weeks, and menstrual-pattern changes.

## Key Findings

Menopausal women had more moderate disease at admission, more oxygen requirement (62.7% vs 20.8%), more ventilatory support (27.5% vs 1%), more progression to severe disease (23.5% vs 7.3%), longer hospital stay (14.1 +/- 8.9 vs 8.6 +/- 3.9 days), and five deaths versus none in the premenopausal group.
However, multivariable regression did not show menopause as an independent predictor of hospital stay or disease progression after accounting for age, severity, comorbidities, and inflammatory markers.
Residual symptoms at 8-12 weeks were more frequent in menopausal women (52.2% vs 12.5%), especially weakness and breathlessness.
Among premenopausal women, 18.75% reported menstrual-pattern changes after infection.

## Relevance

This paper warns against overinterpreting unadjusted menopause-severity associations.
For PAIS, the residual-symptom finding is suggestive but not a long-COVID cohort result because follow-up was short, acute severity differed, and the menopausal group carried more age and comorbidity burden.
It supports the project rule that age, BMI, comorbidities, acute severity, and hormone status must be modeled jointly.

## Limitations

The study is retrospective, single-center, small, and not designed around a long-COVID case definition.
Menopausal status is clinically inferred rather than hormone-measured.
The premenopausal and menopausal groups differ substantially in age, BMI, comorbidities, and baseline severity, leaving high residual confounding.

## Model / Tool Availability

No model, tool, or reusable dataset was released.

## Follow-up

Use as a cautionary example in the synthesis: menopause-associated acute severity can disappear after adjustment.
Longitudinal cohorts need hormone measures and enough sample size to separate age, menopause, comorbidity, and acute severity.
