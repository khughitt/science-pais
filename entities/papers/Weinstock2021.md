## Key Contribution

**HYPOTHESIS ORIGIN, NOT MECHANISTIC EVIDENCE.** This cross-sectional self-report symptom survey is the founding empirical document for the claim that mast cell activation (MCA) / mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) symptoms are common in long COVID (LC) and that pre-existing or SARS-CoV-2-triggered MCAS may underlie LC pathophysiology. It does not measure mast cell mediators in LC patients, does not establish a diagnosis of MCAS in the LC group, and does not test any causal mechanism. What it demonstrates — using the Mast Cell Mediator Release Syndrome (MCMRS) questionnaire — is that for post-COVID symptom burden in a self-selected Facebook-recruited LC cohort (N=136), no statistically significant difference was detected from a clinically diagnosed MCAS comparison group (N=80) on composite measures, while for pre-COVID symptom burden of the same LC respondents, no statistically significant difference was detected from general-population controls (N=136). (These are ordinary null significance tests, not equivalence tests — non-significance here is failure to detect a difference, not demonstrated equivalence, and is subject to this study's modest N and recall design.) The authors explicitly state that these results "support, but do not provide definitive proof" of their prior hypothesis (Afrin et al. 2020). This paper's role in the project is as **provenance for the MCAS-long-COVID hypothesis line**, not as evidence that MCAS mediates long COVID.

## Methods

**Study design:** Cross-sectional online self-report symptom survey. IRB-approved by Missouri Baptist Medical Center (St. Louis, MO). Data collected October 2020 – May 2021.

**Three comparison groups:**
1. **Long-COVID (LC) participants (N=136):** Recruited via LC-focused Facebook support groups. 89.7% female; mean age 46.9 ± 12.9 years; BMI 28.2 ± 8.4. 80.9% confirmed by testing; 16.2% were hospitalized for acute COVID-19. Each LC participant completed two questionnaires: one by recall of pre-COVID symptoms, one for current (post-COVID) symptoms.
2. **General population controls (N=136):** Hospital employees and their spouses from Missouri Baptist Medical Center and the lead investigator's practice. 65.4% female; mean age 49.2 ± 15.5; BMI 25.5 ± 4.6. No prior COVID-19 symptoms.
3. **MCAS patients (N=80):** Patients diagnosed with MCAS at a gastroenterology clinic (by the lead investigator, LBW) within the prior 2 years, using modified Consensus-2 criteria (≥5 organ systems, ≥1 elevated mediator, clinical response to MC-directed therapy). 85.0% female; mean age 47.7 ± 16.4. Completed questionnaire by recall of pre-treatment symptoms.

**Primary instrument:** The Mast Cell Mediator Release Syndrome (MCMRS) questionnaire — a symptom inventory covering ~51 symptoms across 10 body systems (skin, abdomen, neurologic, cardiovascular, respiratory, urinary/pelvic, musculoskeletal, ophthalmic, otolaryngologic, constitutional), developed from the MCAS clinical literature. Symptom severity rated 0–10. Additional validated instruments: Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS; 10-item, scored 10–50) and self-rated overall health / quality-of-life (QOL; 0–100 scale). Neither mediator testing nor biopsy was performed in the LC or control groups — MCAS cannot be formally diagnosed from this questionnaire alone.

**Analysis:** Independent samples t-tests (continuous MCMRS scores) and chi-square tests (symptom prevalence); McNemar's tests for within-LC pre/post comparisons; p ≤ 0.05. Multivariate analysis of medications/supplements. Spider-web plots of normalized MCMRS system sub-scores.

## Key Findings

**Pre-COVID LC vs controls — no significant baseline difference detected:**
- MCMRS score: 11.4 (SD 7.5) vs 10.2 (controls) — not significant (p=0.08)
- MCMRS cumulative severity: 47.2 vs 47.9 — not significant (p=0.9)
- Only 3 of 51 symptoms differed significantly at baseline (controls had more chest pain and brain fog; LC had more constipation)
- Fatigue (FAS 16.6 vs 18.2, p=0.08) and QOL (75.6 vs 75.4, p=0.95) not significantly different

**Post-COVID LC vs pre-COVID LC — marked increase across all metrics:**
- MCMRS score: 11.4 → 21.1 (SD 6.3), p<0.0001
- MCMRS cumulative severity: 47.2 → 154.1 (SD 67.9), p<0.0001
- MCMRS system count: 6.1 → 8.3 (SD 1.4), p<0.0001
- MC/LC symptom count: 11.6 → 22.0 (SD 6.6), p<0.0001
- Fatigue (FAS): 16.6 → 34.5 (SD 10.7), p<0.0001; classified as "substantial-to-extreme" fatigue range
- QOL: 75.6 → 40.5 (SD 21.7), p<0.0001
- Mean severity increased significantly for 50 of 51 symptoms (all except seizures)

**Post-COVID LC vs MCAS patients (pre-treatment) — no significant difference detected on composite measures:**
- MCMRS score: 21.1 vs 20.1 (MCAS), not significant (p=0.4)
- MCMRS cumulative severity: 154.1 vs 161.0 (MCAS), not significant (p=0.6)
- MCMRS system count: 8.3 vs 8.0 (MCAS), not significant (p=0.3)
- MC/LC symptom count: 22.0 vs 20.4 (MCAS), not significant (p=0.2)
- Fatigue: 34.5 vs 31.2 (MCAS), not significant (p=0.06)
- QOL: 40.5 vs 56.6 — **significantly different** (p<0.0001); LC had *worse* QOL than MCAS
- 21 of 51 individual symptoms did differ significantly: MCAS had more abdominal/GI and histamine-reactivity symptoms; post-COVID LC had more weakness, fatigue attacks, muscle pain, cough, sweats, anosmia, hair loss, and ageusia

## Relevance

**Role in this project: hypothesis provenance, not mechanistic support.**

This paper provides the empirical origin for the MCAS-long-COVID hypothesis — the claim that a symptomatic overlap exists between LC and MCAS that justifies testing whether mast cell biology underlies LC. In the project's framework:

- **hypothesis:0001 (shared attractor) — indirect, weak.** Pre-existing MCAS as a host state that lowers the threshold for entry into a self-sustaining post-infectious state is one mechanism by which the attractor could be entered at lower acute severity. This paper provides no test of that mechanism.
- **hypothesis:0004 (severity threshold / host reserve) — indirect, weak.** Pre-existing MCAS as a low-reserve/low-threshold host state is conceptually consistent with h0004's proposition that host reserve modulates the acute-illness severity required to transition into chronic PAIS. This paper does not measure baseline reserve or severity thresholds.
- **hypothesis:0020 (host immune baseline reserve gate) — indirect, weak.** Pre-existing mast-cell priming is one candidate host immune baseline state; this paper is suggestive but does not provide the immune-measure evidence h0020 requires.
- **question:0034 (pre-existing atopic/MCAS as mechanistically distinct PAIS subgroup) — direct provenance.** This paper is the founding symptom-survey observation that anchors q0034's empirical narrative. It is the claim that launched the MCAS-LC hypothesis; subsequent work (e.g., Augustin et al. 2025, conference abstract, n=21, elevated tryptase + ileal MC activation in PCS+) begins to supply actual mediator data that this paper lacked.
- **topic:population-boundary-conditions-and-effect-modifiers-in-pais — section D.** This paper is the founding citation for the MCAS-as-pre-existing-amplifier hypothesis summarized in section D.

**The mechanistic inversion that makes MCAS interesting as a boundary condition:** Standard PAIS models treat mast cell activation as a downstream consequence of persistent antigen or immune dysregulation. In pre-existing MCAS, this ordering is inverted — mast cells are the upstream amplifier. This paper's observation (LC symptom profile matches pre-treatment MCAS) is consistent with this inversion but cannot distinguish it from multiple alternative explanations, including post-COVID MCA that is secondary (not pre-existing), non-specific symptom overlap driven by fatigue and neurological dysfunction, or ascertainment/selection artifact.

## Limitations

1. **Symptom survey only — no MCAS diagnosis in LC.** The most critical limitation: no mast cell mediator testing (tryptase, histamine, prostaglandin D2, etc.) was performed in the LC or control groups. The MCMRS is a symptom inventory, not a diagnostic test. MCA symptoms overlap with many conditions — fibromyalgia, ME/CFS, dysautonomia, histamine intolerance — and symptom equivalence does not establish a shared mechanism or shared diagnosis.

2. **Highly self-selected LC cohort.** LC participants were recruited from Facebook support groups for LC. Members of medical support groups are self-selected, likely more severely ill, and may be primed to attribute symptoms to specific conditions. This is acknowledged by the authors. Self-selection bias likely overestimates symptom burden relative to the general LC population.

3. **MCAS comparison group is specialist-referred, GI-predominant.** The 80 MCAS patients were all diagnosed by the lead investigator (LBW) at a gastroenterology subspecialty clinic. This likely enriches the group for GI-predominant MCAS presentation. Indeed, MCAS patients reported significantly higher abdominal pain, GI cramping, diarrhea, and nausea than post-COVID-LC patients — suggesting the MCAS group's profile was pulled toward GI symptoms in ways that may not represent all MCAS presentations.

4. **Retrospective recall design for pre-COVID symptoms.** The LC group's pre-COVID questionnaire was completed by recall at the time of the post-COVID survey. The striking baseline equivalence between LC and controls is partly reassuring, but recall of pre-illness symptom status in the context of current severe illness is subject to well-documented bias (telescoping, anchoring). The authors acknowledge this limitation.

5. **Gender imbalance between groups (89.7% female LC vs 65.4% female controls).** The groups differ significantly in sex composition. Sex is associated with both MCAS prevalence and LC prevalence. The comparison between groups is not sex-stratified, which may confound the symptom severity comparisons.

6. **No causal architecture or temporal ordering.** The survey captures current symptom state after COVID-19, not pre-existing MCAS diagnosis before infection. Whether the MCA symptom pattern reflects pre-existing MCAS that was destabilized by COVID-19 (the authors' central hypothesis), post-COVID-onset MCA triggered by the infection (a secondary/post-infectious mechanism), or non-specific symptom overlap cannot be distinguished from this design.

7. **Author conflicts of interest.** The lead author (LBW) and co-author Afrin are advisors to MC Sciences Ltd.; co-author Molderings is the company's chief medical officer. Both the hypothesis and the comparison group (patients diagnosed by LBW) originate from the same clinical practice.

8. **MCMRS not independently validated.** The MCMRS questionnaire is not an externally validated diagnostic instrument; it was developed within the same group's clinical framework. No independent validation of its specificity for MCAS vs other multi-system inflammatory or functional conditions is cited.

## Model / Tool Availability

No computational model or software tool released. The MCMRS questionnaire is provided in the paper's appendix (Appendix 4). LC and control data are not publicly deposited.

## Follow-up

**What this paper leaves open (for question:0034 and subsequent work):**

- Does pre-infection MCAS diagnosis prospectively predict long-COVID incidence (not just symptom similarity retrospectively)? No prospective data exist.
- Do LC patients with an MCAS-like profile have elevated mast cell mediators (tryptase, histamine, prostaglandin D2) on formal testing? Augustin et al. (2025, conference abstract, n=21) provided the first partial answer (elevated serum tryptase p=0.020 in PCS+ vs PCS−, with concurrent ileal mast cell activation and gut antigen persistence), now matured to a full peer-reviewed paper (`paper:Augustin2026`, *Mucosal Immunology* 2026, N=43) — still cross-sectional and post-infection, so treat as hypothesis-supporting, not confirmatory, and not evidence of *pre-existing* MCAS.
- Is the MCA symptom pattern in LC driven by pre-existing MCAS (upstream amplifier model, per q0034) or by post-COVID-onset secondary MCA (downstream consequence, consistent with h0002 antigen-persistence frame)?
- Does MC-directed therapy improve LC outcomes in a controlled setting? The authors cite anecdotal clinical improvement; no RCT data existed at the time of publication.

**Papers to read:**
- Afrin et al. 2020 (J Investig Allergol Clin Immunol) — the prior hypothesis paper this study was testing.
- Augustin et al. 2025 (Open Forum Infectious Diseases, conference abstract) — first mediator-level signal in a long-COVID cohort; now published in full as Augustin et al. 2026 (`paper:Augustin2026`, *Mucosal Immunology* 19(3):100328, N=43).
- Wolff et al. 2023 (Clinical & Experimental Allergy) — systematic review showing pre-existing allergic disease (atopy) as a risk factor for long-COVID symptoms; the population-level complement to this individual-mechanism paper.
- Theoharides and Conti 2020 — one of the early mast-cell-COVID hypothesis papers cited here.
