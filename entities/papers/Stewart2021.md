# Long COVID risk - a signal to address sex hormones and women health

- **Authors:** Stuart Stewart, Louise Newson, Tracy A. Briggs, Dimitris Grammatopoulos, Lawrence Young, Paramjit Gill
- **Year:** 2021
- **Journal:** The Lancet Regional Health - Europe
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.lanepe.2021.100242
- **BibTeX key:** Stewart2021
- **Source:** PDF: `~/d/health/processes/post-acute-infection/papers/pdfs/2021_Stewart_Long-COVID-risk-a-signal-to-address-sex-hormones-and-women-s-health.pdf`

## Key Contribution

This commentary argues that the female predominance of long COVID and its overlap with perimenopause and menopause symptoms justify sex-hormone-aware clinical assessment and research.
Its contribution is hypothesis-generating rather than empirical.

## Methods

The paper is a commentary, not a primary study.
It synthesizes early long-COVID risk observations, known sex differences in acute COVID-19, menopause symptom overlap, and immunomodulatory effects of estradiol.

## Key Findings

The authors argue that long COVID disproportionately affects women, especially women under age 50, while acute COVID mortality is higher in men.
They note that fatigue, muscle aches, palpitations, cognitive impairment, sleep disturbance, low stamina, and headache overlap substantially with perimenopause and menopause.
They recommend routine menopause assessment in long-COVID clinics and argue that, when clinically appropriate, response to hormone replacement therapy may help clarify hormone-deficiency contributions.

## Relevance

The commentary anticipates the project concern that female predominance in PAIS could reflect sex-hormone biology, ascertainment, symptom overlap, or combinations of these.
It is useful as a conceptual source for `question:0007-mechanism-of-female-predominance-in-pais`, but it should not be weighted as empirical evidence for HRT or estrogen as long-COVID treatment.

## Limitations

This is a commentary with no new data.
It has potential conflicts through menopause-care advocacy and should be read as an agenda-setting paper.
It does not separate biological sex effects from gendered socioeconomic exposure, healthcare-seeking, or comorbidity effects.
