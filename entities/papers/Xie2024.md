# Long-term outcomes following hospital admission for COVID-19 versus seasonal influenza: a cohort study

## Key Contribution

This large retrospective cohort study from the US Veterans Affairs healthcare system provides a comprehensive comparative evaluation of acute and long-term (18-month) health outcomes following hospital admission for COVID-19 versus seasonal influenza across 94 prespecified outcomes spanning ten organ systems. The central finding is that hospital admission for COVID-19 carries substantially higher long-term mortality risk and multi-organ burden than hospital admission for seasonal influenza, with the important exception of pulmonary outcomes — where seasonal influenza shows higher risk — revealing SARS-CoV-2 as a more deeply multisystemic pathogen in the post-acute phase. Crucially, decomposition analyses demonstrate that for both viruses the post-acute phase contributes a greater burden of health loss than the acute phase itself, quantifying the failure of homeostatic recovery as an underappreciated and dominant component of the overall disease toll.

## Methods

**Study design:** Retrospective observational cohort study using VA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW) electronic health records.

**Cohorts:**
- COVID-19: 81,280 participants admitted to hospital between March 1, 2020 and June 30, 2022 with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test within 5 days before or 30 days after admission.
- Seasonal influenza: 10,985 participants admitted to hospital between Oct 1, 2015 and Feb 28, 2019 with a positive influenza test within 5 days before or 30 days after admission (76.1% influenza A; 23.9% other types).
- Combined: 127,640 person-years of follow-up; median follow-up 1.46 years in both cohorts.

**COVID-19 pandemic sub-groups:** Pre-delta (n=40,481; 38.2%), delta (n=20,680; 25.4%), omicron (n=22,693; 36.4%).

**Outcomes:** 94 prespecified individual health outcomes (ICD-10 codes, lab values, prescription records) aggregated into 10 organ systems: cardiovascular, coagulation/haematological, fatigue, gastrointestinal, kidney, mental health, metabolic, musculoskeletal, neurological, and pulmonary. Outcomes were incident (first occurrence after T0, not present before T0). All-cause mortality and health-care utilisation (readmission, ICU admission) were also assessed.

**Burden quantification:** Disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) computed using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) health burden coefficients applied to cumulative event rates across each organ system.

**Confounding control:** Inverse probability weighting (IPW) based on propensity scores from logistic regression on age, race, sex, smoking, area deprivation index, eGFR, BMI, blood pressure, comorbidities (cancer, cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease, dementia, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, HIV, immune dysfunction, liver disease, peripheral artery disease), medications, and health-care utilisation. Post-weighting all SMDs < 0.1 (good balance achieved).

**Statistical models:** Cox proportional hazards models (competing-risk adjusted) for relative risks (HRs); weighted Poisson regression for absolute rates and DALYs. 95% CIs from 1,000-bootstrap resamples (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles). Power: ≥91% to detect HR of 0.80 at event rates 1-20%.

**Sensitivity analyses:** Seven pre-specified sensitivity analyses including alternative exposure windows, ICU-adjusted models, doubly-robust estimation, overlap weighting, Kaplan-Meier risk estimation, censoring-weight adjustment for reinfection, and influenza A vs. all influenza subgroup analyses; all yielded consistent results.

## Key Findings

**Mortality (18-month follow-up):**
- COVID-19 HR for death: 1.51 (95% CI 1.45–1.58) vs. seasonal influenza.
- Cumulative death rate at 540 days: 28.46 per 100 (COVID-19) vs. 19.84 per 100 (influenza).
- Excess death rate: 8.62 (95% CI 7.55–9.44) per 100 persons in COVID-19 vs. influenza.
- COVID-19 maintained higher death risk in all non-overlapping time windows (0–30, 31–180, 181–360, 361–540 days).

**Adverse health outcomes — scope:**
- COVID-19 associated with significantly increased risk of 64/94 (68.1%) prespecified outcomes.
- Seasonal influenza associated with increased risk of only 6/94 (6.4%) outcomes.
- COVID-19 had higher risk in all organ systems except pulmonary; influenza had higher risk for pulmonary outcomes (cough, shortness of breath, acute stress reaction).

**Selected organ-system excess burdens at 540 days (COVID-19 vs. influenza, per 100 persons):**
- Cardiovascular: +1.62 (95% CI 0.81–2.61)
- Coagulation/haematological: +8.00 (7.27–8.99)
- Fatigue: +10.93 (10.84–11.81)
- Gastrointestinal: +10.93 included; individual outcomes include cholangitis HR 2.04, irritable bowel syndrome HR 2.09, liver abnormalities HR 1.89
- Kidney: +3.19 (2.18–4.20)
- Mental health: +4.28 (3.53–5.26); suicidal ideation HR 2.32 at 0–30 days
- Metabolic: +2.01 (1.19–2.82)
- Musculoskeletal: +12.24 (9.15–13.24); myopathy HR 2.14
- Neurological: +6.38 (5.62–7.40); ischaemic stroke HR 2.55, loss of smell HR 3.21 (0–360 days), paresthesia HR 2.15
- Pulmonary: influenza excess +7.23 (95% CI 6.23–8.03)

**Cumulative adverse event rates across all organ systems:**
- COVID-19: 615.18 (95% CI 605.17–624.88) per 100 persons
- Seasonal influenza: 536.90 (527.38–544.90) per 100 persons
- Excess rate (COVID-19 vs. influenza): 78.72 (95% CI 66.15–91.24) per 100 persons

**Total DALYs across all organ systems:**
- COVID-19: 287.43 (95% CI 281.10–293.59) per 100 persons
- Seasonal influenza: 242.66 (236.75–247.67) per 100 persons
- Excess DALYs (COVID-19): +45.03 (95% CI 37.15–52.90) per 100 persons

**Health-care utilisation:**
- Hospital readmission: COVID-19 HR 1.11 (95% CI 1.08–1.13); excess rate 20.50 (16.10–24.86) per 100 persons.
- ICU admission: COVID-19 HR 1.27 (95% CI 1.19–1.36); excess rate 9.23 (6.68–11.82) per 100 persons.

**Acute vs. post-acute decomposition:**
- For both viruses, >50% of total incident disease burden across most organ systems (except GI) occurred in the post-acute phase (after day 30), not the acute phase.
- COVID-19 had a higher burden in both acute AND post-acute phases than influenza for all organ systems except pulmonary.
- This pattern was consistent across pre-delta, delta, and omicron eras.

**Vaccination analysis:** COVID-19 maintained higher risk of death and involvement in all organ systems except pulmonary regardless of COVID-19 or influenza vaccination status.

## Relevance

This paper is directly central to the project research question on post-acute infection syndromes (PAIS) and the PAIS frame of **failed homeostatic recovery after acute infection**.

1. **Quantifies the post-acute failure mode:** The decomposition result — that >50% of total disease burden across most organ systems accrues after the acute phase — directly operationalises what PAIS research frames as failed homeostatic recovery. The study shows this is not a COVID-specific quirk but applies to seasonal influenza too, suggesting a shared biological principle: the acute infection initiates multi-organ dysregulation that the immune/physiological system fails to resolve within 30 days.

2. **Establishes cross-infection comparability:** By directly juxtaposing COVID-19 and influenza under identical follow-up windows, the paper provides empirical grounding for the project's core hypothesis that different pathogens can trigger a shared post-infectious failure mode, differing in magnitude and organ-system distribution rather than mechanism type.

3. **Pulmonary exception as mechanistic signal:** Seasonal influenza's higher pulmonary burden vs. COVID-19's higher extrapulmonary burden suggests the post-acute failure mode is organ-tropism-conditioned: initial tropism shapes which organ systems fail to recover. This is important for the project's effort to characterise the shared vs. pathogen-specific features of PAIS.

4. **Multisystem severity ranking:** The specific organ systems with highest COVID-19 excess (fatigue, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, coagulation, neurological) map directly onto the canonical long COVID / PAIS symptom clusters studied in other project papers (Komaroff2023, Choutka2022, Bateman2023), providing population-scale epidemiological confirmation of what mechanistic studies propose.

5. **DALY-based burden quantification:** The 45-DALY excess per 100 persons is a population-health metric that can anchor comparative burden estimates when the project synthesises severity across different PAIS conditions (relevant to health-meta cross-project scope).

## Limitations

1. **VA cohort generalisability:** Study population is predominantly older white males (~95% male, mean age ~70 years). Findings may not generalise to younger adults, women, or non-veteran populations. Women, younger adults, and paediatric populations are likely underrepresented in PAIS research based on VA data; the project should weight this accordingly.

2. **Historical influenza comparator:** Influenza cohort enrolled 2015–2019 before the pandemic. Temporal differences in clinical care, co-circulating viruses, and the secular trend in ICU management could introduce unmeasured confounding. Also, hospital admission for influenza was rare during the COVID-19 enrolment period, necessitating a historical comparison that cannot be contemporaneous.

3. **Residual confounding:** IPW balances observed covariates; unobserved confounders (e.g., precise smoking pack-years, environmental exposures, genetic immune variation) cannot be eliminated. Cox model results are sensitive to proportional hazards assumption violations.

4. **Hospital-admission threshold:** Results apply only to people severe enough to require hospitalisation. Mild/moderate infections not requiring admission (the majority) are excluded; post-acute burden in non-hospitalised individuals may differ substantially in magnitude and pattern.

5. **Exposure misclassification:** Screening practices for COVID-19 vs. influenza differed during the pandemic, potentially biasing case ascertainment. Sensitivity analyses using a broader admission window (±5 days) were consistent.

6. **Outcome ascertainment from administrative data:** ICD-10 coding does not perfectly capture all clinical events; some outcomes may be under- or over-ascertained depending on clinical documentation practices.

7. **No mechanistic resolution:** The study documents epidemiological burden but does not resolve the biological mechanisms (persistent viral antigen, immune dysregulation, autoimmunity, microbiome disruption) driving the post-acute excess — which is the primary scientific question for this project.

8. **Era comparisons limited:** The study compared across pandemic eras for COVID-19 internal consistency but was not designed to assess whether omicron-era COVID-19 outcomes converge with influenza — a question of ongoing public health relevance.

No computational model or software tool is released with this paper. ## Follow-up

**Papers to read/cross-reference:**
- Bowe B, Xie Y, Al-Aly Z. *Postacute sequelae of COVID-19 at 2 years* (Nat Med 2023) — extends the same group's follow-up to 24 months and refines organ-system burden estimates.
- Xu E, Xie Y, Al-Aly Z. *Long-term neurologic outcomes of COVID-19* (Nat Med 2022) — deeper dive into the neurological system excess documented here.
- Xie Y et al. *Long-term cardiovascular outcomes of COVID-19* (Nat Med 2022) — dedicated cardiovascular analysis underpinning the cardiovascular findings here.
- Al-Aly Z, Bowe B, Xie Y. *Long COVID after breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection* (Nat Med 2022) — informs the vaccination stratification results in this paper.

**Questions this raises for the project:**
1. Does the post-acute phase dominance of disease burden (~50%+ in most organ systems) hold equally across other PAIS-defining infections (e.g., Lyme disease, Q fever, dengue)? If so, this would strongly support a shared failure-mode model rather than pathogen-specific pathology.
2. What biological mechanisms differentiate COVID-19's extrapulmonary post-acute burden from influenza's pulmonary post-acute burden — and does this reflect differential viral tropism, immune resolution failure, or both?
3. The fatigue domain shows the largest absolute excess (+10.93/100 persons), which maps to the ME/CFS spectrum. Can the VA cohort data be used to identify the subset meeting ME/CFS diagnostic criteria, and what are their biological predictors?
