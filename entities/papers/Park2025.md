# Shifts in Influenza and Respiratory Syncytial Virus Infection Patterns in Korea After the COVID-19 Pandemic Resulting From Immunity Debt: Retrospective Observational Study

- **Authors:** Minah Park, Won Suk Choi, Benjamin J Cowling
- **Year:** 2025
- **Journal:** JMIR Public Health and Surveillance, vol. 11, e68058
- **DOI:** 10.2196/68058
- **PMCID:** PMC12309781
- **BibTeX key:** Park2025
- **Source:** PDF (full text read directly)

## Key Contribution

This retrospective observational study uses seven years of South Korea's national sentinel surveillance data (KINRESS/KDCA, 2017–2024) to document how COVID-19-era nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) reshaped the epidemiology of influenza and RSV. The central finding is that the post-pandemic resurgence did **not** manifest as an overall increase in disease burden (the feared "tripledemic") but as a **redistribution of severe disease toward age groups that missed typical exposure windows** — school-aged children (7–18 y) for influenza and toddlers (1–6 y) for RSV. This provides the strongest East Asian, nationally representative evidence that "immunity debt" — population-level immune naivety from NPI-suppressed exposure — operates as an age-specific susceptibility shift rather than a uniform burden amplifier.

## Methods

**Study design:** Retrospective observational time-series analysis.

**Data source:** Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) Influenza and Respiratory Viruses Surveillance System (KINRESS), comprising 300 sentinel outpatient clinics, 18 provincial laboratories, and 221 inpatient hospitals. Data from week 36/2017 through week 35/2024 (7 full respiratory seasons).

**Periods:** Pre-pandemic (2017/18–2019/20; 3 seasons), pandemic (2020/21; 1 season), post-pandemic (2021/22–2023/24; 3 seasons). The 2021/22 season is classified as post-pandemic because Korea's "Living with COVID-19" phased NPI-relaxation plan started November 2021.

**Incidence proxy:** Product of the ILI consultation proportion (outpatient) and the respiratory-specimen positivity rate for each influenza subtype or RSV (virologic), derived from K-RISS real-time RT-PCR surveillance.

**Hospitalization rates:** Age-specific inpatient hospital admissions confirmed for influenza or RSV divided by age-specific mid-year population estimates from Statistics Korea (adjusted per 100,000 persons).

**Statistical methods:** Time-series analysis to characterize seasonality changes. Age-specific proportions and hospitalization rates compared across pre- and post-pandemic periods. All analyses in R 4.1.1.

**Ethics:** Aggregated surveillance data; IRB exemption granted (Ewha Womans University no. 2025-0096).

## Key Findings

**Seasonality disruption:**
- Both RSV and influenza showed delayed and prolonged epidemics post-pandemic vs the typical narrow winter peak.
- RSV returned first (2021/22, peak week 5 — 7–8 weeks late), then developed a bimodal pattern in 2022/23 (early peak wk 41, second peak wk 17).
- Influenza took one additional season to return, reappearing in 2022/23 with a typical winter peak (wk 1).

**Overall burden — lower than pre-pandemic, not higher:**
- Hospitalization rates returned toward pre-pandemic levels by 2022/23–2023/24 but did not exceed them overall.
- Pre-pandemic mean influenza hospitalizations: ~17,000 (SD 4483) per season; RSV: ~14,583 (SD 2441).
- Post-pandemic: RSV hospitalizations ~8,728 per season (≈60% of pre-pandemic mean), partially reflecting the delayed rebound.
- By 2023/24, overall influenza rate (24.3/100k) and RSV rate (17.2/100k) remained near or slightly below 2019/20 levels (24.7 and 23.3/100k, respectively).

**Age-specific shift — influenza (older children hardest hit):**
- Children aged 7–12 y: hospitalization rate rose from 46.8 to 64.4 per 100,000 (2019/20 → 2023/24); share of influenza admissions rose from 10% to 18% (2019/20 → 2022/23).
- Children aged 13–18 y: rate rose from 16.4 to 30.0 per 100,000; share rose from 4% to 10%.
- Infants (<1 y) and young children (1–6 y) for influenza: hospitalization rates declined post-pandemic.

**Age-specific shift — RSV (toddlers hardest hit):**
- Children aged 1–6 y: rate rose from 230.8 to 357.5 per 100,000 (2019/20 → 2022/23); share of RSV admissions rose from 48% to 61%.
- This was the only RSV age group showing an increased *proportion* of all RSV admissions post-pandemic.
- Infants (<1 y): RSV rate dropped from 1,396 to 1,236 per 100,000 (2019/20 → 2022/23), the highest-burden group still but with a relative decline.

**Vaccine context:**
- Influenza vaccination coverage among children declined from 78.5% (2019/20) to 69.5% (2023/24), with school-aged children (13 y) at lowest uptake (49.2% vs 82.5% for <5 y).
- Nirsevimab (RSV monoclonal antibody) was not yet available in Korea at time of publication.

**RSV vs influenza NPI resilience:**
- Authors attribute RSV returning faster than influenza to RSV's greater surface stability and direct-contact transmission, making it more resilient to airborne-focused NPIs (masking, distancing).

## Relevance

**Immunity debt as population-level immune-reserve depletion.** The immunity debt concept documented here is a population-scale analog of the host-immune-reserve gate (`hypothesis:0020`): NPI-driven absence of exposure depletes antigen-specific immune memory across birth cohorts, lowering the threshold for severe disease upon first encounter — particularly when primary infection is delayed past the developmentally typical window. This is the epidemiological expression of the reserve-gate mechanism operating across whole age cohorts rather than individual hosts.

**Acute severity threshold linkage.** The finding that older children (7–18 y for influenza, 1–6 y for RSV) experienced disproportionate hospitalization post-pandemic is consistent with the hypothesis that primary infections (never previously encountered) carry higher acute burden, which may in turn gate PAIS risk (`hypothesis:0004`). The paper's observation that "primary RSV infections tend to be more severe than reinfections" (citing Borchers 2013) maps directly onto the severity-threshold framing: delayed first exposure → higher acute severity → potentially higher PAIS risk for that cohort.

**Vaccination and prevention.** Declining influenza vaccination coverage among school-aged children combined with immunity debt compounds the susceptibility signal and provides context for `question:0012` on whether vaccination/prevention modifies PAIS incidence. Here the relevant mechanism is prevention of severity-gating acute events, not direct PAIS prevention.

**Population-level immune imprinting.** The heterogeneous age-specific shifts (different strata for RSV vs influenza, with infants *relatively* protected post-pandemic for RSV) suggest pathogen-specific imprinting dynamics that are conceptually adjacent to antigenic imprinting hypotheses in the project's broader immune-set-point framing (`hypothesis:0009`).

**Scope caveat for this project.** This paper studies the epidemiology of acute respiratory infections post-pandemic; it does not directly study PAIS, long COVID, or post-infectious sequelae. Its relevance is **upstream**: it characterizes the exposure landscape that determines who enters the acute-infection funnel, and at what severity, in the post-pandemic era.

## Limitations

- **Aggregated surveillance data only; no serological data.** Cannot directly measure population immunity levels or confirm the immunity debt mechanism — the immunity-gap interpretation is inferred, not demonstrated.
- **Surveillance is sentinel-based:** findings reflect patterns from 300 clinics and 221 hospitals, not a population-representative sample; testing-rate changes and health-seeking behavior shifts post-pandemic can bias comparisons.
- **Increased post-pandemic testing and awareness** may inflate case counts, though the authors' reliance on hospitalization rates (less sensitive to testing frequency) partially mitigates this.
- **No individual-level data** on vaccination status, prior infection history, or serological immune state linked to clinical outcomes; cannot disentangle immunity debt from vaccine-effectiveness changes or behavior changes.
- **Korea-specific NPI context:** Korea maintained very strict, prolonged NPIs through March 2023, making the magnitude of immunity debt potentially larger than in countries with shorter or milder NPI periods. Generalizability to other settings requires caution.
- **No long-term follow-up:** the study ends at the 2023/24 season; whether age-specific burden shifts persist, attenuate, or invert over subsequent seasons is unknown.
- **Conflict of interest:** two co-authors (Choi and Cowling) have industry consulting relationships with multiple vaccine manufacturers (Moderna, Sanofi Pasteur, Pfizer, GlaxoSmithKline, etc.), relevant given the policy-relevant vaccination coverage discussion.

## Model / Tool Availability

No model, tool, or dataset is released for reuse. Underlying data is publicly available through the KDCA weekly surveillance reports.

## Follow-up

**Papers to read next:**
- Chen L et al. 2025 (cited as ref 6): ecological analysis and cohort study quantifying influenza immunity debt magnitude across countries as a function of NPI stringency — directly tests the NPI-intensity → immunity-debt-magnitude association.
- Munro & House 2024 (cited as ref 7): theoretical "cycles of susceptibility" framework for immunity debt dynamics post-pandemic.
- Rao et al. (JAMA Pediatrics 2023, ref 9): US RSV cohort showing increased 2–5-year-old burden post-pandemic — direct comparator to the Korean RSV finding here.
- Suss & Simões (JAMA Network Open 2024, ref 17): US hospital-based RSV disease burden 2015–2022 across under-5s — longer time series for cross-context comparison.

**Questions this raises for the project:**
- Does NPI-driven immunity debt elevate PAIS risk in the cohorts that experienced delayed primary infections? (The mechanism: more severe primary infection → higher probability of crossing the severity threshold → higher PAIS incidence.) This is not tested here but follows from hypotheses 0004 and 0020.
- Is there a measurable post-pandemic increase in PAIS incidence attributable specifically to influenza or RSV in the age cohorts identified as immunity-debt-affected here (school-age children for influenza, toddlers for RSV)?
- How do population-level immunity-debt dynamics interact with individual-level host reserve to determine who, within an affected cohort, develops PAIS after their delayed primary infection?
