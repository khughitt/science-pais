# Detection of G Protein–Coupled Receptor Autoantibodies in Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome Using Standard Methodology

- **Authors:** Juliette Hall, Kate M. Bourne, Steven Vernino, Viktor Hamrefors, Isabella Kharraziha, Jan Nilsson, Robert S. Sheldon, Artur Fedorowski, Satish R. Raj
- **Year:** 2022
- **Journal:** Circulation, 146(8):613–622
- **DOI:** 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.122.059971
- **PMID:** 35766055
- **PMCID:** PMC9390234
- **BibTeX key:** Hall2022
- **Source:** PMC full text (Europe PMC / PubMed Central)

## Key Contribution

This two-center, case-control study administered the identical commercial binding ELISA (CellTrend) used by prior positive studies to sera from 116 POTS patients and 81 healthy controls, and found **no significant difference** in autoantibody concentrations to any of 11 cardiovascular G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) between the groups. The critical finding is that with manufacturer-supplied seropositivity thresholds, ~98% of POTS patients **and ~100% of healthy controls** tested positive for anti-α1 adrenergic receptor (α1-AR) antibodies, yielding a specificity of approximately zero. When a control-derived threshold (mean + 2 SD) was applied, no receptor showed a significant POTS-vs-control difference. The paper therefore constitutes a **direct specificity refutation** of the CellTrend binding ELISA as a diagnostic or mechanistic tool for GPCR autoimmunity in POTS: the assay cannot discriminate patients from healthy people, and seropositive results in prior studies likely reflect ubiquitous low-titer natural antibodies rather than disease-specific pathogenic autoimmunity.

## Methods

**Design:** Multicenter cross-sectional case-control study. Two sites: Calgary, Canada (52 POTS, 16 controls) and Malmö, Sweden (64 POTS, 65 controls).

**POTS case definition:** Heart rate increase ≥30 bpm within 10 minutes of standing (without orthostatic hypotension), with symptom reproduction, and ≥6 months symptom duration. Standard tilt-table or standing protocol per site.

**Cohort:** 116 POTS patients (91% female, median age 29 years); 81 healthy controls (84% female, median age 27 years). Control selection was sex- and age-matched to address the known demographic skew of POTS.

**ASSAY — CellTrend binding ELISA (the same commercial assay used by prior positive studies):** Commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (CellTrend GmbH, Luckenwalde, Germany). ELISA plates pre-coated with GPCR peptide antigen; patient serum incubated; bound IgG detected with peroxidase-labeled anti-human IgG antibody. Tests were run for autoantibodies to 11 cardiovascular GPCRs: α1-adrenergic receptor (α1-AR), α2-adrenergic receptor (α2-AR), β1-adrenergic receptor (β1-AR), β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR), M1–M5 muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R), and endothelin type A receptor (ETAR).

**Thresholds evaluated:**
1. Manufacturer-provided seropositivity cutoffs (available for 8 of 11 receptors; e.g., 7 units/mL for α1-AR).
2. Alternative control-derived threshold: mean + 2 SD of the control group.

**Statistical analyses:** Median autoantibody concentrations compared by Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Seropositivity rates compared by Fisher's exact test or chi-squared. Discriminatory performance assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with C-statistics (area under the curve) for individual receptors and receptor panels. Analyses run separately by center and combined.

## Key Findings

### Null result — no significant group difference for any receptor

Autoantibody concentrations against all 11 receptors tested were **not significantly different** between POTS patients and healthy controls. This held at both study sites independently and in the combined analysis.

### Seropositivity at manufacturer threshold — near-universal in BOTH groups (the core refutation)

| Receptor | POTS seropositive (%) | Controls seropositive (%) | P-value |
|---|---|---|---|
| α1-AR | **98.3%** | **100%** | 0.5 |
| All other receptors | Lower rates | No significant differences | All non-significant |

The manufacturer-threshold seropositivity for α1-AR was ~98% in POTS patients and 100% in healthy controls — this is not a signal of disease-specific autoimmunity; it reflects a threshold so low that it captures essentially all humans. No receptor showed a significant POTS-vs-control seropositivity difference.

### Seropositivity at control-derived threshold (mean + 2 SD of controls)

| Receptor | POTS (%) | Controls (%) | P-value |
|---|---|---|---|
| AT1R | 12.9% | 7.4% | 0.2 |
| ETAR | 8.6% | 7.4% | 0.8 |
| All others | Not reported as significant | — | All non-significant |

With a stringent threshold calibrated to the healthy control distribution, no receptor showed a statistically significant excess in POTS. The numerically small absolute differences (e.g., AT1R: 12.9% vs. 7.4%) are consistent with chance variation given sample sizes.

### ROC / C-statistic (discriminatory ability)

- **Individual receptors:** Most had C statistics ≤ 0.51 (chance-level discrimination). Best individual receptor: C statistic approximately 0.58.
- **Best multi-receptor panel:** C statistic approximately 0.60–0.65.
- Conclusion by authors: "None of the autoantibody tests had significant ability to discriminate between patients with POTS and healthy controls."

### Consistency across centers

Results were consistent between Calgary and Malmö, ruling out center-specific technical artifacts as an explanation for the null. The Malmö site shares personnel with the Kharraziha2020 positive functional-bioassay group, making the ELISA null particularly informative: the same laboratory population yields different conclusions depending on assay type (binding ELISA vs. functional bioassay).

## Relevance

This paper is the **key disconfirming / specificity-critique paper** for the project's functional autoantibody thread.

**Against `proposition:0018-pais-sfn-anti-gpcr-autoantibody-pathogenicity`:** The proposition that functional anti-GPCR autoantibodies are pathogenic for the PAIS autonomic lesion depends critically on whether GPCR autoantibodies are actually disease-specific. Hall2022 shows that with the standard commercial binding ELISA, virtually all healthy controls test positive — the assay identifies something ubiquitous in human sera (natural low-titer GPCR antibodies) rather than a POTS-specific pathogenic autoantibody. Any positive-study claim resting solely on CellTrend ELISA seropositivity is refuted by this paper as evidence of disease-specific autoimmunity.

**For `question:0009-functional-autoantibodies-drive-dysautonomia`:** This paper registers as cautionary/conflicting evidence. It does **not** rule out that functional autoantibodies drive POTS — it specifically disclaims testing functional assays. The project must hold this distinction carefully: binding ELISA positivity is not meaningful; functional assay positivity (receptor activation in cell-based systems) is the remaining evidentiary question.

**Scope boundary (critical):** Hall2022 tests only binding ELISA seropositivity — the CellTrend commercial assay. It does **not** test:
- Functional cell-based bioassays measuring receptor agonist/antagonist activity
- Immunoadsorption/eluate receptor-activation experiments
- Passive-transfer animal models
- The Bhattacharjee-type luciferase reporter assays

The positive functional-assay literature (e.g., the Löffelholz / Scheibenbogen group's ELISA in ME/CFS, or the Kharraziha2020 functional activity in POTS) uses different methodology. Hall2022 is a direct critique of the **binding ELISA** specifically, and is correctly cited as showing ELISA seroposivity is nonspecific — but it is not a direct refutation of functional autoantibody positivity.

## Limitations

### What this paper does not rule out

- **Functional autoantibodies:** Hall2022 explicitly acknowledges it did not test functional bioassays. The positive rebuttal from the functional-assay camp is valid: a binding ELISA detecting antibody presence is mechanistically distinct from a cell-based assay detecting receptor activation. This scope limitation must be stated clearly whenever Hall2022 is cited as disconfirming.
- **Subgroup-specific pathogenic autoantibodies:** POTS is heterogeneous; a minority subgroup (e.g., post-COVID POTS, hyperadrenergic POTS) might harbor functionally active anti-GPCR antibodies not captured by the overall group comparison.
- **Different ELISA platforms:** Assays from vendors other than CellTrend might have different epitope targeting or threshold calibration.

### Methodological limitations internal to the paper

- **Single assay vendor:** Only CellTrend ELISA was tested. Results cannot be extrapolated to all commercial or research ELISA platforms.
- **Cohort composition:** 91% female POTS patients; results are most applicable to the typical young-female POTS phenotype. Post-COVID POTS (which may have different autoimmune profile) was not specifically characterized.
- **Disease-activity state:** Samples were not collected at a defined point in disease course; autoantibody titers can fluctuate with disease activity, though the near-universal control positivity makes this unlikely to explain the null result.
- **Small Calgary control arm:** Only 16 healthy controls in Calgary; most statistical power came from the Malmö arm (65 controls), creating site imbalance.
- **Cross-sectional design:** Cannot address whether GPCR antibody titers change after interventions (e.g., immunoadsorption) or over time.

### What this paper does rule out (firmly)

- That CellTrend ELISA seropositivity, at manufacturer-provided thresholds, constitutes meaningful evidence of POTS-specific GPCR autoimmunity. Any positive study relying only on this assay and threshold is producing false-positive signals from ubiquitous background antibodies.
- That α1-AR ELISA positivity at the manufacturer threshold distinguishes POTS from health: ~98% POTS and 100% controls are both "seropositive."

## Follow-up

- The decisive next step for q0009 is a head-to-head comparison: same serum samples, CellTrend binding ELISA vs. validated functional cell-based bioassay (receptor-activation luciferase reporter or CHO cell cAMP assay). If functional positivity also fails to discriminate POTS from controls, the autoantibody hypothesis for POTS would be largely refuted; if functional positivity does discriminate, it would suggest binding ELISA is the specific problem, not autoantibody biology.
- Kharraziha2020 (from the overlapping Malmö group) used a functional activity assay and found positive signals; reconciling Hall2022 (ELISA null, same population) with Kharraziha2020 (functional positive) is a priority interpretive task.
- For proposition:0018 specifically: the functional assay evidence base should be reviewed separately (see also Bai2023, and any immunoadsorption RCTs in POTS) before treating this proposition as definitively supported or refuted.
- For q0009's broader scope (long COVID dysautonomia, ME/CFS): a post-COVID POTS cohort studied with functional assays would provide the most relevant evidence update.
