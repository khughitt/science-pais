# Therapeutic options for the treatment of post-acute sequelae of COVID-19: a scoping review

- **Authors:** Yu Bin Seo, Yu Jung Choi, Jun-Won Seo, Eun Jung Kim, Jacob Lee, Joon Young Song
- **Year:** 2025
- **Journal:** BMC Infectious Diseases, vol. 25, article 731
- **DOI:** 10.1186/s12879-025-11131-x
- **BibTeX key:** Seo2025
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This PRISMA-ScR scoping review synthesizes evidence for nine therapeutic classes evaluated for post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC): hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT), ivermectin, metformin, naltrexone (low-dose), palmitoylethanolamide (PEA), micronutrient supplements, antifibrotic agents (pirfenidone, nintedanib), antiviral agents (NMV-r, molnupiravir, remdesivir), and SSRIs. From 413 deduplicated records it identified 39 clinical studies for inclusion, reviewed January 2020 – July 2024. The review provides the most current panoramic map of pharmacological PASC treatment options, highlighting that HBOT, antifibrotic agents, and SSRIs show the most consistent promising signals, while ivermectin evidence is clearly negative and the evidence base for most agents remains too heterogeneous and small-scale to support definitive clinical recommendations.

## Methods

Scoping review conducted per PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-analyses extension for Scoping Reviews). Databases searched: PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library; search period January 1, 2020 – July 22, 2024. Drug-specific MeSH terms were used (Table 1). From 517 records identified, 104 duplicates removed; 413 screened; 129 full-text assessed; 39 included after excluding 284 non-PASC, 32 full-text inaccessible, and 58 lab-based experimental studies. Quality appraised with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist. Studies covering acute-phase COVID-19 treatment (rather than PASC), laboratory studies, and case reports were excluded. Primary evidence hierarchy: meta-analyses and RCTs preferred; observational studies included where evidence was scarce. No quantitative meta-analysis was performed; synthesis is narrative.

## Key Findings

**Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT)**
- Proposed mechanisms: anti-inflammatory (reduces cytokines/interleukins), promotes angiogenic growth factors, enhances mitochondrial respiration and mass. Cognitive and fatigue symptoms are the primary PASC targets.
- One RCT (n < 40): improvements in cognitive function, attention, executive function, energy, sleep quality, psychiatric symptoms, and pain compared to sham; sustained at one-year follow-up.
- Structural neuroimaging (fMRI): HBOT improved disruptions in white-matter tracts and neural-pathway connectivity.
- Cardiac: improvements in global work efficiency and normalization of global longitudinal strain (left ventricular systolic function recovery).
- Safety: 12/20 patients in one study experienced adverse events (31 AEs total), mostly cough, chest pain, chest discomfort; most were transient. Two main risks: barotrauma to middle ear/sinuses and oxygen toxicity (VENTID acronym: vision, ears, nausea, muscle twitching, irritability, dizziness). All studies conducted with n < 40; large-scale RCTs needed.
- Conclusion: promising but limited evidence; careful risk-benefit assessment warranted.

**Ivermectin**
- Hypothesis: suppresses SARS-CoV-2 replication by blocking importin α/β1-mediated transport of viral proteins across the nuclear membrane.
- Large RCT (TOGETHER trial): no reduction in hospitalizations or emergency visits for acute COVID-19.
- COVID-OUT study: unlike metformin, ivermectin had no effect on PASC incidence.
- PRINCIPLE trial: no meaningful improvement in recovery, hospital admissions, or long-term results in PASC.
- Conclusion: No demonstrated benefit; administering ivermectin for PASC is not reasonable based on current evidence.

**Metformin**
- Proposed mechanisms: inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β production; anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic.
- COVID-OUT trial (n = 1,126 observed for 9 months; drug given during acute phase): 93/1,126 (9.3%) developed PASC; metformin group had hazard ratio 0.59 (95% CI 0.39–0.89; p = 0.012) for PASC development vs. placebo. No clinically concerning adverse events.
- Caveat: metformin was administered during acute infection (days 1–14), not post-diagnosis of PASC. Effect on established PASC remains uncertain. Drug given only to non-diabetic adults.
- Conclusion: Promising as a PASC-prevention agent when administered early; clinical trials targeting established PASC patients are needed.

**Low-dose naltrexone (LDN)**
- Dose: 1.5–4.5 mg/day (off-label immunomodulatory / anti-inflammatory use).
- Mechanism: antagonizes TLR-4 on microglia and macrophages → reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α); temporarily blocks opioid receptors → compensatory endogenous opioid upregulation → improved pain and mood.
- Observational studies: 52–67% of PASC patients receiving LDN reported improvements in fatigue, pain, and cognitive function (brain fog, memory, concentration).
- Retrospective cohort (n = 108): hazard ratio 5.04 for improvement vs. physical therapy alone (p = 0.02).
- Cross-sectional survey: low-dose naltrexone showed improvements in PASC symptoms.
- Tolerability: mild fatigue and diarrhea reported; generally well-tolerated.
- Conclusion: Consistent observational signal; RCTs needed to validate efficacy and establish standardized dosing.

**Palmitoylethanolamide (PEA)**
- Cannabimimetic compound; anti-inflammatory via downregulation of mast cells and PPARα agonism; neuroprotective via mast cell–microglia axis.
- umPEA-LUT (ultramicronized PEA with Luteolin) in olfactory dysfunction trials:
  - Olfactory training (OT) + umPEA-LUT: TDI improvement 21.8±9.4 to 29.7±7.5 (p < 0.01) vs. OT alone: 19.6±6.29 to 27.5±2.7 (p < 0.01).
  - umPEA-LUT alone (no OT): no significant improvement in olfactory function.
  - co-ultraPEA-LUT: significant improvement in PRMQ score (prospective memory; T0: 51.94±10.55, T1: 39.67±13.02, p < 0.01) and MoCA raw score (T0: 25.76±2.3, T1: 27.2±2, p = 0.026).
- PEA restores GABAB receptor activity and cortical plasticity (LICI and LTP-like cortical plasticity) in PASC patients.
- OT remains the cornerstone for olfactory dysfunction; PEA may supplement it.
- Conclusion: Promising for olfactory dysfunction and neuroinflammatory PASC symptoms; large-scale RCT warranted.

**Micronutrient supplements**
- Agents reviewed: vitamins A, B, C, D, E; alpha-lipoic acid; L-arginine; omega-3 fatty acids.
- L-arginine + liposomal vitamin C (1.66 g L-arginine + 500 mg liposomal vitamin C) RCT in PASC with persistent fatigue: 6-minute walk distance improvement +40.5 m vs. placebo +75 m (p = 0.001); handgrip strength improvement +6.6 kg (p = 0.03); endothelial function (brachial artery dilation after forearm ischemia) +4.3% vs. +9.4% (p = 0.03).
- Alpha-lipoic acid (300 mg twice daily for 3 months) for olfactory dysfunction in PASC: no significant differences from control in CCCRC score (p = 0.63), threshold (p = 0.50), identification (p = 0.96), or VAS (p = 0.97).
- Meta-analysis on vitamins during acute phase to prevent PASC: inconclusive — insufficient studies, heterogeneous designs, low quality data.
- Conclusion: Evidence insufficient for micronutrients specifically in PASC; low AE risk favors well-designed studies but no current clinical recommendations supportable.

**Antifibrotic agents (pirfenidone, nintedanib)**
- Indication: pulmonary fibrosis as a PASC sequela; ARDS develops in ~42% of COVID-19 pneumonia patients; pulmonary fibrosis in 61–81% of ICU cases.
- Mechanism: nintedanib inhibits FGF, VEGF, PDGF receptor pathways → reduced fibroblast proliferation; pirfenidone inhibits TGF-β and TNF-α → reduced fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis.
- RCT (n = 30): both agents significantly improved forced vital capacity (FVC), diffusing capacity (DLCO), 6-minute walk distance, and O₂ saturation over 12 weeks. Nintedanib: greater 6MWT improvement (p = 0.02) and O₂ saturation (p = 0.005), but higher AEs including diarrhea (80%) and nausea/vomiting (66.6%).
- Retrospective cohort (n = 5,000): pirfenidone and nintedanib preserved pulmonary function parameters at 12 months.
- Cohort study: antifibrotic group 84.42% 1-year survival vs. 69.87% control (HR 0.434, 95% CI 0.264–0.712).
- Nintedanib: statistically significant 1-year mortality reduction (p = 0.013); pirfenidone: improved survival without achieving statistical significance (p = 0.601).
- Conclusion: Multidisciplinary approach including antifibrotics is supported; larger RCTs needed to refine treatment strategies.

**Antiviral agents (NMV-r, molnupiravir, remdesivir)**
- Hypothesis: early antiviral treatment reduces sustained viral replication that drives PASC through persistent inflammation.
- Two meta-analyses of observational studies: early antiviral administration significantly prevented PASC vs. non-antiviral treatment and reduced PASC-related hospitalization and death.
- NMV-r (nirmatrelvir-ritonavir): observational studies showed reduction in PASC incidence; target trial emulation (matched cohorts, n in thousands) showed no significant difference in 29/31 post-COVID conditions except venous thromboembolism and pulmonary embolism.
- STOP-PASC RCT (15-day NMV-r course in established PASC patients): no significant benefit demonstrated.
- Remdesivir: observational benefit signal; RCTs showed no significant long-term benefit.
- Conclusion: Antiviral agents during acute phase may reduce PASC incidence in some patients, but efficacy in established PASC is questionable and requires further study.

**SSRIs (Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors)**
- Proposed PASC mechanisms: immunomodulation, antiplatelet effect, enhancement of vagal activity, neurotransmitter regulation; sigma-1 receptor (S1R) agonism (fluvoxamine, fluoxetine, escitalopram).
- N3C database (n = 17,908): SSRIs with S1R agonist activity (fluvoxamine, fluoxetine, escitalopram) → 29% relative risk reduction in PASC vs. unexposed (RR = 0.704, 95% CI 0.58–0.85; p < 0.001); Non-S1R SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine) → 21% RR reduction (RR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.67–0.93; p = 0.005).
- N3C database (n = 302,626): SSRI use during acute COVID-19 → adjusted risk ratio 0.92 (95% CI 0.86–0.99) for PASC development vs. non-SSRI users.
- Systematic review and meta-analysis (14 clinical studies): fluvoxamine did not consistently prevent PASC development; reductions in neuropsychiatric symptoms (fatigue, depression) reported in some studies.
- Conclusion: SSRIs, especially S1R agonists, represent a promising anti-PASC option; large-scale RCTs and mechanistic studies are essential.

## Relevance

This scoping review is directly relevant to the project research question (**research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes**) and the PAIS frame of **failed homeostatic recovery after acute infection**, specifically by mapping the pharmacological intervention landscape against the biological failure modes that PAIS represents.

**Treatment as probe of mechanisms:** The pattern of which agents show promise — anti-inflammatory (HBOT, LDN, PEA, SSRIs), antifibrotic (pirfenidone, nintedanib), and metabolic/immunomodulatory (metformin) — provides indirect evidence that neuroinflammation, persistent immune activation, and tissue fibrosis are the dominant failure modes in PASC, consistent with the project's mechanistic framework.

**Failed recovery as the frame:** The negative result for NMV-r in established PASC (STOP-PASC trial) versus modest prevention during acute phase supports a key PAIS hypothesis: the window for intervention that prevents failed homeostatic recovery may be narrow and acute-phase-specific. Once the dysregulated state is established (persisting inflammation, fibrosis, neuroinflammation), direct antiviral treatment no longer restores homeostasis — supporting the concept of a locked-in post-infectious state distinct from ongoing viral replication.

**SSRI data as mechanistic evidence:** The differential effect of S1R-agonist SSRIs (29% RR reduction) versus non-S1R SSRIs (21% reduction) suggests that sigma-1 receptor modulation — which affects neuroinflammation, ER stress, and mitochondrial function — contributes to PASC pathophysiology beyond serotonin reuptake, pointing to neuroinflammatory and cellular-stress mechanisms the project should track.

**Antifibrotic evidence:** Directly connects to and complements paper:Vreeman2025 on post-COVID pulmonary fibrosis mechanisms and targets; confirms clinical benefit of nintedanib and pirfenidone with survival data.

**Cross-syndrome relevance (PAIS frame):** The treatments showing promise (LDN, HBOT, PEA) are also used or explored in ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, and PTLDS — conditions the project tracks as related PAIS entities — providing indirect evidence of shared pathological mechanisms (neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, immune dysregulation) across PAIS diagnoses.

## Limitations

1. **Scoping review design — no meta-analytic pooling:** The review explicitly does not pool effect sizes or conduct quantitative synthesis; findings are narrative. The 39 included studies are highly heterogeneous in design (RCTs, retrospective cohorts, cross-sectional surveys), PASC case definitions, populations, follow-up durations, and outcome measures.

2. **Narrow and inconsistent PASC case definitions:** No universal case definition was applied across the reviewed studies; WHO, CDC, and NIH definitions differ and were inconsistently applied by primary studies, making cross-study effect size comparisons unreliable.

3. **Literature cutoff July 22, 2024:** Substantial PASC trial data published since that date (including updated STOP-PASC analyses and ongoing RECOVER trial outputs) are not captured.

4. **Small study sizes for most agents:** Nearly all HBOT, LDN, and PEA studies involved n < 40–200 participants; effect estimates are imprecise and susceptible to selection bias.

5. **Confounding of acute-phase vs. established-PASC treatment:** The metformin evidence (COVID-OUT) and antiviral evidence conflate prevention (administered during acute infection) with treatment of established PASC; the review discusses this distinction but it is easily overlooked.

6. **Selection toward English-language publications:** Search was restricted to English, potentially excluding relevant non-English studies (particularly from Asia and Europe where PASC treatment trials are active).

7. **Eight-agent scope constraint:** The authors note they constrained the review to eight therapeutics that have been extensively studied and are practical for clinical use; emerging targets (e.g., antihistamines, mast-cell stabilizers, stellate ganglion block) are not covered.

8. **Risk-of-bias not systematically applied:** While the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist was used for quality assessment, the review is a scoping review and does not formally incorporate risk-of-bias in grading its conclusions.

## Model / Tool Availability

No computational model, dataset, or software tool is released. This is a clinical scoping review. Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 (referenced in paper) provide detailed extracted data on target symptoms, study designs, drug regimens, evaluation tools, and clinical outcomes for all 39 included studies.

## Follow-up

- **STOP-PASC trial (Geng et al. 2024, JAMA Internal Med):** The key negative RCT for NMV-r in established PASC — read to understand the patient selection criteria and what PASC subgroups were enrolled; null result informs the locked-in state hypothesis.
- **COVID-OUT trial (Bramante et al. 2023, Lancet Infect Dis):** The metformin prevention trial (n = 1,126); read for the full dosing protocol and subgroup analyses (especially whether prevention was uniform across COVID severity strata).
- **N3C SSRI analysis (Sidky et al. 2024):** The large database study (n = 17,908) on S1R-agonist SSRIs and PASC risk — evaluate whether the sigma-1 receptor mechanism holds in prospective data and whether it extends to other PAIS conditions.
- **Relationship to paper:Vreeman2025:** Both papers converge on antifibrotics for pulmonary PASC; Vreeman provides the mechanistic basis (ECM/TGF-β/ACE2) while Seo provides the clinical evidence (RCT data, survival curves). Together they represent the most complete current evidence for antifibrotic treatment in PASC.
- **Cross-PAIS question:** Do LDN, HBOT, or PEA show similar benefit in ME/CFS or PTLDS cohorts? If so, this would strongly support neuroinflammation as a shared PAIS mechanism. Review existing ME/CFS treatment trials for these agents.
- **Open mechanism question:** Is the efficacy differential between S1R-agonist and non-S1R SSRIs for PASC prevention replicable in a prospective RCT? If yes, sigma-1 receptor biology (ER stress response, mitochondrial calcium buffering, neuroinflammation) deserves promotion to the project's core mechanistic hypothesis set.
