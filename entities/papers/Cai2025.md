## Key Contribution

A large retrospective VA cohort study (n ≈ 836,913 total; 231,899 COVID-positive, 605,014 test-negative controls) showing that a positive SARS-CoV-2 test is associated with elevated rates of subsequent bacterial, viral, and fungal infections across the 12 months following acute illness. A separate hospitalized sub-cohort comparison (COVID-19-admitted vs. influenza-admitted) found higher rates of hospital readmission for infections, sepsis, and antimicrobial use in the COVID-19 arm. The findings constitute a population-level, cross-pathogen signal of post-COVID immune vulnerability in a real-world US veteran population — a finding that is consistent with, but does not demonstrate the mechanism behind, lasting immune dysregulation after SARS-CoV-2.

## Methods

**Study design:** Retrospective observational cohort using the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) electronic health record databases.

**Enrollment period:** November 1, 2021 – December 31, 2023. This window spans the Omicron-dominant era, after widespread vaccination; results are therefore more applicable to the contemporary post-vaccination context than the pre-vaccine Cai2024 cohort.

**Cohorts:**
- COVID-19-positive: n = 231,899 veterans with a confirmed positive SARS-CoV-2 test.
- Test-negative control: n = 605,014 veterans with a negative COVID-19 test (spatiotemporally aligned).
- Hospitalized sub-cohort: n = 12,450 hospitalized for COVID-19 vs. n = 3,293 hospitalized for seasonal influenza.

**Follow-up:** 12 months, beginning 30 days after the index test (through 12 months, death, or July 18, 2024).

**Outcomes:** Rates of incident bacterial, viral, and fungal infections, assessed via outpatient diagnosis, hospital admission, microbiological culture results (blood, respiratory, urine biospecimens), and antimicrobial use. Sepsis was a prespecified hospitalization outcome.

**Comparators:**
1. *Primary:* Test-negative controls (for the full outpatient + inpatient non-hospitalized analysis).
2. *Secondary:* Influenza-hospitalized veterans (for the hospitalized COVID-19 sub-cohort analysis) — provides a more biologically comparable comparison than test-negative, because influenza is also a respiratory infection causing acute immune activation.

**Confounding control:** Full methodological details are [INACCESSIBLE] (full text paywalled). VA propensity-score or inverse-probability-weighting approaches are standard in this Al-Aly group's prior work; specific covariates and model specifications cannot be confirmed at abstract level.

**Statistical model:** Rate ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. The metric is labeled "RR" in the abstract.

**Population note:** The VA cohort is predominantly older and male (≥85% male in prior Al-Aly VA studies); generalizability to women, younger adults, and non-veteran populations is limited.

## Key Findings

All statistics below are from the published abstract (confirmed via PubMed), except where marked [UNVERIFIED].

**COVID-19-positive vs. test-negative controls (outpatient + inpatient, full cohort):**

| Outcome | RR (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Outpatient diagnosis of infectious illness | 1.17 (1.15–1.19) |
| Respiratory infections | 1.46 (1.43–1.50) |
| Hospital admission for infectious illness | 1.41 (1.37–1.45) |

**Hospitalized COVID-19 vs. hospitalized seasonal influenza:**

| Outcome | RR (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Hospital admission for infectious illness | 1.24 (1.10–1.40) |
| Hospital admission for sepsis | 1.35 (1.11–1.63) |
| In-hospital antimicrobial use | 1.23 (1.10–1.37) |

All RRs in both tables above are quoted directly from the published abstract (PMID 40185115).

**Infection types elevated:** The abstract reports elevated rates across bacterial, fungal, and viral infections — the non-hospitalized arm's outpatient elevation is explicitly worded "bacterial, fungal, and viral infections" — with named viral reactivations including Epstein–Barr virus and herpes simplex virus; the pattern is broad-spectrum rather than limited to a single pathogen class.

## Relevance

This paper is the primary population-level quantitative evidence in the project for the claim that COVID-19 elevates subsequent susceptibility to a broad range of secondary infections, consistent with the hypothesis of post-COVID immune vulnerability or exhaustion.

**`hypothesis:0003` (immune exhaustion feedback loop):** This is the most directly relevant linkage. An immune-exhaustion hypothesis predicts exactly this cross-pathogen vulnerability — CD8+ T cells and innate immune cells chronically activated and then exhausted by persistent antigen or viral-reactivation signals would fail to suppress diverse pathogen classes. The finding of elevated bacterial, viral, and fungal infection rates across the 12-month follow-up is consistent with (but does not prove) this mechanism. Critically, the effect extends beyond long-COVID patients to the broader COVID-positive population, which is the populationscale framing of `hypothesis:0003`. This paper should be read alongside Pedroso2024 (direct T-cell exhaustion/senescence measurement after COVID, supporting the mechanism) and Cheong2023 (epigenetic HSC reprogramming, antigen-independent maintenance route).

**`hypothesis:0001` (shared dysregulated attractor):** The cross-pathogen breadth of the signal — bacterial, viral, and fungal — is notable. A narrow innate immune defect might explain susceptibility to one class; a broad signal across pathogen classes is more consistent with a systemic immune-state shift. This supports the shared-attractor framing (though cannot distinguish a SARS-CoV-2-specific attractor shift from a general post-severe-infection vulnerability).

**Comparison to influenza:** The secondary hospitalized analysis using an influenza-hospitalized comparator is important because it partially controls for the healthcare-seeking behavior confounder that affects test-negative designs: both groups sought care for respiratory illness severe enough to require hospitalization. The residual signal (RR 1.24 for infections, RR 1.35 for sepsis) above influenza suggests COVID-19 specifically — not just respiratory illness severity in general — carries excess infection risk.

**Tsergas2025 (citing context):** The Tsergas2025 BMJ news feature cited this paper as one of five key primary studies supporting the immune-disruption vs. immunity-debt debate, noting the 830,000-veteran infection-vulnerability signal. This project holds Tsergas2025 as a secondary pointer; the present paper is the primary source.

**D-001 / D-003 scope:** This paper belongs to this project (trigger is acute COVID-19 infection) rather than `health-immunity` (which would hold the general immune-competence mechanism literature). The finding of cross-pathogen vulnerability is a PAIS-epidemiology result that informs `hypothesis:0003` rather than a general immunology question.

## Limitations

1. **VA cohort generalizability:** The study population is overwhelmingly male (≥85% in prior Al-Aly VA studies) and older. The sex bias is particularly important for PAIS research, where women are disproportionately affected by long COVID; the male-skewed cohort may underestimate sex-specific immune-vulnerability effects. [UNVERIFIED — exact sex breakdown for this cohort]

2. **Test-negative control confounding:** The primary comparator is a test-negative group, which introduces differential care-seeking bias: COVID-negative individuals who sought testing may not be a perfect counterfactual for COVID-positive individuals (e.g., they may be systematically healthier, sicker, or have different testing behaviors). This is a known limitation of test-negative designs.

3. **Healthcare-utilization confounder:** COVID-positive individuals may have more healthcare contact post-infection (follow-up visits, concern about COVID sequelae), increasing ascertainment of incidental infections that would not have been detected in the control group. This is a standard limitation for VA administrative database studies.

4. **Causal attribution:** This is an observational association study. The observed elevated infection rates are consistent with immune vulnerability, but cannot exclude confounding by residual severity differences, healthcare access patterns, or unmeasured patient characteristics. No mechanistic data are presented.

5. **Influenza comparator limitations:** For the hospitalized sub-cohort, the influenza comparator is imperfect: COVID-19 and influenza hospitalized patients differ in mean age, comorbidity burden, variant era, and treatment (COVID patients may receive dexamethasone or antivirals, which themselves affect immune function). The net direction of these confounders on the RR estimates is unclear. [INACCESSIBLE — full propensity adjustment details not available at abstract level]

6. **Enrollment era (Omicron):** The study covers November 2021–December 2023, spanning primarily the Omicron era in a partially vaccinated population. Results may not generalize to the ancestral-SARS-CoV-2 / pre-vaccine era (cf. Cai2024) or to future variant landscapes. Vaccination and antiviral treatment (Paxlovid) effects on the infection-vulnerability signal cannot be disentangled at abstract level.

7. **Follow-up limited to 12 months:** Unlike Cai2024 (3-year follow-up), this study covers only 12 months post-infection. Whether the elevated infection susceptibility persists beyond 12 months is not reported.

8. **Severity and hospitalization sub-cohort caveats:** The secondary analysis of hospitalized COVID-19 patients is informative but mechanistically ambiguous: hospitalized patients are inherently sicker, may have received immunosuppressive treatments (corticosteroids), and face additional nosocomial exposure. The subsequent infection elevation may partly reflect iatrogenic effects rather than intrinsic SARS-CoV-2 immune dysregulation.

9. **Full methods and sensitivity analyses are [INACCESSIBLE]:** Propensity score specification, covariate balance, negative outcome controls, and sensitivity analyses are not available from the abstract alone.

## Follow-up

**Primary papers to cross-reference:**

- **Pedroso et al. (J Leukoc Biol 2024)** — Direct T-cell exhaustion and senescence measurements after COVID-19, even in mild infection; the most direct mechanistic support for the exhaustion arm explaining this study's population-level vulnerability signal.
- **Cheong et al. (Cell 2023)** — Epigenetic reprogramming of haematopoietic stem/progenitor cells after SARS-CoV-2; antigen-independent maintenance mechanism for immune dysregulation consistent with the broad cross-pathogen vulnerability shown here.
- **Wang et al. (Open Forum Infect Dis 2024)** — Ontario viral cohort: higher bacterial co-infection rates in COVID-19 vs. influenza or RSV; corroborates the cross-pathogen elevation in a different health-system context.
- **Cai2024** — the 3-year PASC outcomes study from the same Al-Aly group; this paper is complementary (it addresses broader multi-organ PASC sequelae but does not focus on infection susceptibility specifically).

**Questions raised for the project:**

- Is the cross-pathogen infection susceptibility signal driven predominantly by the severely ill / hospitalized COVID-19 patients, or is it also present in outpatient / mild-COVID survivors? The abstract reports both a full-cohort result and a hospitalized sub-cohort result, but does not break the full-cohort result down by acute severity. [INACCESSIBLE — requires full text]
- Does vaccination status or antiviral treatment modulate the subsequent infection risk, given the Omicron-era enrollment window?
- Is the viral-reactivation signal (EBV, HSV) distinct in magnitude or timing from the bacterial infection signal? If EBV reactivation peaks earlier and bacterial infections later, this would suggest a sequence of events consistent with the exhaustion loop (EBV reactivation → further exhaustion → bacteremia susceptibility).
- How does the 12-month absolute excess infection burden compare to the per-person DALY burden from the same group's Cai2024 3-year follow-up? This could anchor the infection-susceptibility arm of PAIS as a quantifiable population-level contribution to DALY burden.
