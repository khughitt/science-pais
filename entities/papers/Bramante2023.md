# Outpatient treatment of COVID-19 and incidence of post-COVID-19 condition over 10 months (COVID-OUT): a multicentre, randomised, quadruple-blind, parallel-group, phase 3 trial

- **Authors:** Carolyn T. Bramante, John B. Buse, David M. Liebovitz, Jacinda M. Nicklas, Michael A. Puskarich, Ken Cohen, Hrishikesh K. Belani, Blake J. Anderson, Jared D. Huling, Christopher J. Tignanelli, Jennifer L. Thompson, Matthew Pullen, and the COVID-OUT Study Team
- **Year:** 2023
- **Journal:** The Lancet Infectious Diseases, vol. 23, no. 9
- **DOI:** 10.1016/S1473-3099(23)00299-2
- **PMID:** 37302406
- **BibTeX key:** Bramante2023
- **Source:** Full-text PDF (papers/pdfs/2023_Bramante_metformin-covid-out-postcovid-incidence-rct.pdf), read 2026-06-20 (supersedes earlier read).

## Key Contribution

The COVID-OUT trial is the strongest randomised controlled evidence that early outpatient pharmacologic treatment can prevent long COVID. Among three candidate drugs tested in a quadruple-blind factorial design, **metformin** reduced the incidence of a provider-diagnosed long-COVID outcome by approximately 41% relative risk reduction (RRR) over 10 months (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.39–0.89; p = 0.012). When started fewer than 4 days after symptom onset the benefit was larger (HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.15–0.95). **Ivermectin** and **fluvoxamine** showed no effect. This establishes proof of concept that a metabolically targeted, globally available, inexpensive generic drug can reduce PASC incidence if given early, and uniquely distinguishes *prevention* from *treatment* of established long COVID. It provides direct human RCT evidence implicating metabolic/mitochondrial disruption during the acute phase as an upstream driver of chronic post-infectious illness.

## Methods

**Design.** Decentralised, multicentre (six US sites), randomised, quadruple-blind (participants, investigators, care providers, outcome assessors all masked), parallel-group, phase 3 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04510194). 2×3 factorial randomisation: six arms in a 1:1:1:1:1:1 allocation — metformin + ivermectin; metformin + fluvoxamine; metformin + placebo; ivermectin + placebo; fluvoxamine + placebo; placebo + placebo. Each participant received two types of pill to maintain masking; all participants received metformin or matching placebo, and a subset additionally received ivermectin, fluvoxamine, or their matching placebos. The fluvoxamine arm was closed early (January 7, 2022) by the independent data and safety monitoring board.

**Population.** Adults aged 30–85 years with overweight or obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m², or ≥23 kg/m² for people identifying as Asian or Latino), COVID-19 symptoms for <7 days, and a documented SARS-CoV-2 positive PCR or antigen test within 3 days before enrolment. No previous SARS-CoV-2 infection was permitted. Pregnant and lactating women were included (assigned only to metformin or placebo, not to ivermectin/fluvoxamine arms). Enrolled December 30, 2020 – January 28, 2022. N = 6,602 assessed for eligibility; 1,431 enrolled and randomly assigned; 1,323 received ≥1 dose (modified intention-to-treat, mITT); 1,126 consented for long-term follow-up and completed ≥1 post-day-180 survey (564 received metformin, 562 received matched placebo). 1,074 (95.4%) of 1,126 completed ≥9 months of follow-up.

**Key demographic features.** Median age 45 years (IQR 37–54); median BMI 29.8 kg/m² (IQR 27.0–34.2); 56.1% female (44 [7.0%] of 632 women pregnant); 55.0% (619/1126) had received a primary SARS-CoV-2 vaccination series before enrolment; 5.1% had received an initial 2021 monovalent booster. Race: 83.2% White, 7.4% Black, 3.7% Asian, 1.6% Native American. Median time from symptom onset to study drug initiation was 5 days (IQR 4–6); 519 (46.8%) of 1108 participants started within 4 days. Dominant variant at randomisation: alpha 5.6%, delta 71.1%, omicron 23.3%.

**Study drug dosing.** Metformin: titrated over 6 days (500 mg day 1; 500 mg twice daily days 2–5; then 500 mg morning + 1000 mg evening, up to day 14). Ivermectin: 390–470 µg/kg per day for 3 days (median 430 µg/kg per day). Fluvoxamine: 50 mg on day 1 followed by 50 mg twice daily up to day 14.

**Primary endpoint (acute illness).** Severe COVID-19 by day 14 — previously published separately.

**Long-COVID secondary endpoint (this paper).** Provider-diagnosed long COVID (post-COVID-19 condition) as a prespecified long-term secondary outcome. Ascertainment: participants were asked in monthly follow-up surveys (days 180, 210, 240, 270, 300) whether a medical provider had given them a diagnosis of long COVID; a calendar prompt captured the date, and diagnoses were confirmed via medical record review in the EHR. Assessment for long COVID was not in the original 2020 protocol; it was added to the protocol April 23, 2021, and survey tools were IRB-approved July 2021.

**Case definition for long COVID.** Participant-reported receipt of a provider-diagnosed long-COVID label, confirmed in the EHR. This was chosen as a balance between sensitivity and specificity: WHO defines long COVID as continuation or development of new symptoms 3 months after initial infection lasting ≥2 months with no other explanation; the trial did not require symptom duration criteria. The outcome is thus a clinician-assigned diagnostic label, not a research-criteria symptom endpoint — note the limitation below. The definition was adopted because (a) it requires active clinical judgement to exclude other explanations, and (b) EHR codes for long COVID have low specificity and ICD-10 coding for long COVID was not added until October 2021. Most diagnoses (72/93, 78%) were made by primary care providers.

**Statistical analysis.** Time-to-event analysis using Cox proportional-hazards models with adjustment for baseline vaccination status and receipt of other study drugs in the factorial randomisation, as prespecified. Kaplan-Meier estimates of cumulative incidence at 60-day intervals. Log-rank test (two-sided α = 0.05). No correction for multiple comparisons across arms (factorial design with distinct parallel treatments renders correction non-indicated per the analysis plan). Participants who did not report long COVID and completed the day-300 survey were censored on day 300; those who did not complete the day-300 survey were censored at the date of the last long-COVID survey completed. Prespecified subgroup analyses: sex, BMI, symptom-onset-to-treatment timing (<4 days vs ≥4 days), age, dominant SARS-CoV-2 variant, vaccination status, and study group (factorial cell).

## Key Findings

**Metformin arm (primary result of this paper).**
- Raw events: 35/564 (6.2%) metformin vs. 58/562 (10.3%) matched placebo.
- Cumulative incidence by day 300: 6.3% (95% CI 4.2–8.2%) metformin vs. 10.4% (95% CI 7.8–12.9%) placebo.
- Absolute risk reduction: 4.1 percentage points (95% CI 0.9–7.4).
- Relative risk reduction: ~41%.
- HR 0.59 (95% CI 0.39–0.89); log-rank p = 0.012.
- The adjusted HR (controlling for baseline vaccination status and other study drugs) did not appreciably change the estimate.
- Benefit was consistent across all prespecified subgroups; no significant interaction terms (p-interaction all >0.05).
- **Early-treatment effect (prespecified subgroup):** when metformin was started in **fewer than 4 days** (<4 days) of symptom onset (n = 130 metformin, 144 placebo), HR = 0.37 (95% CI 0.15–0.95); for those who started ≥4 days after symptom onset (n = 427 metformin, 407 placebo), HR = 0.66 (95% CI 0.41–1.06). The greater benefit with earlier initiation is consistent with an antiviral mechanism operative early in infection. (Note: 130/564 [23.3%] metformin participants and 144/562 [26.1%] placebo participants started within <4 days.)
- **Metformin vs. placebo only subgroup** (excluding factorial cells with active ivermectin/fluvoxamine): HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.23–0.98); n = 221 metformin + placebo vs. 229 placebo + placebo.
- Variant-era consistency: HR 0.68 (0.42–1.12) during delta-dominant period; HR 0.45 (0.18–1.11) during omicron-dominant period; HR 0.21 (0.02–1.87) during alpha-dominant period (small n). Effect appears consistent across variant eras.
- Vaccination status: HR 0.44 (0.24–0.80) among unvaccinated; HR 0.85 (0.46–1.57) among vaccinated — point estimate weaker in vaccinated (smaller cell, wide CI, p-interaction = 0.12, not significant).

**Ivermectin arm.** Cumulative incidence 7.7% (95% CI 5.0–10.4%) with ivermectin vs. 8.1% (95% CI 5.3–10.9%) with matched placebo. HR 0.95 (95% CI 0.57–1.59) — no effect on long-COVID incidence. HR consistent across subgroups.

**Fluvoxamine arm.** Cumulative incidence 10.1% (95% CI 6.6–13.5%) with fluvoxamine vs. 7.5% (95% CI 4.4–10.4%) with matched placebo. HR 1.36 (95% CI 0.78–2.35) — no effect; point estimate directionally unfavorable (not statistically significant; likely chance given no mechanistic expectation for harm). HR consistent across subgroups.

**Overall long-COVID incidence.** 93 of 1,126 (8.3%) participants reported a provider-diagnosed long-COVID diagnosis by day 300. Most diagnoses made by primary care providers (72/93, 78%); remainder by urgent care, emergency department, specialists (cardiologists, neurologists, infectious disease, pulmonologists, otolaryngologists), and other providers.

**Symptom burden.** Participants who received a long-COVID diagnosis were markedly more likely to report at least one symptom affecting ability to work or leisure (~55–60% vs ~10%) across the entire follow-up period. Most common symptoms among those diagnosed: tiredness, difficulty focusing, difficulty sleeping, shortness of breath, headache, feeling depressed, loss of appetite, loss of sense of taste.

## Relevance

**To PAIS research generally.** This is the most rigorous RCT evidence to date that a pharmacologic intervention given during acute COVID-19 reduces long-COVID incidence. It directly tests the hypothesis that acute-phase processes are causally upstream of PASC and shows that intercepting them can alter the trajectory. The null results for ivermectin and fluvoxamine sharpen the inference: the effect is specific to metformin's mechanisms, not attributable to any early outpatient treatment effect or to antiviral activity generically.

**Mechanism implication.** Metformin's principal pharmacological actions include AMPK activation, inhibition of mitochondrial complex I, anti-inflammatory effects (reduced NF-κB signalling, reduced cytokine production), antiviral properties (mTOR suppression reduces viral replication), and reduction of cellular senescence and fibrin-clot formation. The effect cannot disambiguate which of these is operative, but the result is consistent with both: (a) a metabolic/mitochondrial disruption during acute infection priming the PASC state, and (b) metformin reducing viral burden or the immunopathological response that seeds chronic illness. The null ivermectin result rules out a simple antiviral-load explanation for metformin's benefit; the null fluvoxamine result rules out a simple anti-inflammatory/serotonergic explanation.

**Prevention vs. treatment distinction.** COVID-OUT addresses *prevention* of long COVID by early acute-phase treatment — a fundamentally different question from treating established PASC. Results here cannot be extrapolated to treating patients who already have long COVID. This is critical for t004 (therapeutics task): the therapeutic target is the acute window.

**Connects to `question:0012`.** Provides the best current RCT evidence that early acute-phase intervention reduces PAIS incidence, upgrading this question from observational inference to randomised proof of concept for a metabolic agent.

**Connects to `question:0011` and the metabolic/mitochondrial thread.** The metformin effect supports a role for acute metabolic disruption (mitochondrial dysfunction, mTOR/AMPK signalling, energetic stress) as a pathway into PASC. Metformin's complex-I inhibition and AMPK activation are directly relevant to the bioenergetic failure framing in `question:0011-mitochondrial-basis-of-pem`.

## Project Framework Mapping

**Hypothesis 0001 (shared dysregulated attractor).** The trial provides indirect support: if the attractor is entered via acute immunopathological/metabolic events, then a metabolic intervention during that window should reduce attractor-entry probability — which is what the data show. The null ivermectin result (antiparasitic with some antiviral activity) is consistent with the multi-loop attractor framing requiring more than viral clearance alone. The fluvoxamine null challenges a simple serotonergic/anti-inflammatory single-node explanation.

**Metabolic/mitochondrial mechanism thread.** The specificity of the metformin effect is the project's most direct RCT anchor for the claim that metabolic disruption during acute infection is causally upstream of chronic illness. This is the strongest interventional evidence yet in the project's literature for this thread; prior evidence was observational or mechanistic (Che2025, Naviaux2016-class proposals).

**Hypothesis 0004 (acute severity threshold).** COVID-OUT restricted enrolment to mild-to-moderate outpatients with overweight/obesity — a population with elevated PASC risk but not the most severe hospitalised patients. The early-treatment hazard ratio for those starting <4 days from symptom onset (HR 0.37, 95% CI 0.15–0.95) vs. ≥4 days (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.41–1.06) is consistent with a time-limited window in which acute-phase processes that set the PASC threshold are still interruptible.

## Limitations

1. **Long-COVID case definition is methodologically weak.** The primary outcome is participant-reported receipt of a provider-diagnosed long-COVID label confirmed in the EHR — subject to ascertainment bias (provider diagnostic practices, awareness, and access vary), healthcare-access differences, and label heterogeneity. The trial did not use a standardised research case definition requiring specific symptom duration (e.g. WHO: new or continuing symptoms at 3 months lasting ≥2 months). The authors explicitly chose this endpoint as a balance between sensitivity and specificity given the rapidly changing definition of long COVID when the protocol was written. Importantly, providers and participants were both masked, which limits differential ascertainment between arms. This is the principal inferential limitation of the long-COVID endpoint.

2. **Population specificity.** Restricted to adults with overweight or obesity (BMI ≥25, or ≥23 for Asian/Latino), ages 30–85, with no prior SARS-CoV-2 infection. Metformin's benefit may not generalise to normal-weight individuals, younger adults, paediatric populations, or people with prior infection. Overweight/obese adults are a pre-selected elevated-PASC-risk group, and the effect size may not replicate in lower-risk populations. Trial population was predominantly White (83%) and female (56%), limiting generalisability to more diverse groups; 12.3% identified as Hispanic or Latino.

3. **Enrolment era and variant heterogeneity.** Enrolment spanned pre-Delta through early Omicron (December 2020 – January 2022). The majority (71%) were enrolled during the delta-dominant period. Vaccination rates at enrolment were 55% (primary series). The study pre-dated widespread use of antivirals (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir). Subgroup HRs by variant era and vaccination status are directionally consistent with the overall estimate but are underpowered (wide CIs); p-interaction values were all non-significant (0.12–0.73).

4. **Mechanism opacity.** The trial cannot identify which of metformin's pleiotropic mechanisms mediates PASC prevention. AMPK activation, mitochondrial complex-I inhibition, mTOR suppression (reducing viral translation), anti-inflammatory effects (NF-κB, IL-1β, IL-18), inhibition of senescent phenotype induction, and antifibrin/microclot effects are all candidate mechanisms — they are not separable by this design.

5. **Fluvoxamine null result and arm closure.** The HR of 1.36 for fluvoxamine (non-significant) should not be interpreted as evidence of harm. The fluvoxamine arm was closed early by the DSMB (January 7, 2022), reducing power for that comparison. Prior signals for fluvoxamine in acute COVID severity (TOGETHER trial) used different doses (100 mg twice daily vs. 50 mg twice daily here) and earlier initiation; the null long-COVID result is not directly comparable.

6. **Factorial design and event-rate asymmetry in control arms.** About 50% of participants in the ivermectin and fluvoxamine control groups also received active metformin; the event rate in the ivermectin and fluvoxamine control groups was therefore lower than in the metformin placebo group, which appropriately facilitates the factorial evaluation but means the overall placebo event rate (8.3%) overestimates the "true no-treatment" long-COVID incidence. No formal interaction test between factors was significant.

7. **Selection bias in long-COVID follow-up.** Participants who chose to enrol in clinical trials and complete 10 months of follow-up surveys may differ systematically from the general population affected by COVID-19 and long COVID. The 77 participants who completed the trial before long-COVID consent was added were not reconsented for long-COVID follow-up.

8. **Late addition of long-COVID endpoint.** The long-COVID secondary outcome was added to the protocol in April 2021 (approximately 16 months into the trial) and was not in the original 2020 protocol; participants enrolled before this addition were reconsented. The delay reflects the novelty of long COVID as a recognized clinical entity in 2020 but introduces a protocol-amendment caveat.

## Follow-up

- **Mechanism disentanglement.** Which of metformin's mechanisms (AMPK, complex I, mTOR, anti-inflammatory, antifibrinolytic) mediates PASC prevention? Biomarker substudies or dose-response designs needed.
- **Generalisation.** Does metformin's effect hold in normal-weight populations, fully vaccinated cohorts, or different SARS-CoV-2 variants (including Omicron subvariants dominant post-enrolment)? Does it generalise to other PAIS (post-influenza, post-EBV)?
- **Case definition.** Studies using standardised PASC case definitions (symptom-based, functional, or biomarker-anchored) needed to validate the provider-diagnosis endpoint used here.
- **Combination therapy.** Given the attractor-model prediction that no single node is sufficient (hypothesis:0001), does combining metformin with an antiviral (e.g. nirmatrelvir) or immunomodulator further reduce PASC incidence?
- **Treatment window.** The <4-day-from-onset HR (0.37) vs. ≥4-day HR (0.66) vs. overall HR (0.59) suggests strong time-dependence. Prospective characterisation of the precise interruptible window and dose-timing interaction is needed.
- **Connects to `question:0012`:** This trial should be listed as the primary supporting evidence for the possibility that early acute intervention reduces PASC incidence, upgrading that question's evidence tier from observational inference to RCT proof of concept.
