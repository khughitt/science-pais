## Key Contribution

This systematic review synthesizes the first comprehensive evidence base (57 peer-reviewed articles + 4 grey literature documents) on Q-fever fatigue syndrome (QFS) — persistent fatigue following acute *Coxiella burnetii* infection. It establishes that approximately 20% of patients remain chronically fatigued, documents a broad symptom constellation spanning musculoskeletal, neurocognitive, autonomic, and psychiatric domains, and identifies critical knowledge gaps in definition, aetiology, and treatment. The review proposes a uniform QFS case definition (based on the Dutch national guideline) and advocates standardised measurement tools to enable cross-study comparison.

## Methods

- **Design:** Systematic literature review following PRISMA guidelines; databases Medline/PubMed, Embase, and PsycInfo searched through 26 May 2015.
- **Inclusion:** 57 peer-reviewed articles and 4 grey literature documents; classified into five domains: diagnosis (n = 4), background/descriptive (n = 29), aetiology (n = 18), prevention/therapy (n = 6), prognosis (n = 0, no studies available).
- **Quality assessment:** Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort/case-control studies; Evers checklist for economic evaluations; an 18-criteria tool (score ≥14 = acceptable) for case-series; CoCanCPG criteria for case-reports. Overall methodological quality was rated low to moderate across the included literature.
- **Case definition used:** The Dutch QFS guideline (RIVM 2012) requires: severe fatigue causing significant disability for ≥6 months, temporal relationship with confirmed acute Q-fever, absence of fatigue before infection (or significant worsening), and exclusion of co-morbidity as the primary cause.

## Key Findings

### Epidemiology and natural history

- Most patients recover within 6–12 months; approximately **20%** remain chronically fatigued (QFS).
- Prevalence estimates span widely: 8–28% meeting CFS criteria at 5–14 years post-infection vs. 0% in uninfected controls; up to **69% reporting any fatigue 5 years post-infection**.
- Fatigue followed *C. burnetii* (and EBV and Ross River virus) at similar rates across 6-week, 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month time points (~35%, 27%, 12%, 9% respectively), suggesting a shared post-infectious trajectory. The QFS trajectory is described as mostly continuous, but relapsing/remitting patterns also occur; fatigue may persist up to 20 years.
- Twelve to 26 months post-infection, ~40% of patients reported additional non-fatigue complaints.

### Symptom phenotype

QFS is accompanied by a cluster of nonspecific but consistent symptoms:
- **Musculoskeletal:** myalgia and arthralgia reported in up to 70% of laboratory-confirmed patients; arthralgia reported in 69% at 6 years.
- **Neurocognitive:** concentration and memory difficulties in 4–24% at 12–26 months; decreased concentration/mental acuity persisting 5–10 years.
- **Sleep:** disturbed sleep patterns in 65% at 6 years; unrefreshing sleep frequently noted.
- **Headache:** frequent across studies; present in 47% at 6 years; severity (not frequency) significantly higher than controls.
- **Night sweats:** in up to 53% compared to controls; most QFS patients report this symptom for 5–10 years.
- **Respiratory:** breathlessness in 51%, cough in 59%, chest pain in 51% at 6 years — all significantly more frequent than controls.
- **Mood:** depression in ~10% of cases; significant psychosocial distress and somatisation observed; cytokine abnormalities postulated as a biological substrate.

### Burden and functional impact

- At 12 months, **50% of patients had reduced general quality of life**; SF-36 domain scores remained lower than controls at 27 months in 52%.
- Work absence: 40% absent >1 month post-infection; 9% unable to function at pre-morbid levels at 12–26 months; 30% had not fully resumed daily activities at 26 months, 81% of these due to fatigue.
- Burden of disease quantified as >8 times higher than influenza on a per-case basis; QFS was one of the largest Q-fever-related economic costs during the Dutch 2007–2010 outbreak.

### Aetiology

- **Pathogen persistence:** Detection of low-level *C. burnetii* DNA in bone marrow, liver biopsies, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells supports ongoing immune stimulation. One core hypothesis: a non-viable, undegraded *C. burnetii* immunomodulatory complex (containing DNA or antigens) impairs macrophage clearance, generates persistent pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and drives fatigue. Contradictory results exist regarding detection of this DNA across cohorts.
- **Cytokine dysregulation:** QFS PBMCs showed aberrant cytokine release patterns — accentuated IL-6 release, decreased IL-2 responders, decreased IFNγ responders. In vitro: increased IL-6 and IL-10, decreased IL-2. A significant correlation between IL-6 and total symptom score was found. However, markers of inflammation did not remain altered in patients with post-infective fatigue in some studies, leaving no clear immunological consensus.
- **Genetic susceptibility:** HLA-DR-11 frequency significantly increased in QFS vs. controls; polymorphisms in NRAMP1 and IFNγ genes found; IFNy+874T/A SNP predicted increased fatigue after several infections including *C. burnetii*; IL-10-592C/A (C allele) was protective for neurocognitive difficulties. Results overall contradictory.
- **Autonomic dysfunction:** Post-infective fatigue associated with heightened heartbeat discrimination accuracy, increased resting heart rate, decreased heart rate variability, lower pressure-pain threshold — interpreted as autonomic hyper-vigilance with inflexible physiological response.
- **(Bio)psychological factors:** Some studies support biopsychosocial aetiology (somatisation, higher hypochondriac tendencies); others find psychological distress not an important explanatory factor. No consensus reached.

### Predictors of QFS

- Severity of acute illness is the most consistent predictor of long-term poor health status and QFS.
- Pre-existing health problems and hospitalisation during acute infection also predict worse outcomes.
- Neither psychological nor microbial factors reliably predict post-infective fatigue; no relationship found between antibody titres and fatigue at 6 years.
- Absence of symptoms during acute infection was not protective for persistent symptoms.

### Prevention and treatment

- No RCTs for QFS prevention found.
- Long-term antibiotic trials (minocycline, doxycycline, levofloxacin) showed inconsistent and mostly unsupported efficacy; evidence is insufficient to recommend antibiotic therapy for QFS.
- CBT and graded exercise therapy (GET) recommended in the Dutch guideline (extrapolated from CFS evidence); RCT investigating doxycycline + CBT in QFS patients was ongoing at time of publication.
- Dutch guideline's preventive advice: stay active, alternate activities, maintain sleep-wake regularity, avoid focusing on fatigue.

## Relevance

This paper is directly relevant to research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes and the PAIS frame of failed homeostatic recovery for several reasons:

1. **QFS as a canonical PAIS instance.** With a known trigger (*C. burnetii*), a documented ~20% failure-to-recover rate, and follow-up extending to 20 years, QFS provides one of the best-characterised natural histories of failed post-infectious recovery available. It validates the core project claim that a subset of patients systematically fail to resolve homeostasis after acute infection.

2. **Cross-pathogen convergence.** QFS shares temporal prevalence curves and symptom phenotypes (fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, neurocognitive difficulties, sleep disturbance) with post-EBV and post-Ross River virus fatigue, strengthening the "shared failure mode" hypothesis central to this project's cross-syndrome synthesis frame.

3. **Antigen persistence as a mechanistic driver.** The immunomodulatory complex hypothesis — non-viable *C. burnetii* DNA/antigens impeding macrophage clearance and maintaining cytokine dysregulation — maps precisely onto the project's antigen/pathogen-persistence candidate mechanism for PAIS.

4. **Cytokine dysregulation without sustained elevation.** The finding that pro-inflammatory markers (IL-6, CRP) are elevated acutely but do not remain persistently altered in QFS patients complicates simple "ongoing inflammation" models; it suggests that initial immune dysregulation may program downstream dysfunction without requiring continuous inflammatory activity — relevant to the project's question of how homeostatic failure is maintained.

5. **Diagnostic ambiguity as a shared PAIS challenge.** The absence of a uniform international QFS definition — and the 20+ synonymous terms in use — mirrors the broader PAIS field problem of inconsistent case definitions (e.g., long COVID, ME/CFS) that hinders cross-study comparison and confounds prevalence estimates.

6. **Severity of acute phase as predictor.** The convergent finding that acute illness severity predicts long-term sequelae aligns with project hypotheses about initial immune insult magnitude setting homeostatic recovery trajectories.

## Limitations

- **No prognosis domain.** Zero studies were found on QFS prognosis, leaving the long-term natural history trajectory after the chronic phase entirely uncharacterised.
- **Low/moderate study quality.** Most cohort/descriptive studies scored low on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale; few studies had adequate controls or corrected for confounders.
- **No uniform QFS definition** across studies, making prevalence estimates incomparable. Definitions ranged from self-reported fatigue to formal CFS criteria to the Dutch guideline's 6-month threshold.
- **Heterogeneous outcome measures.** Different fatigue scales, health status instruments, and cut-offs across studies preclude direct quantitative synthesis; no meta-analysis was possible.
- **Most studies from a single research group** (Radboud/Dutch outbreak context), limiting generalisability to other outbreak settings, populations, and *C. burnetii* strains.
- **No RCT evidence** for QFS treatment; antibiotic and CBT/GET recommendations borrowed from CFS literature without direct QFS validation.
- **Confounding from Dutch outbreak context:** Most descriptive data derive from the 2007–2010 Netherlands epidemic, which involved a specific goat-farm exposure route and predominantly symptomatic patients, potentially over-representing severe cases.
- **Systematic monitoring of accompanying symptoms absent:** prospective data for non-fatigue symptoms were not available in most studies, preventing temporal mapping of symptom emergence relative to the infection.
- **No biological biomarker validated** for QFS diagnosis; reliance on clinical criteria only.

## Model / Tool Availability

No computational model or software tool is described or released. The paper proposes a uniform case definition and standardised measurement instruments (Checklist Individual Strength or Chalder Fatigue Scale; NCSI, SF-36, or Sickness Impact Profile) for future use.

## Follow-up

- **Keijmel et al. 2017 (The Qure Study):** RCT of doxycycline + CBT in QFS patients; cited as ongoing — follow the outcome of this trial (ref [42] in the paper).
- **Marmion et al. (QMJ 2005):** Long-term persistence of *C. burnetii* after acute primary Q-fever (ref [21]) — core antigen-persistence mechanistic paper.
- **Sukocheva et al. (QMJ 2010):** Long-term persistence of non-infective *Coxiella burnetii* cell components and antigens (ref [36]) — directly supports the immunomodulatory complex hypothesis.
- **Piraino & Vollmer-Conna (2012):** Genetic associations of fatigue domains post-acute sickness (ref [60]) — IFNy+874T/A and IL-10 SNP associations with QFS symptoms.
- **Kadota et al. (Biol Psychol 2010):** Autonomic hyper-vigilance in post-infective fatigue (ref [46]) — mechanisms underlying dysautonomia-like phenotype in QFS.
- **Cross-syndrome comparison:** This paper explicitly links QFS to post-EBV, post-Ross River virus, and ME/CFS; a cross-pathogen comparison synthesis drawing on Choutka2022 and Bai2023 (already in the project) would be valuable.
