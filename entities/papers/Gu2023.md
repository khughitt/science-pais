## Key Contribution

This Perspective article proposes that severe SARS-CoV-2 infection induces "trained immunity" — durable epigenetic reprogramming of monocytes and their upstream hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) — and that this maladaptive reprogramming constitutes a central mechanism driving the chronic inflammatory burden of long COVID / PASC. It synthesizes emerging evidence (primarily Cheong et al. 2023) to argue that IL-6, acting through STAT3, is the key upstream cytokine imprinting durable chromatin changes in HSPCs that propagate a hyperreactive myeloid phenotype to monocyte progeny for months after viral clearance. The paper also identifies IL-1β as a potential (but as yet unconfirmed) additional driver, and discusses the spike S1 protein as a possible trigger for NLRP3-mediated monocyte reprogramming in PASC populations. This is a **secondary synthesis** (Perspective, ~4,350 words, no original data); its empirical anchor is Cheong et al. 2023 (Cell), which is cited extensively throughout as the primary evidence base. Evidence claims not independently derived from new data should be tracked to their source papers, not to this Perspective.

## Methods

**Article type and scope.** This is a Perspective article (Frontiers "brief-report" classification), not a systematic review or meta-analysis. No formal search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or data extraction protocol is described. The authors synthesize published literature to construct a mechanistic hypothesis. 71 references are cited; the paper spans 9 pages.

**Argumentative structure.** The Perspective proceeds in four steps:
1. Review of trained immunity evidence in COVID-19 monocytes — drawing on ATAC-seq studies of convalescent monocytes (primarily Cheong2023, ref 17; also You et al., ref 22) to establish the epigenetic reprogramming phenotype.
2. Review of trained immunity in low-density neutrophils (LDNs) in PASC — based on one single-cell RNA-seq study cited (ref 28).
3. Mechanistic hypotheses for upstream cytokines — IL-6 (based on Cheong2023's tocilizumab natural experiment and MHV-1 mouse data) and IL-1β (explicitly labeled speculative: "Although current research has not directly explored the specific role of IL-1β or other cytokines in trained immunity, it is hypothesized...").
4. Clinical implications — PASC inflammation, chronic disease exacerbation, therapeutic targets (IL-6/IL-1β blockade), and transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (speculative).

**What this Perspective does not do.** It presents no new patient data, no new epigenomic profiling, no new mouse experiments. It does not perform systematic evidence weighting or formal risk-of-bias assessment. The IL-1β and transgenerational-inheritance sections are explicitly framed as hypotheses requiring future investigation.

## Key Findings

**Core claim: COVID-19 induces trained immunity via HSPC epigenomic imprinting.** Circulating CD14+ monocytes from COVID-19 convalescent patients show durable (months to ~12 months) increases in chromatin accessibility at genes related to cytokine production and leukocyte activation (TNIP2, MAPKAPK2, IL21R, MMP1, CREB1), and enhanced cytokine release upon TLR agonist (R848) and IFN-α stimulation. This is attributed to epigenetic reprogramming originally inscribed in HSPCs, transmitted to monocyte progeny through differentiation — accounting for persistence despite classical monocytes' short lifespan of days. The primary evidence for these claims is drawn from Cheong et al. 2023 (ref 17) and You et al. (ref 22). This claim is a synthesis of published primary findings, not a novel finding of this Perspective.

**IL-6 as the key upstream imprinting signal.** IL-6 receptor blockade (tocilizumab) during acute COVID-19 hospitalization was associated with reduced monocyte epigenetic changes at inflammation-linked loci months later, reduced biased myelopoiesis, and fewer hyperresponsive monocyte phenotypes (derived from Cheong2023, ref 17). In MHV-1 mouse model, IL-6R blockade alleviated increased myeloid progenitor frequencies and monocyte infiltration in lungs and brain during recovery. STAT3 motif accessibility was persistently elevated in untreated vs. tocilizumab-treated or healthy patients. The Perspective synthesizes this to argue that acute IL-6 → STAT3 → HSPC chromatin remodeling is the central mechanistic pathway for long COVID trained immunity.

**IL-1β: speculative contributory role.** The paper hypothesizes that IL-1β, elevated in severe COVID-19 and associated with post-COVID sequelae, may contribute to HSPC metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming alongside IL-6. This is grounded in IL-1β's general role in trained immunity (non-COVID contexts), its ability to drive glycolytic metabolic reprogramming in HSPCs, and its association with PASC symptoms — but the paper explicitly acknowledges this is a hypothesis: no study is cited that directly demonstrates IL-1β-driven trained immunity in the COVID-19 context. This claim should be treated as [SPECULATION] per project convention.

**NLRP3 inflammasome triggered by spike S1 protein.** The Perspective cites evidence that: (a) circulating SARS-CoV-2 spike S1 protein is detectable in higher proportions of PASC patients (64%) vs. non-PASC (35%); and (b) the S protein can selectively trigger NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β secretion in macrophages from COVID-19 patients. The paper proposes that persistent S1 may cause ongoing reprogramming of monocytes/macrophages in PASC, "allowing rapid inflammasome assembly." The primary citations are Sefik et al. 2022 (Nature; ref 4/48) and Swank et al. (ref 49). This is synthesized from primary data, not derived from new data in this paper.

**LDN neutrophil trained immunity in PASC.** One study (ref 28) identified distinct subsets of low-density neutrophils (LDNs) with trained immunity features in recovering Omicron patients, including MME-hi, MX1+, and PI3+ subsets with elevated inflammatory cytokines and metabolic reprogramming (nicotinamide salvage, glycogenolysis, mTOR). The Perspective treats this as supporting a trained immunity contribution to PASC beyond the monocyte compartment. This rests on a single study.

**Clinical and downstream consequences proposed.** Hyperreactive monocytes are proposed to: sustain chronic low-grade inflammation and tissue damage in PASC organs; drive coagulopathy via tissue factor production; infiltrate tissues causing lung CT abnormalities and neurological symptoms (citing MHV-1 monocyte brain infiltration data from Cheong2023); impede the resolution of inflammation by blocking the monocyte-to-anti-inflammatory-macrophage transition needed for tissue repair; exacerbate pre-existing autoimmune inflammatory diseases; and potentially accelerate atherosclerosis and arthritis. Most of these consequences are drawn from mechanistic inference and limited observational data rather than direct interventional studies.

**Therapeutic proposals.** IL-6R blockade (tocilizumab) as a candidate strategy to prevent or reverse monocyte epigenetic imprinting. One case report cited suggests tocilizumab ameliorates PASC symptoms (ref 67). Combined IL-6 + IL-1β inhibition proposed for future investigation.

**Transgenerational inheritance.** Raises the speculative question of whether COVID-19-induced epigenetic changes are transmitted to offspring. Cites one mouse study showing Candida albicans-induced trained immune phenotype can be transgenerationally inherited. No human COVID-19 evidence exists; explicitly presented as a question for future research. [SPECULATION]

## Relevance

**`question:0026` (IL-6/STAT3 HSPC imprinting) — supportive synthesis context.** The paper's primary contribution to `question:0026` is providing an explicit synthesis argument that connects IL-6/STAT3 → HSPC epigenomic imprinting → hyperreactive monocyte progeny to the long COVID inflammatory burden. However, all empirical support cited here derives from Cheong2023 — Gu2023 itself presents no new data on this pathway. For hypothesis and belief-state tracking in `question:0026`, `paper:Cheong2023` is the load-bearing primary source; `paper:Gu2023` provides contextualization and synthesis framing but does not add independent empirical weight.

**`question:0024` (NLRP3/inflammasome/pyroptosis) — partial relevance via spike S1 NLRP3 link.** The paper raises the mechanism that persistent spike S1 protein in PASC patients triggers NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages — citing Sefik 2022 (ref 4/48) for the in vitro inflammasome finding and Swank et al. (ref 49) for S1 detection prevalence. This positions spike persistence as an upstream trigger for NLRP3-mediated monocyte/macrophage reprogramming — a plausible route connecting antigen persistence to the inflammasome loop in `question:0024`. The IL-1β speculative section also provides a narrative connecting COVID-19 cytokine dynamics to IL-1β-mediated trained immunity, but without primary COVID-specific evidence. The primary inflammasome evidence remains in Sefik 2022, not in this Perspective.

**`question:0023` (cGAS-STING → IFN-I) — no direct support.** The paper does not discuss cGAS, STING, or the type-I interferon axis. The connection to `question:0023` is entirely via the shared trained-immunity topic umbrella: trained myeloid cells could hypothetically be more responsive to cGAS-STING signaling, but this paper provides no locus-level, epigenomic, or functional evidence for that link. **This cross-link should be interpreted as contextual (both entities belong to the trained-immunity topic) rather than as Gu2023 providing evidence for the cGAS-STING pathway specifically.**

**`hypothesis:0003` (immune exhaustion feedback) — narrative complement.** Gu2023 proposes that epigenetically trained hyperreactive monocytes sustain a chronic inflammatory state that is antigen-independent. This complements h0003 (adaptive immune exhaustion driving persistent inflammation via T-cell dysfunction) by adding an innate compartment maintenance mechanism that operates in parallel and does not require adaptive T-cell engagement. The paper does not directly engage with T-cell exhaustion or the adaptive arm, so this link is thematic rather than explicitly argued within the Perspective.

**`hypothesis:0004` (acute-severity threshold) — indirect support.** IL-6 levels are proportional to COVID-19 severity; the paper argues that IL-6 imprints HSPCs. This implies that the depth of HSPC training is proportional to acute-illness severity, providing a plausible molecular substrate for the severity-based threshold in h0004. The Perspective does not develop this relationship quantitatively or compare severity strata; it is an inference from the IL-6/severity relationship (refs 29-30) and the Cheong2023 IL-6 blockade data (ref 17).

## Limitations

**1. Secondary source with single primary anchor.** Gu2023 is a Perspective that presents no original data. Its empirical core rests almost entirely on Cheong et al. 2023 (ref 17). Multiple paragraphs in the IL-6/HSPC sections are essentially a restatement of Cheong2023's findings. For evidence-tracking purposes, claims in Gu2023 about IL-6/STAT3/HSPC imprinting should be attributed to Cheong2023 directly; Gu2023 adds interpretive framing but not independent evidence.

**2. IL-1β claims are explicitly speculative, without COVID-19-specific primary support.** The paper's own text acknowledges: "Although current research has not directly explored the specific role of IL-1β or other cytokines in trained immunity, it is hypothesized..." All IL-1β claims should be held at hypothesis level, not as established mechanisms.

**3. Scope limited to COVID-19.** The Perspective does not address whether the trained-immunity mechanism extends to other PAIS triggers (Borrelia, Coxiella, EBV, dengue, influenza). All evidence is COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2-specific.

**4. Severity confounding not addressed.** The paper does not distinguish between mild and severe COVID-19 trained immunity. Given that Cheong2023's strongest HSPC data come from hospitalized (WHO 6-7) patients, and the majority of long COVID follows mild-to-moderate infection, this is a material gap the Perspective does not flag.

**5. Causation vs. correlation gap not resolved.** The paper proposes that trained HSPC → monocyte imprinting *drives* PASC symptoms, but no study in the Perspective establishes a correlation between HSPC imprinting depth and PASC severity, duration, or symptom burden. The causal chain from epigenetic imprint to clinical symptoms is inferential.

**6. Therapeutic claims are weakly evidenced.** The tocilizumab-for-PASC proposal rests on a single case report (ref 67). The combined IL-6 + IL-1β inhibition proposal is entirely speculative.

**7. Transgenerational inheritance section is highly speculative.** This section rests on a mouse Candida study with no human COVID-19 equivalent. Its inclusion in a clinical Perspective without adequate caveating risks overstating the evidence base.

**8. LDN section is tangential.** The neutrophil trained immunity section (one study) is briefly treated and not well integrated into the IL-6/HSPC framing. It could be a valuable mechanistic thread but is not developed here.

## Model / Tool Availability

No new data, tools, datasets, or code are released with this Perspective. The paper describes no experimental outputs. All cited primary data are available through the original publications (primarily GEO GSE196990 from Cheong2023 for the HSPC epigenomic data).

**Project questions this Perspective contextualizes:**

1. **`question:0026` (IL-6/STAT3 → HSPC imprinting):** Gu2023 provides the explicit synthesis argument linking this mechanism to long COVID inflammatory burden — useful as a citation for the claim that the trained-immunity mechanism is recognized as clinically relevant to PASC, even though Cheong2023 is the empirical anchor.
2. **`question:0024` (NLRP3/inflammasome):** The spike S1 → NLRP3 → monocyte reprogramming pathway described here represents a potential connecting mechanism between antigen persistence (spike S1) and trained immunity. Whether this is a one-time priming event or a sustained loop depends on the dynamics of S1 persistence in PASC — a question for `topic:antigen-pathogen-persistence`.
3. **`question:0023` (cGAS-STING):** Not addressed by this paper; the connection is through the broader trained-immunity framing only.
4. **Therapeutic intervention timing:** The Perspective's discussion of IL-6R blockade preventing epigenomic imprinting raises the question of whether there is a window of opportunity for acute-phase intervention to prevent PAIS. This is relevant to `question:0056` (pharmacological reversal of innate immune training).
