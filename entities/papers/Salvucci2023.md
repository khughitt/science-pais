# Antihistamines improve cardiovascular manifestations and other symptoms of long-COVID attributed to mast cell activation

- **Authors:** Fabrizio Salvucci, Roberto Codella, Adriana Coppola, Irene Zacchei, Gabriella Grassi, Maria Luisa Anti, Nicolita Nitisoara, Livio Luzi, Carmine Gazzaruso
- **Year:** 2023
- **Journal:** Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine, vol. 10, p. 1202696
- **DOI:** 10.3389/fcvm.2023.1202696
- **BibTeX key:** Salvucci2023
- **Source:** PDF

## Key Contribution

This retrospective controlled case series provides clinical evidence that dual blockade of histamine H1 (fexofenadine 180 mg/day) and H2 (famotidine 40 mg/day) receptors produces significant improvement of long-COVID symptoms — including cardiovascular manifestations (tachycardia, palpitations, exercise intolerance), fatigue, brain fog, and abdominal disorders — in a carefully selected, previously treatment-refractory cohort. Complete symptom resolution occurred in 4/14 treated patients (29%) after just 20 days, while all 13 matched untreated controls remained symptomatic. The findings support mast cell activation (MCA) as a pathophysiological substrate of a clinically recognizable long-COVID subtype and argue for H1/H2 blockade as a rational symptomatic treatment for this subgroup.

## Methods

**Design:** Retrospective controlled study, single centre (Italy), with a 20-day follow-up.

**Treated cohort (n = 14):** Selected via strict inclusion/exclusion criteria from 373 long-COVID patients treated with H1/H2 blockers at the centre. Inclusion required: RT-PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection ≥6 months prior; long-COVID diagnosis; prior treatment failure (any therapy except antihistamines/cromoglycate/montelukast); presence of fatigue plus ≥1 of increased heart rate, brain fog, or abdominal disorders.

**Exclusion criteria (key):** personal history of allergy or any MCA-characterised disease; any long-COVID-attributed symptom pre-dating COVID-19; SARS-CoV-2 vaccination; any comorbid disease or concomitant medication; symptoms not attributed to MCA by prior literature.

**Control cohort (n = 13):** Matched for sex, age, and BMI, drawn from ~1,000 long-COVID patients in the institutional database; same inclusion/exclusion criteria; declined further pharmacological treatment.

**Demographics:** Treated — age 49.5 ± 11.5 years, F/M = 9/5, BMI 25.6 ± 4.4. Controls — age 47.3 ± 8.0 years, F/M = 8/5, BMI 25.1 ± 2.7. No significant baseline differences between groups on any variable.

**Intervention:** Fexofenadine 180 mg once daily before dinner + famotidine 40 mg once daily at bedtime for 20 days.

**Outcome assessment:** Four symptoms evaluated: fatigue, brain fog, increased heart rate (HR), and abdominal disorders. Symptoms self-reported by patients using a 4-point scale (unchanged / improved / disappeared / worsened), except resting/postural HR, which was physician-assessed. Definitions were operationalised: increased HR = HR >100 bpm at rest or postural tachycardia (delta HR >30 bpm within 5–10 min of standing).

**Statistics:** Student's t-test (log-transformed for non-normal variables), Fisher's exact test, chi-square; p < 0.05 threshold.

## Key Findings

**Primary outcomes at 20 days (treated vs. untreated):**

| Symptom | Baseline treated | Unchanged at 20 d (treated) | p | Baseline controls | Unchanged at 20 d (controls) | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fatigue | 100% | 21% | <0.0001 | 100% | 85% | 0.48 |
| Brain fog | 93% | 43% | 0.013 | 85% | 70% | 0.64 |
| Increased HR | 93% | 29% | 0.001 | 92% | 85% | 1.00 |
| Abdominal disorders | 79% | 14% | 0.005 | 77% | 62% | 0.67 |

**Complete resolution (treated group):**
- Fatigue: disappeared in 6/14 patients (43%)
- Brain fog: disappeared in 6/14 patients (43%)
- Increased heart rate: resolved in 8/14 patients (57%)
- Abdominal disorders: resolved in 8/14 patients (57%)
- Full long-COVID remission: 4/14 (29%); 0/13 controls (p = 0.0977, underpowered)

**Comparison of symptom prevalence at 20 days (inter-group):**
- Unchanged fatigue: 21% (treated) vs. 85% (controls), p = 0.0018
- Increased HR persisting: 29% (treated) vs. 85% (controls), p = 0.0063
- Abdominal disorders persisting: 14% (treated) vs. 62% (controls), p = 0.0183

**Safety:** No adverse effects observed. No worsening of any symptom in any patient.

**Mechanistic commentary (authors):** Beta-blockers, commonly used for long-COVID tachycardia, may be counterproductive in MCA-driven tachycardia by lowering blood pressure and triggering compensatory baroreflex-mediated HR increase, whereas H1/H2 blockade addresses the primary mediator (histamine-driven vasodilation and tachycardia) and can raise blood pressure while lowering HR.

## Relevance

This paper is directly relevant to the project's research question (research-question:post-acute-infection-syndromes) and the PAIS frame of failed homeostatic recovery in multiple ways:

1. **Mechanistic subtype identification:** The study operationalises MCA-subtype long-COVID as a discrete pathophysiological entity within the PAIS family — a failure mode in which mast cell dysregulation, likely triggered or amplified by SARS-CoV-2 infection, persists far beyond acute illness and disrupts cardiovascular, neurological, and gastrointestinal homeostasis simultaneously.

2. **Shared PAIS failure mode:** MCA as a post-infectious immune perturbation echoes the shared mechanism hypothesis. Mast cells, positioned at the interface of innate immunity and autonomic/vascular regulation, can sustain chronic low-grade inflammatory signalling (histamine, tryptase, VIP, phospholipid metabolites) that prevents homeostatic recovery of heart rate regulation, gut motility, and cognitive clarity.

3. **Dysautonomia link:** The prominent cardiovascular phenotype — resting tachycardia and POTS-like postural tachycardia — places this study in direct conversation with the broader dysautonomia literature in PAIS and connects to the failed autonomic homeostasis frame. The observation that 57% of treated patients had HR normalise within 20 days implicates MCA as a reversible driver of dysautonomia, not a permanent structural lesion.

4. **Therapeutic tractability:** Unlike many PAIS mechanisms, MCA-driven pathology is pharmacologically targetable with widely available, low-cost, low-risk medications. This makes the MCA subtype an important "proof of concept" that at least a fraction of PAIS reflects persistent, treatable immunological dysregulation rather than irreversible structural damage.

5. **Subtype stratification imperative:** The observation that roughly 29% experienced complete remission while others showed partial or no benefit underscores the heterogeneity of long-COVID and the need for biomarker-guided subtyping — a core theme of the PAIS research question.

## Limitations

1. **Very small sample size (n = 14 treated, n = 13 controls):** Underpowered for the primary endpoint of full long-COVID remission (p = 0.097). All significant findings at the symptom level require replication in larger cohorts.

2. **Retrospective design:** No randomisation; allocation to treatment group was driven by patient refusal of further treatment in controls, introducing selection bias (controls may differ in unmeasured attitudes or severity).

3. **No validated questionnaires:** Symptom reporting was via simple 4-point scales; no standardised instruments (e.g. SF-36, FSS, COMPASS-31) were used, limiting comparison with other studies.

4. **Short follow-up (20 days):** Durability of response is unknown; it is unclear whether improvements persist after treatment cessation or whether indefinite maintenance dosing is required.

5. **No MCA biomarker confirmation:** No serum tryptase, histamine, urinary N-methylhistamine, or prostaglandin D2 measurements were performed. The MCA attribution is clinical, not biochemical — the cohort may include patients without true MCA who responded via other mechanisms, or miss patients with confirmed MCA who were excluded.

6. **Unvaccinated-only cohort:** While justified by the authors as controlling for a known confounder, this limits generalisability to the broader (mostly vaccinated) long-COVID population.

7. **Symptom self-report for most outcomes:** Except physician-measured HR, all outcomes are patient-reported without blinding, introducing placebo effect and response bias.

8. **No placebo arm:** Controls were untreated, not placebo-treated; the contribution of expectation effects cannot be excluded.

9. **Single-centre, Italian cohort:** Ethnic, dietary, and healthcare-access homogeneity may limit generalisability.

10. **Highly selected cohort:** The 14 treated patients came from 373 antihistamine-treated long-COVID patients — the basis for this selection is described but the representativeness of these 14 for the broader MCA-subtype population is unclear.

## Follow-up

- **Weinstock et al. 2021** (Int J Infect Dis 112:217–226): The referenced study comparing long-COVID, MCAS, and controls — foundational for the MCA hypothesis; worth summarising.
- **Glynne et al. 2022** (J Investig Med 70:61–67): The referenced retrospective study of H1/H2 response in 26 long-COVID patients; provides the strongest prior evidence and should be read alongside this paper.
- **Afrin, Weinstock & Molderings 2020** (Int J Infect Dis 100:327–332): The original hypothesis paper connecting COVID-19 hyperinflammation and post-COVID illness to MCAS.
- **Montelukast trials (ongoing as of 2023):** Leukotriene receptor antagonism as complementary mast cell stabiliser strategy — literature search recommended.
- **Biomarker question:** What tryptase, histamine metabolite, or cytokine profile distinguishes the MCA-subtype from other long-COVID subtypes? This is the critical gap.
- **Relationship to POTS literature:** Many long-COVID POTS studies do not screen for MCA; how much of post-COVID POTS is MCA-driven and reversible with antihistamines?
