# Key Pathophysiological Role of Skeletal Muscle Disturbance in Post COVID and Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS): Accumulated Evidence

- **Authors:** Carmen Scheibenbogen, Klaus J. Wirth
- **Year:** 2024
- **Journal:** Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle, 16, e13669
- **DOI:** 10.1002/jcsm.13669
- **BibTeX key:** Scheibenbogen2024
- **Source:** Full-text PDF (papers/pdfs/2024_Scheibenbogen_skeletal-muscle-disturbance-postcovid-mecfs.pdf), read 2026-06-20.

## Key Contribution

This review synthesises accumulated evidence that skeletal muscle is the primary site of the bioenergetic lesion in both post-COVID syndrome (PCS) and ME/CFS, and elaborates the Scheibenbogen/Wirth mechanistic model in which microvascular hypoperfusion during exertion triggers a cascade — anaerobic metabolism → proton excess → Na⁺/H⁺ exchange → intracellular sodium loading → reversal of the sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX) → calcium overload → mitochondrial damage — that is self-perpetuating once established. The authors explicitly frame ME/CFS as an "acquired ischemic mitochondrial myopathy" (AIMM), positioning secondary vascular-driven mitochondrial dysfunction (not primary mitochondrial disease) as the final common disturbance, and propose this shared pathomechanism explains why PCS can transition into a chronic ME/CFS-like phenotype in a subset of patients. The review also delineates risk factors governing that transition and surveys emerging treatments targeting the vascular/ionic upstream causes.

## Methods

This is a **narrative review with mechanistic synthesis** (not a systematic review or meta-analysis). The authors draw on:

- Histopathological and electron microscopy studies of skeletal muscle biopsies from PCS and ME/CFS cohorts (six key biopsy studies summarised in Table 1; including Appelman et al. 2024, Bizjak et al., Aschman et al., Hejbøl et al., Agergaard et al., and Walitt et al.).
- Functional exercise testing data (ergometry, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, VO₂ studies) from multiple groups.
- MRI sodium imaging (²³Na-MRI) demonstrating elevated intracellular sodium in skeletal muscle of ME/CFS patients.
- In vitro mitochondrial function assays from biopsies (respirometry).
- Circulating miRNA studies reflecting angiogenic and mitochondrial adaptation signals.
- Prior mechanistic papers by the same authors (Wirth & Scheibenbogen 2021; Wirth, Scheibenbogen & Paul 2021) as the theoretical foundation.
- Evidence from biomarker, autoantibody, endothelial function, and retinal microvasculature imaging studies for the vascular component.

The review covers two populations: PCS patients (typically less than 2 years post-infection) and ME/CFS patients (including post-COVID ME/CFS, PC-ME/CFS), distinguishing them where evidence permits.

## Key Findings

### 1. Skeletal muscle damage and mitochondrial pathology are demonstrated in biopsy studies

Multiple independent biopsy studies report: fibre atrophy, necrosis and regeneration (signs of ongoing myopathic damage), moderate leukocyte infiltration, capillary rarefaction (loss of capillaries), and basement membrane endothelial changes in PCS/ME/CFS muscle. Critically, electron microscopy studies reveal subsarcolemmal mitochondrial damage preferentially (vs. interfibrillary mitochondria), a pattern consistent with the mechanistic model because NCX — the proposed calcium import route — is located at the sarcolemmal membrane where calcium concentration peaks when NCX reverses. In vitro respirometry from biopsies demonstrates reduced mitochondrial function in ME/CFS. mRNA sequencing of biopsies shows downregulated mitochondrial and hexose metabolism genes. Importantly, biopsy studies do not find evidence of obstructed capillaries, viral persistence, or immune myositis as primary drivers, narrowing the plausible causes of damage to the ionic/ischaemic mechanism.

### 2. Elevated intracellular skeletal muscle sodium confirmed by ²³Na-MRI

A ²³Na-MRI study (Petter et al., cited as ref. 12) found elevated intracellular sodium in skeletal muscle of ME/CFS patients, with levels inversely correlated with handgrip strength (HGS). Sodium elevation was present at rest in some muscle types and rose further after exercise. This provides in vivo imaging evidence for the sodium-loading step that is central to the proposed mechanism — distinguishing it from purely theoretical speculation.

### 3. The Scheibenbogen/Wirth ionic cascade model

The core mechanistic model (Figure 1 in the paper) proposes:

1. Microvascular hypoperfusion (from multiple upstream causes: thromboinflammation, autoantibodies against GPCR/vasoregulatory receptors, endothelial dysfunction, autonomic dysfunction, impaired erythrocyte deformability, low cardiac preload) restricts oxygen delivery to skeletal muscle.
2. Exertion under hypoperfusion drives anaerobic metabolism, generating excess protons.
3. The Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger subtype 1 (NHE1) extrudes protons in exchange for sodium (1:1), raising intracellular Na⁺.
4. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity is insufficient to clear the sodium load due to: low ATP (from mitochondrial dysfunction), ROS inhibition, dysfunctional β2-adrenergic receptors (autoantibodies or stress-mediated desensitisation), and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) deficiency from small-fibre neuropathy.
5. At a threshold intracellular sodium level, the NCX reverses — instead of exporting calcium, it imports it, causing calcium overload in the myocyte.
6. Calcium overload (particularly at the subsarcolemmal location near NCX) damages subsarcolemmal mitochondria and impairs cytoplasmic metabolism.
7. Mitochondrial dysfunction → further reduced ATP and ROS generation → further impairment of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and vascular function → positive feedback loop.

The authors term this vicious cycle an "acquired ischemic mitochondrial myopathy" (AIMM). Once established, the mitochondrial dysfunction can sustain itself even if the original microvascular trigger partially heals, explaining disease chronification.

### 4. Distinction between PCS and PC-ME/CFS

An important clinical and mechanistic distinction is drawn between two populations (Figure 2):

- **PCS (early stage):** Dominant microvascular/capillary disturbance with inflammation; this is the initial post-COVID vascular pathology. Many patients in this stage improve over 1–2 years as the initial capillary injury heals.
- **PC-ME/CFS (established):** Dominant mitochondrial disturbance with vascular dysfunction now driven primarily by ROS from dysfunctional mitochondria rather than direct inflammatory capillary damage. Patients at this stage stagnate or worsen.

The transition from PCS to PC-ME/CFS is determined by risk factors including: autoantibodies (especially against GPCR vasoregulatory receptors), connective tissue diseases (Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, joint hypermobility), orthostatic dysfunction, variants in mitochondrial/vascular/muscle genes, mast cell hyperactivity, and TRPM3 ion channel dysfunction.

### 5. Repetitive damage and the PEM threshold

Increased anaerobic metabolism (evidenced by elevated glycolytic fibres in biopsies) from mitochondrial dysfunction and malperfusion generates excessive protons, triggering sodium overload even with small exertions. As mitochondria become depleted, anaerobic metabolism dominates further. The presence of simultaneous necroses and regenerating fibres in biopsy samples supports recurrent damage events. Once intracellular sodium approaches the NCX reversal threshold, previously non-damaging minor efforts become injurious — the PEM threshold falls progressively. This mechanistically explains the characteristic PEM profile of ME/CFS: disproportionate, delayed symptom exacerbation from minor exertion.

### 6. Muscular force as a biomarker

Diminished hand grip strength (HGS) is associated with symptom severity and prognosis in PCS and ME/CFS. A prospective observational study found early HGS reduction correlated with symptom persistence. Quadriceps and HGS measurements are proposed as objective biomarkers reflecting the underlying muscle pathology and sodium loading (given the inverse correlation of intracellular sodium with HGS from the ²³Na-MRI study).

### 7. Microclots and capillary obstruction: a qualified conclusion

The review notes that obstructed capillaries and microclots, as reported in earlier PCS studies, were no longer found in the two most recent biopsy studies (Appelman et al. 2024; Bizjak et al.). This is interpreted as reflecting disease stage: early PCS may have capillary blockage that heals, while the persistent vascular dysfunction in PC-ME/CFS is functional (endothelial dysfunction, ROS-mediated vasoconstriction, autoantibodies) rather than structural capillary obstruction. Microclots may disappear, but residual vascular and mitochondrial damage persists.

### 8. Therapeutic implications

Treatment rationale follows directly from the mechanistic model — targeting upstream causes of hypoperfusion:
- **Mestinon (pyridostigmine):** Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor; improves vascular perfusion by enhancing preload and cardiac output. A randomised placebo-controlled trial (Joseph et al.) showed benefit.
- **Vericiguat (soluble guanylate cyclase activator):** Promotes vasodilation via cGMP mimicking nitric oxide; also improves erythrocyte deformability. Under NCT05697640.
- **Immunoadsorption:** Removes autoantibodies transiently; shown to improve symptoms and normalise endothelial function in two-thirds of patients in observational studies.
- **B-cell/plasma-cell depletion:** Rituximab or similar for durable autoantibody depletion; trials ongoing.
- **GPCR aptamer neutralisation:** Phase II trial (NCT05911009) targeting GPCR autoantibodies.

## Relevance

This review is directly central to `question:0011-mitochondrial-basis-of-pem`. The Scheibenbogen/Wirth model proposes that mitochondrial dysfunction in PEM/ME/CFS is **secondary** — downstream of microvascular hypoperfusion and ionic dysregulation — rather than a primary mitochondrial disease. This is an important distinction for interpreting exercise biopsy data such as Appelman2024, which shows genuine intrinsic OXPHOS impairment but cannot distinguish primary from secondary causation. The subsarcolemmal localisation of mitochondrial damage in ME/CFS (not found in early PCS) is predicted by the model and is a testable mechanistic signature.

The review also feeds directly into `question:0010-vascular-microclot-subphenotype` through its stage-dependent reframing of the microclot evidence: structural capillary obstruction may characterise early PCS, while functional vascular dysfunction (ROS, autoantibodies, endothelial) becomes the dominant mechanism in established ME/CFS. This reconciles the apparent contradiction between studies finding and not finding microclots.

The AIMM concept and the PCS-to-PC-ME/CFS transition model are highly relevant to `topic:shared-failure-mode-across-pais` and `topic:mecfs-long-covid-convergence` — they provide a unified mechanism explaining both the shared phenotype and the variability in chronification across individuals.

The vascular upstream causes (autoantibodies, thromboinflammation, endothelial dysfunction) connect to `topic:thromboinflammation-and-endothelial-dysfunction`.

## Limitations

- **Narrative review, not systematic.** Study selection is not systematic or pre-registered; there is no formal assessment of study quality or risk of bias across the cited biopsy and clinical studies. The mechanistic synthesis reflects the authors' theoretical framework and may over-emphasise evidence consistent with their model.
- **Author conflicts of interest.** Carmen Scheibenbogen holds patents related to beta-adrenergic receptor antibody diagnostics and soluble guanylate cyclase activators. Klaus J. Wirth is managing director of Mitodicure GmbH, developing a small-molecule therapeutic for ME/CFS. These interests are disclosed but should be weighed when evaluating advocacy for particular mechanistic frames and treatment candidates.
- **The AIMM model remains a working hypothesis.** While the individual mechanistic steps have supporting evidence, the complete causal chain (hypoperfusion → proton excess → NHE1 → Na⁺ loading → NCX reversal → Ca²⁺ overload → subsarcolemmal mitochondrial damage) has not been validated as a continuous pathway in a single controlled experimental study. Each link is plausible but the model's assembly is synthetic.
- **Causation vs. association in biopsy findings.** Most cited biopsy studies are cross-sectional comparisons of patients vs. controls; they demonstrate the presence of mitochondrial damage and ionic disturbance but do not directly test whether hypoperfusion caused them. The ²³Na-MRI evidence (elevated intracellular Na⁺) is correlational.
- **Heterogeneous cohorts with variable case definitions.** The biopsy studies reviewed used different case definitions (CCC criteria, WHO long COVID, self-reported PCS), different disease durations (9 months to > 2 years post-infection), and different exercise-challenge protocols. Pooling findings across these studies requires caution; some apparent discrepancies (e.g., presence vs. absence of microclots) may reflect these design differences rather than disease-stage transitions.
- **Small sample sizes in biopsy studies.** Most individual studies cited involve 8–25 patients. The review synthesises across studies but this does not compensate for the limited statistical power of each underlying dataset.
- **The PCS-to-ME/CFS transition model is observational.** The staged model (Figure 2) is based on cross-sectional comparisons between PCS and PC-ME/CFS cohorts at different disease durations, not longitudinal tracking of individuals who transitioned. The proposed risk factors (autoantibodies, Ehlers-Danlos, TRPM3) are associative rather than causally validated.
- **Absence of direct calcium overload measurement in human muscle.** Calcium overload is inferred from the ionic model and from histological necrosis patterns analogous to ischaemia-reperfusion; direct in vivo calcium imaging in human muscle biopsies is not cited. In vitro calcium loading studies are referenced but not from the PAIS context.
- **ME/CFS without post-COVID trigger.** The review primarily addresses post-COVID ME/CFS and early PCS; generalisability to ME/CFS triggered by other pathogens (EBV, enteroviruses, Lyme) is asserted but less directly evidenced in the cited studies.

## Model / Tool Availability

No computational model, software, or dataset is released with this review. The mechanistic model (Figure 1 ionic cascade, Figure 2 PCS-to-ME/CFS staging) is a conceptual/graphical framework intended to guide research and therapeutic targeting, not a quantitative computational model.

## Follow-up

- The ²³Na-MRI method (intracellular skeletal muscle sodium) cited here as evidence for the sodium-loading step warrants direct follow-up: what are the sensitivity/specificity characteristics as a diagnostic or stratification tool? Does sodium level normalise in patients who clinically improve or respond to Mestinon/immunoadsorption?
- The subsarcolemmal mitochondrial damage pattern (present in ME/CFS, absent in early PCS per Bizjak et al.) is a testable mechanistic signature of the AIMM model. Comparison of Appelman2024's ultrastructural data against Bizjak et al. would clarify whether the Appelman cohort (1–2 year PCS with ME/CFS overlap) shows this pattern.
- The role of CGRP deficiency from small-fibre neuropathy in impairing Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is a mechanistic bridge between dysautonomia and muscle ionic dysregulation that deserves dedicated literature review — currently underexplored in this project.
- Therapeutic trials of pyridostigmine (Joseph et al., ref. 10 = Joseph2023) and the guanylate cyclase activator vericiguat (NCT05697640) provide near-term mechanistic tests of the vascular hypothesis. A positive result from either would substantially strengthen the upstream vascular causation arm.
- The TRPM3 ion channel as an additional risk factor (refs. 61–63) introduces a separate ionic disturbance pathway that is underspecified in this review; dedicated summary of TRPM3 evidence in ME/CFS is warranted.
- The paper does not engage with the gut microbiome, mast cell activation, or viral reactivation (EBV) threads from the broader PAIS literature; these may operate upstream of or in parallel with the vascular/ionic cascade described here.
- Direct comparison with `paper:Appelman2024`: Appelman found no capillary obstruction and amyloid deposits outside vessels — consistent with the "late PCS / transition stage" interpretation in this review rather than early structural microclot occlusion. The immune infiltration (T-cells, macrophages) found by Appelman is not prominently addressed by the Scheibenbogen/Wirth model, which focuses on vascular and ionic mechanisms; immune-mediated mitochondrial damage remains a competing or complementary explanation.
