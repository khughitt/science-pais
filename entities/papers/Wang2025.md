# Identifying subtypes of Long COVID: a systematic review

- **Authors:** Bingyi Wang, Xufei Luo, Meihua Wu, Zijun Wang, Jie Zhang, Zijing Wang, Qianling Shi, Jiayi Liu, Wenhao Cao, Xiaoyinggu, Yaolong Chen, Bin Cao, Janne Estill, and others
- **Year:** 2025 (published online 18 December 2025)
- **Journal:** eClinicalMedicine, vol. 91, article 103705
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103705
- **BibTeX key:** Wang2025
- **Source:** Full-text PDF (papers/pdfs/2025_Wang_identifying-subtypes-long-covid-systematic-review.pdf), read 2026-06-20.

## Key Contribution

This is the first comprehensive systematic review to synthesize all published approaches to clustering and classifying Long COVID patients and their symptoms. Across 64 studies encompassing 2.43 million participants from 20 countries, the authors identify four major categorization frameworks: symptom co-occurrence (n = 30, 46.9%), organ system affected (n = 16, 25.0%), severity stratification (n = 9, 14.1%), and clinical biomarker indicators (n = 3, 4.7%). The review integrates a cross-study symptom co-occurrence network, meta-analyses of organ system-based cluster proportions, and an exploratory analysis of demographic and viral-variant determinants of subtype distribution — ultimately proposing a practical management framework for directing patients toward appropriate care levels based on their subtype and symptom severity.

## Methods

**Design:** Systematic review and meta-analysis following PRISMA 2020 guidelines; registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF.IO/J483F).

**Search:** PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Google Scholar searched from inception to January 21, 2025, with an updated search on October 1, 2025. Search terms combined MeSH (e.g. "Post-Acute COVID-19 Syndrome") with free-text variants (e.g. "PASC," "long COVID," "cluster," "subtype," "phenotype," "pattern," "endotype").

**Inclusion criteria:** Original studies explicitly reporting patient or symptom clusters or subtypes of Long COVID, regardless of clustering method (algorithmic, descriptive, expert-based). Excluded: conference abstracts, duplicate publications, studies clustering symptoms from combined ME/CFS and Long COVID datasets, and studies classifying Long COVID patients without symptom-based clustering.

**Screening:** 4004 records identified; 64 studies included after multistage screening. Study designs: prospective cohort (n = 26, 40.6%), retrospective cohort (n = 20, 31.2%), cross-sectional (n = 17, 26.6%), mixed cohort (n = 1). Median sample size 594 for prospective cohorts; range 95–907,391 across all studies. Most studies targeted adults (n = 49, 76.6%).

**Long COVID definitions used in included studies:** WHO criteria (n = 25, 39.1%), other established definitions (n = 25, 39.1%), adapted definitions (n = 10, 15.6%), not specified (n = 4, 6.2%).

**Quality assessment:** Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies (mean 7.5/9, majority rated high quality); Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool for cross-sectional studies (mean 0.61/1.00, moderate quality; key weaknesses: non-representative sampling, non-probabilistic methods).

**Clustering methods used in included studies (multiple permitted):** Hierarchical clustering (n = 24, 37.5%), K-means (n = 12, 18.8%), latent class analysis (n = 7, 10.9%), exploratory factor analysis (n = 5, 7.8%), PAM (n = 5, 7.8%), topic modeling (n = 4, 6.3%), multiple correspondence analysis (n = 3, 4.7%), HDBSCAN (n = 3, 4.7%), PCA (n = 2, 3.1%), Louvain community detection (n = 2, 3.1%), others (n = 11, 17.2%). Number of clusters identified ranged from 2 to ≥6.

**Synthesis approach:**
- *Co-occurrence network analysis:* Pairwise co-occurrence frequencies across the 30 symptom co-occurrence studies were computed and visualized as a hierarchical heatmap (Ward's method, Euclidean distance) and a network diagram using ChiPlot.
- *Meta-analysis:* For the 16 organ system-based studies, pooled percentage of patients in each organ system cluster was estimated via random-effects models using the `metaprop` function in R (v4.4.3), to account for high heterogeneity (I² = 100% across all clusters).
- *Exploratory analysis:* Qualitative synthesis of demographic, clinical, and viral-variant factors associated with differential subtype distribution, extracted from the original studies.

## Key Findings

**Four classification frameworks and their clusters (Table 2)**

1. **Symptom co-occurrence (n = 30, 46.9%):** The dominant approach. The most frequently defined cluster was olfactory-gustatory dysfunction (n = 6 studies, 20.0%), followed by fatigue-dyspnea (n = 5, 16.7%), fatigue-cognitive/neurological symptoms (n = 5, 16.7%), and cardiopulmonary symptoms (n = 4, 13.3%). Fatigue appeared in 15 of 30 studies either defining its own cluster or co-occurring with other symptoms. Pairwise co-occurrence network analysis identified the most frequent symptom dyads as olfactory-gustatory dysfunction (n = 10 studies), anxiety-depression (n = 10), joint pain/swelling-muscle pain (n = 9), and joint pain/swelling-fatigue (n = 9). Hierarchical clustering of the cross-study co-occurrence matrix revealed three broad meta-clusters: (a) a chronic physical-decline cluster (fatigue, dyspnea, exercise intolerance, muscle pain, chest pain); (b) a neuropsychiatric cluster (cognitive symptoms, insomnia, anxiety, depression); (c) an upper-respiratory/post-acute infection cluster (olfactory-gustatory dysfunction, runny nose/nasal congestion, sneezing, cough). Proposed underlying mechanisms: autonomic dysfunction, microglial activation, peripheral nerve sensitization.

2. **Organ system (n = 16, 25.0%):** Neurological (n = 12, 75.0%), respiratory (n = 11, 68.8%), gastrointestinal (n = 7, 43.8%), fatigue (n = 5, 31.3%), ENT (n = 3, 18.8%), cardiopulmonary (n = 3, 18.8%), and olfactory/gustatory dysfunction (n = 3, 18.8%) clusters. Meta-analysis pooled proportions (random-effects): respiratory 47% [95% CI: 29–65%]; neurological 31% [3–60%]; gastrointestinal 28% [0–57%]; fatigue 37% [19–55%]; musculoskeletal 28% [0–57%]; olfactory/gustatory 41% [0–94%]; ENT 19% [0–54%]. All clusters showed extreme heterogeneity (I² = 100%). Proposed mechanisms: local tissue damage (e.g., pulmonary fibrosis), neuroinflammation, endothelial dysfunction.

3. **Severity stratification (n = 9, 14.1%):** Mild/moderate/severe groupings (n = 5, 55.6%), recovery vs. persistent categories (n = 2, 22.2%), quality of life/physical and mental health grading (n = 2, 22.2%). Proposed mechanisms: differences in immune response intensity, persistent viral antigens, metabolic exhaustion, vascular injury (hypercoagulability).

4. **Clinical indicators (n = 3, 4.7%):** Molecular-feature-based subtypes. One study identified subtypes including: (a) molecular features resembling healthy populations with no clear Long COVID risk factors; (b) triglyceride and organic acid-based clusters; (c) high molecular heterogeneity linked to severe symptoms and poor prognosis. Another used MRI gas retention, pulmonary function testing (PFT), and blood cell membrane conductance values to stratify patients. A third used CRP, lymphocyte counts, comorbidity burden, and D-dimer. Proposed mechanisms: microvascular abnormalities (pulmonary capillaritis or thrombosis), persistent immune activation, metabolic dysregulation.

5. **Other (n = 6, 9.4%):** Studies based on sociodemographic heterogeneity without a clearly defined clustering nomenclature.

**Exploratory determinants of subtype distribution**
- Sex: Females had significantly higher rates of neuropsychiatric symptoms and fatigue; males had higher rates of respiratory symptoms.
- Age: Older individuals were more prone to respiratory, cardio-renal, and ENT symptoms.
- Race/ethnicity: Hispanic and African American populations were more likely to develop respiratory/cardiac and neuropsychiatric clusters; white individuals had higher rates of fatigue and musculoskeletal symptoms.
- Viral variant: Alpha variant was strongly associated with olfactory and respiratory symptoms; Delta increased the risk of ENT-related symptoms.
- Host factors: High BMI, COPD, and socioeconomic deprivation were associated with increased risk of cardiopulmonary cluster and greater Long COVID burden.

**Proposed clinical management framework (Fig. 4)**
The authors propose a workflow: clinicians classify patients first by symptom co-occurrence, then map co-occurrence phenotypes to organ system clusters (using ICD-10/CCSR codes), then refer to relevant specialists. Symptom severity (mild/moderate/severe) determines care level: mild to general practitioners, moderate to community-based Long COVID specialty/rehabilitation clinics, severe to tertiary multidisciplinary care teams (MDTs) integrating physical therapy, occupational rehabilitation, psychological support, and precision pharmacotherapy.

## Relevance

**To question:0014 (which PAIS case definition is most biologically coherent):** Wang2025 directly documents the definitional fragmentation across the 64-study corpus: 39.1% used WHO criteria, 39.1% used other established definitions, 15.6% used adapted definitions, and 6.2% specified none. This creates non-comparable subtype maps — the same biological phenotype will land in different clusters depending on whether the case definition requires symptom onset within 4 weeks (WHO) or includes earlier presentations. The review cannot identify which definition is most biologically coherent; it instead underscores why that question is unanswered.

**To question:0015 (does PEM requirement improve cross-study comparability):** The review notes high heterogeneity (I² = 100%) even within method-matched subsets (e.g., all organ-system studies), and explicitly lists differing "symptom naming conventions and clustering algorithms" as drivers. The absence of any PEM-specific filtering in the included studies, and the failure of organic symptom convergence across those studies, supports the hypothesis that requiring PEM (or another biologically anchored criterion) might reduce spurious cluster heterogeneity — though Wang2025 does not test this directly.

**To question:0001 (shared molecular signature across triggers):** The existence of at least three broadly stable meta-clusters (neuropsychiatric, cardiopulmonary/physical-decline, upper-respiratory/olfactory) across methods and populations provides limited support for a shared dysregulated attractor: the neuropsychiatric and physical-decline clusters look most like what one would expect from a systemic failure of immune/autonomic homeostasis rather than organ-specific damage. However, Wang2025 also shows that only 4.7% of studies used molecular/clinical-indicator-based subtypes, meaning nearly all subtyping is phenotypic, leaving the question of shared underlying mechanisms open.

**To hypothesis:0001 (shared-dysregulated-attractor):** The cross-system co-occurrence network (Fig. 2B) — with fatigue as the most multiply-connected node, bridging neurological, musculoskeletal, respiratory, and cardiovascular domains — is consistent with a single attractor state expressed across organ systems. But the review does not distinguish whether this reflects one shared mechanism or multiple converging pathologies that happen to produce similar symptom constellations.

**To topic:pais-case-definition-heterogeneity:** This review is the most comprehensive empirical audit of Long COVID subtyping methodology to date. Its core finding — four non-overlapping classification frameworks, definitional variability, extreme statistical heterogeneity, and the rarity of biomarker-based approaches — is the most direct evidence that the case-definition heterogeneity problem is deeply embedded in the existing literature.

**To topic:biomarkers-and-objective-endpoints:** Only 3 of 64 studies (4.7%) used biological/clinical indicator-based subtyping (triglycerides, imaging, CBC parameters). The authors call explicitly for multi-omics integration to achieve mechanism-oriented subtyping, which aligns with the project's interest in objective, biology-grounded classification.

## Limitations

1. **Extreme heterogeneity across all strata (I² = 100%):** Pooled estimates for organ-system cluster proportions are descriptive summaries, not reliable prevalence estimates. The random-effects meta-analysis mitigates but does not resolve this. The authors acknowledge the heterogeneity weakens the reliability of pooled percentages.

2. **Four incommensurable classification frameworks:** Co-occurrence, organ system, severity, and clinical-indicator subtypes are not mutually exclusive and cannot be directly compared. No attempt is made to reconcile or adjudicate between them.

3. **Case definition fragmentation in included studies:** 78.2% of included studies used WHO or other established definitions, but definitional heterogeneity is documented and unresolved; 6.2% specified no definition.

4. **Predominantly symptom-level data; biomarker-based studies severely underrepresented:** Only 3 studies used molecular/clinical indicators, making mechanistic interpretation of clusters almost entirely speculative. The hypothesized mechanisms presented in Table 2 are explicitly labelled as hypothetical in the paper.

5. **Self-reported symptom data dominant (56.3% of studies):** Subject to recall bias, variable symptom capture, and inconsistent symptom naming conventions across studies. EHR-based studies may underreport subjective symptoms; survey-based data may overreport them.

6. **Pediatric populations underrepresented:** Only 6.2% of studies specifically targeted children and adolescents.

7. **Cross-sectional data predominate in a temporally dynamic condition:** Symptoms fluctuate and co-occurrence patterns change over the disease course; static classification models may not capture this. No temporal trend analysis was conducted.

8. **Co-occurrence analysis is study-level, not patient-level:** The pairwise co-occurrence frequencies aggregate study-level data, which cannot capture individual patient-level co-occurrence patterns or cluster stability.

9. **Studies without subtyping excluded:** Excluding studies that did not apply clustering may introduce a publication or methodology bias, potentially underrepresenting broader symptom data.

## Model / Tool Availability

No model, tool, or dataset is released with this paper. The co-occurrence analysis and hierarchical clustering visualization used ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/) and R v4.4.3 (`metaprop` function from the `meta` package). The study protocol is registered at OSF.IO/J483F. Supplementary data are available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2025.103705. Raw data underlying the findings are available from the corresponding authors (chevidence@lzu.edu.cn) on reasonable request.

## Follow-up

**Papers to read next:**
- Kuodi et al. 2022 (Nat Commun, ref 73 in this paper) — three typical Long COVID symptom groups identified from seven studies; referenced as a benchmark for cross-study convergence.
- Zhang et al. 2023 (Nat Med, ref 3) — data-driven identification of post-acute SARS-CoV-2 infection subphenotypes; one of the higher-impact primary studies in this corpus.
- Klein et al. 2023 (Nature, ref 74) — distinguishing features of Long COVID via immune profiling; would ground the symptom-cluster framework in mechanistic biology.
- Geng et al. 2024 (RECOVER, ref 37) — RECOVER adult Long COVID research update including symptom-based clustering from one of the largest US cohorts (RECOVER program).
- Blankestijn et al. 2024 (BMJ Open Respir Res, ref 30) — distinct clinical phenotypes at 3–6 months post-SARS-CoV-2 from the P4O2 consortium; one of only a few studies integrating multi-modal clinical data.

**Questions this raises for the project:**
- Given that fatigue is the single most multiply-connected node in the co-occurrence network and appears across all four cluster families, should the project treat it as a candidate attractor-state marker rather than a symptom in one cluster among many?
- None of the 64 included studies required PEM as a case-defining criterion. How would prevalence estimates and cluster structure change if the cohorts were restricted to individuals meeting ME/CFS-like criteria (ICC or CCC)? This is the empirical test implied by question:0015.
- The three clinical-indicator-based subtypes (biomarker subtypes) used triglycerides, MRI gas retention/PFT, and CRP/lymphocyte/D-dimer panels — all quite different. What would a systematic effort to harmonize multi-omics biomarker panels across these 64 cohorts produce?
- The exploratory analysis found SARS-CoV-2 variant substantially influenced cluster distribution (Alpha → olfactory/respiratory; Delta → ENT). Does this mean some "Long COVID subtypes" are effectively variant-specific and will not replicate across pandemic waves? This is a major confounder for cross-study synthesis.
