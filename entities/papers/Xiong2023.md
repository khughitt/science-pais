# Multi-'omics of gut microbiome-host interactions in short- and long-term ME/CFS patients

## Key Contribution

Duration-stratified ME/CFS host-microbiome study showing that microbiome dysbiosis is strongest in
shorter-duration ME/CFS, while longer-duration ME/CFS can retain severe phenotypic and metabolic
abnormalities after microbial composition partially normalizes. This is the key cautionary result for the
gut-axis model: dysbiosis can be an entry/early-maintenance axis without being a sufficient explanation
for established chronic disease.

## Methods

- **Design:** case-control, multi-omic stool and plasma study.
- **Participants:** short-term ME/CFS (<4 years, n=75), long-term ME/CFS (>10 years, n=79), and healthy
  controls (n=79).
- **Assays:** deep metagenomic stool profiling, plasma metabolomics, clinical phenotyping.

## Key Findings

- Short-term ME/CFS showed significant microbial dysbiosis.
- Long-term ME/CFS had largely resolved microbial dysbiosis but more severe clinical/metabolic
  abnormalities.
- Candidate mechanisms included reduced microbial butyrate biosynthesis and reductions in plasma
  butyrate, bile acids, and benzoate.
- The study identifies phenotypic, microbial, and metabolic biomarkers that differ by disease-duration
  stratum.

## Relevance

This paper supports `proposition:0031` while narrowing its interpretation. It favors a time-dependent
gut-host interaction model rather than a simple "persistent dysbiosis alone maintains PAIS" model.

## Limitations

- Cross-sectional duration strata rather than within-person longitudinal transition.
- Disease duration is confounded by survivorship, adaptation, treatment/diet changes, and cohort
  composition.
- The long-term normalization pattern weakens any claim that microbiome composition is a universal
  maintenance driver in established ME/CFS.
